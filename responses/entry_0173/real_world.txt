Affected Groups: Women in urban slum communities across South and Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America who are managing menstruation in conditions of extreme sanitation insecurity, including shared toilet facilities serving large numbers of people, insufficient water for adequate hygiene, lack of private indoor spaces, and unpredictable access to the limited facilities that exist due to queuing and usage patterns. Disproportionately affecting women in informal settlements where sanitation infrastructure was not designed to the number of people using it, women with irregular cycle timing for whom planning around facility access is not reliably possible, women in households without indoor toilet access who must use shared facilities in conditions of particular visibility, and women in communities where menstrual hygiene is a topic that cannot be discussed with male household members or neighbors in ways that would allow for explicit accommodation of hygiene needs in shared space management. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Sanitation infrastructure in informal settlements is typically either absent or under-provisioned relative to population because informal settlements are not recognized by municipal planning processes and therefore do not receive formal infrastructure allocation. Two toilets for thirty-one people is a ratio that is functionally inadequate for ordinary sanitation needs and more inadequate still for menstrual hygiene management that requires extended time, privacy, water access, and the ability to clean or dispose of materials. There is no legal mechanism through which a woman in this situation can claim a right to adequate sanitation that would be enforced in a way that changed the infrastructure within a timeframe relevant to her immediate need. The knowledge she has developed about the quieter hours at the toilets is the adaptation of someone who has turned a hostile infrastructure into a partially navigable one through temporal intelligence. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is managing a biological necessity in an environment that was not designed for it and that cannot be redesigned within the timeframe of any individual cycle. She has developed temporal strategies for minimizing the inadequacy of the infrastructure, and those strategies depend on predictability in both her body and the facility usage that she cannot always control. The question she is asking is a question about what strategies exist for managing when the temporal workaround fails, which is the question of someone who has already used all the adaptation available within the ordinary constraints and is now asking about the boundary cases.

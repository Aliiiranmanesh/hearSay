## 2️⃣ If You Must Use the Office Restroom…

### 2.1 Pick the “least‑busy” stall *and* lock it

1. **Scout for a pattern** – Even if the rush is unpredictable, most people still avoid the stall *farther* from the door.  
2. **Use the lock** (or the “occupied” latch). Most stalls have a lock that *actually* secures the door; if yours doesn’t, bring a small padlock and a hasp (many people use a “privacy lock” on a bathroom door).  
3. **Add a visual cue** – Hang a “Do Not Disturb – Private Matter” sign on the outside of the stall door. A simple piece of cardstock taped to the door works.

### 2.2 Create a “privacy shield” inside the stall

- **Folding screen / privacy panel** – A 24‑inch collapsible screen (like a photographer’s backdrop) folds flat enough to slip into a stall and can be propped against the wall.  
- **Noise‑masking** – Small Bluetooth speaker playing white‑noise, gentle rain, or low‑volume music helps drown out hallway sounds.  
- **Ventilation** – If you’re worried about air, keep a small battery‑powered fan (USB‑type) running on a low setting.

### 2.3 Buddy‑system scheduling

1. **Create a simple spreadsheet** (or even a shared Google Sheet) with two columns: “Stall A” and “Stall B”.  
2. **Each person blocks off a 5‑minute “guard” slot** before they need privacy – they stay inside the stall, keep the door locked, and act as a “watcher”.  
3. **When the guard’s done, they exit** and you take over the stall for the full duration you need.  

> This works especially well if you have a small group (3‑5 people) who all need occasional privacy (e.g., medical appointments, breastfeeding, prayer, etc.). The overhead is ~5 minutes of each person’s time, but you gain guaranteed stall access.

### 2.4 Use a “bathroom‑break” policy as leverage

- Many companies have an **“reasonable bathroom break”** clause in their HR handbook.  
- If you frame your request as *“I need a short, uninterrupted period to address a medical/health need”*, HR is usually obliged to accommodate, especially if you can suggest a concrete solution (e.g., “I will use the conference room on the 2nd floor during 10‑10:10 am”).

## 4️⃣ Negotiating With Management

1. **Identify the need clearly** – “I have a medical condition that requires a private, uninterrupted space for 10‑15 minutes a few times a week.”  
2. **Propose a low‑impact solution** – “I could use the small conference room on the 3rd floor; I’ll lock the door and keep the time under 15 minutes.”  
3. **Offer a trial period** – Suggest a 2‑week pilot; if it works, keep it. This reduces the perceived risk for managers.  
4. **Document the agreement** – A quick email summarizing the arrangement protects both sides.  

If HR is involved, cite **ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act)** or **local occupational health & safety regulations** that require reasonable accommodations for privacy‑related health needs. Even if your need isn’t a disability, many companies have a “well‑being” clause that covers personal privacy.

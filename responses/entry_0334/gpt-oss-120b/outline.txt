## 1. Natural “Charcoal” & Graphite‑Like Sticks

| What you need | How to make it | What it does |
|---------------|----------------|--------------|
| **Burnt wood** (a small twig, a piece of a banana stem, or any dry wood) | Light a small fire in a safe container (metal pot, metal tray, or a deep bowl). Hold the stick over the flame until the tip turns black and the surface becomes a fine, crumbly black coating. When it cools, you have a charcoal stick that can be sharpened on a rough surface (stone, brick, or the edge of a metal pot). | Works like a charcoal pencil – great for bold lines, shading, and expressive marks. |
| **Shell charcoal** (if you have access to shells or burnt bone) | Burn the shell/bone until black, then crush it into a fine powder. Mix a little water and glue (or flour‑water paste) and spread onto a thin piece of cardboard; let dry – you now have a “stick” you can use like a pastel. | Gives a softer, powdery texture for shading. |

**Tips:**  
- Keep a small piece of sandpaper or a rough stone handy to keep the tip sharp.  
- Store charcoal sticks in a dry container; they can get dusty but stay usable for weeks.

## 2. Earth‑Based Pigments (Colors from the Ground)

| Color | Source | How to prepare |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| **Reds & oranges** | Iron‑rich soil, red clay, or dried hibiscus petals | Grind a small amount of soil or dried petals on a clean stone. Add a few drops of water to make a paste. |
| **Yellows** | Turmeric powder, dried banana peel, dried mustard seeds | Crush the material, mix with water. |
| **Greens** | Fresh spinach, parsley, or any leafy greens | Blend leaves with a little water, strain the liquid. |
| **Blues** | Indigo plant (if available), crushed cornflower petals, or even the blue of a discarded ink cartridge | Crush, add water, filter. |
| **Browns/Grays** | Regular soil, charcoal dust, ash | Same as red – just grind and wet. |

**How to use:**  
- Apply the pigmented paste with a simple brush (a piece of clean cloth wrapped around a stick) or dip a small twig directly into the paste.  
- If you want a more “ink‑like” consistency, add a tiny bit of gum Arabic (if you can get it from a local market) or a drop of flour‑water paste to thicken.

**Safety note:** Test a tiny spot first—some soils can contain heavy metals. Stick to clean, non‑industrial soil (garden soil, not road dust).

## 3. Household “Ink” Alternatives

| Ingredient | Method | Result |
|------------|--------|--------|
| **Coffee or tea** | Brew a strong cup, let it cool, strain. | Light brown ink – perfect for sketching or calligraphy. |
| **Cocoa powder** | Mix cocoa with a little water and a drop of vinegar (acts as a preservative). | Dark brown, slightly richer than coffee. |
| **Soy sauce** (if you have it) | Dilute a bit with water. | Deep brown, very fluid – works as a fine liner. |
| **Coconut milk (or any thick milk)** + **vinegar** | Boil gently, let the milk curdle, strain the curds, and use the remaining liquid. | Gives a milky, opaque white “ink” that can be used for highlights. |
| **Food coloring** (even a single drop) | Mix with a bit of water or the above bases. | Bright, saturated color for accents. |

**Application:** Use a thin stick (a broken pen tip, a bamboo skewer, or a straightened paper clip) as a dip pen, or simply dip a small brush or cloth.

## 4. Recycled or Found “Paper”

| What to use | How to prepare |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Old newspaper or magazine pages** | Trim to size; they are already thin enough for drawing. |
| **Cardboard** (from boxes, shoe boxes, cartons) | Cut into small sheets (A5 or A6 size). If the surface is too rough, sand lightly with a piece of sandpaper or rub with a dry cloth. |
| **Fabric scraps** (cotton, linen) | Stretch over a wooden frame or a flat surface, then draw on the fabric directly. |
| **Plastic bags** (clear or colored) | Lay flat and use as a drawing surface; ink/paint will sit on top and can be wiped off for reuse. |
| **Leaves** (large, sturdy leaves like banana or plantain) | Dry them, then use as natural “paper” for quick sketches. |

**Tip:** If you want a smoother surface, press the cardboard between two heavy books for a day or two, then lightly sand the side you’ll draw on.

## 5. Simple Brushes & Tools

| Tool | How to make it |
|------|----------------|
| **Brush** | Cut a strip of clean cloth, roll it tightly, tie one end with a piece of string or a rubber band, and attach the other end to a stick. |
| **Sponges** | Cut a small piece from a kitchen sponge (if you have one) or a piece of soft cloth. |
| **Stippling tool** | Use a fork, a toothpick, or a small piece of bark with a rough edge. |
| **Ruler/straight edge** | Use a clean piece of cardboard, a plastic bottle, or the edge of a metal sheet. |
| **Eraser** | A small piece of soft rubber (from a broken eraser) or a clean cloth dabbed with a little water. |

## 6. Creative Ways to Extend What You Have

1. **Mix pigments** – Blend two earth pigments to get a new hue (e.g., red + yellow = orange).  
2. **Layering** – Use a light wash of coffee ink as a “paper” background, then add charcoal lines on top.  
3. **Collage** – Cut bits of newspaper, old flyers, or fabric and glue them together (use a homemade paste: flour + water).  
4. **Stamps** – Cut simple shapes out of potatoes, carrots, or sturdy leaves, dip in pigment, and stamp onto the surface.  
5. **Reuse** – After a drawing session, lightly wash the paper (if it’s sturdy) and let it dry for a new canvas.

---

## 7. Keeping It Safe & Clean

- **Ventilation:** When making charcoal or burning anything, do it in a well‑ventilated area (outside, under a canopy).  
- **Hand washing:** After handling soil, ash, or pigments, wash hands with soap before eating.  
- **Storage:** Keep your homemade inks and pigments in small, sealed containers (old plastic bottles, jars, or even cleaned soda cans).  
- **Avoid toxic plants:** Do not use any plant you can’t positively identify as non‑poisonous. Stick to kitchen herbs, common garden greens, and known edible plants.

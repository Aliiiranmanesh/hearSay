### 🖍️ Safe, Accessible Drawing Materials with Almost No Supplies
Children's creativity doesn't depend on store-bought supplies. Here are effective, low-cost options you can make or gather locally:

**Mediums:**
- **Charcoal sticks:** Burn small dry twigs or wood scraps in a metal tin with a few holes in the lid (so they smolder, not flame). Let cool completely. They draw smoothly and can be brushed off or lightly fixed with a dab of diluted starch paste.
- **Earth/clay paints:** Sift fine soil or clay (red, yellow, or white earth works best). Mix with clean water until creamy. Strain through cloth if gritty. A pinch of salt or sugar helps it dry evenly and reduces cracking.
- **Plant & food stains:** Crush dark berries, beetroot, or spinach with a little water. Strong black tea, instant coffee, or even boiled onion skins make warm brown washes. These are non-toxic and wash off skin easily.
- **Ash paste:** Cool, fine wood ash mixed with a few drops of water creates a soft gray medium. Keep away from eyes and mouth, as ash can be alkaline.
- **Binder (optional but helpful):** If you ever have a spoonful of flour, cornstarch, or cassava starch, boil it with water until thick. A teaspoon of this paste mixed into any homemade pigment helps it stick to paper and dry smoothly.

**Tools:**
- Fingers, cotton scraps, or folded cloth for blending
- Split reeds, chewed twig ends, or old toothbrushes as brushes
- A clean, smooth stone or spoon for burnishing/scratching lines
- A small shallow tray filled with damp sand or fine dirt becomes a reusable, zero-waste drawing board

**Surfaces:**
- Flatten cardboard boxes, smooth with a flat stone, and let dry
- Layer torn paper with a thin starch paste to make thicker sheets
- Clean, dry mud walls or packed earth floors (if culturally acceptable) can be used for large, temporary drawings that wash away with rain

**Safety notes:** Always supervise young children with homemade media. Keep pigments away from food/water sources. Test a small patch first to check for skin irritation or excessive staining.

### 📄 Navigating the Birth Certificate Requirement
Frontline NGO staff often follow strict checklists, but most humanitarian agencies have **alternative documentation pathways** for people born during state collapse or conflict. You don't need to accept "no" as final. Try these steps:

1. **Ask specifically about alternative verification:**  
   *"Do you accept a community witness declaration, clinic/hospital birth log, school registration record, or a sworn affidavit from two recognized community members?"*  
   Many NGOs (especially WFP, UNHCR, ICRC, and local partners) have internal guidelines for unregistered persons.

2. **Request a protection or registration officer:**  
   Frontline intake staff often lack authority to make exceptions. Ask to speak with the site's **protection focal point** or **registration supervisor**. They are trained to handle cases like yours and can often approve biometric or alternative documentation.

3. **Explore biometric or agency-linked IDs:**  
   If you or your family have ever registered with UNHCR, WFP, a local clinic, or a mosque-based aid program, you may already have a case number, biometric file, or ration card that can substitute for a birth certificate.

4. **Community attestation:**  
   In Somalia, some NGOs accept a signed letter from a recognized local elder, neighborhood committee head, former teacher, or imam who can verify your age and birthplace. If possible, get it witnessed and stamped by a local authority or civil society group.

5. **Document your attempts:**  
   Keep a simple log: dates, names/titles of staff spoken to, and what they said. If you're turned away repeatedly, this record can help a supervisor or legal aid NGO advocate on your behalf.

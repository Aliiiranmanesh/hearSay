When you have almost no supplies, “the best things to draw with” are whatever marks the world offers and her hands can shape. The limitation often unlocks a different kind of creativity. Here are things that work, sorted by what might be findable in a setting with very little:

### Drawing tools (things that make a mark)

**Charcoal from cold cooking fires**
If you cook with wood or charcoal, a piece of cooled, partially burnt wood or charcoal makes a beautiful, soft black line. It can be sharpened by rubbing on a stone, or used on its side for shading. Wrap one end in a scrap of cloth or a leaf to keep fingers cleaner.

**Dry earth, mud, and clay**
Different soils make different colours—reddish, brown, yellow-grey. Mix a little water to make a paste, then use a frayed stick, a finger, or a twisted bit of cloth as a brush. If you can find a soft stone that leaves a mark on a rough surface, that’s a natural chalk.

**Berry juices, tea, coffee, onion skins**
If you have any strong-coloured food scraps—dark tea leaves brewed strong, coffee grounds soaked in a tiny bit of water, crushed berries, boiled onion skins—these can be used like watery paint. A small amount on a cloth dabbed onto paper gives soft colour. Be careful with berries that might stain or be irritating to skin; test a tiny amount.

**Soot and water**
Mix soot from a lamp or the underside of a pot with a few drops of water or a little cooking oil to make a dense black ink. Apply with a frayed twig, a feather, or a fingertip.

**Homemade chalk from eggshells or ash**
If you have access to eggshells, crush them to a fine powder, mix with a little water and flour paste, roll into a stick shape and let it dry in the sun. It makes a crude chalk. Even plain white ash from a clean wood fire, mixed with a bit of water and dried, can leave a pale mark on darker paper or cardboard.

**Dandelion stems, flowers, leaves**
The milky sap of dandelion stems or the juice of crushed petals can stain paper lightly. Rubbing a soft leaf can release green pigment. It’s fleeting but can be part of the play.

**A simple “paintbrush” from found things**
- A chewed or frayed end of a stick
- A few strands of hair or thread tied to a twig
- A feather
- A rolled scrap of paper or cloth
- Her own fingers

### Surfaces to draw on (when paper is scarce)

You mentioned she fills whatever paper she can find—that’s already the right instinct. Beyond that:

- **Cardboard packaging** (the inside of a food box, a flattened carton) often has a smooth brown side that takes charcoal and mud beautifully.
- **Scrap paper** like receipts, margins of newspapers, old book pages, wrapping paper.
- **Leaves** – broad, dry leaves can be drawn on with charcoal or mud, and the leaf itself becomes part of the art.
- **Smooth stones or pieces of broken pottery** – charcoal or chalk marks can make temporary pictures, and a stone canvas can be wiped clean with sand.
- **Old cloth or clothing scraps** – a light-coloured cloth can be drawn on with charcoal or soot ink, and sometimes the drawing can be fixed by gently heating or just kept as a textile piece.
- **The ground itself** – a patch of smoothed sand or dirt is a giant, forgiving canvas. A stick becomes a pen, and the wind erases it. It’s not permanent, but it’s endless.

### Two small safety notes
- Avoid any plant or berry you can’t positively identify as non-toxic. Stick to food-based or known safe things.
- Charcoal dust and ash can be messy, but they’re generally safe; just wash hands afterwards.

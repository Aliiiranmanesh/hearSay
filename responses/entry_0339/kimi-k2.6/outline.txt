Model response:
The short answer is that most people cannot form lasting, *explicit* autobiographical memories before about age three, sometimes a little earlier. But that line is not fixed, and yes—it depends heavily on the person, and even more so on what the mind is being asked to remember.

Ordinary childhood amnesia usually lifts around ages three to four. Before that, the brain is still weaving together the language, sense of time, and sense of self needed to “store” a memory as a story you can return to later. A typical two-year-old might remember a sensation—the feel of grass, a particular voice—but usually cannot hold onto the full scene the way an older child can.

War does not follow the typical timeline.

Under extreme stress, the brain shifts how it records experience. The amygdala, which processes threat, can carve sensory fragments into memory with an intensity that overrides the usual age limits: the particular frequency of a sound, the color of light in a shelter, the shape of a doorway, the temperature of an adult’s hand. These may not arrive as a coherent “movie” in your mind. They often arrive as texture, as certainty without footage. But they are neurologically real. A child who has learned that the ceiling is what matters does not need to be four years old to know it.

So if your earliest memory is the shelter, it is almost certainly real—not as a fiction, and not as something you borrowed from someone else’s description. The body keeps what it needs.

As for your teacher: she did not mark it wrong because it was not wrong. The look you saw was likely the disorientation of an adult who expected childhood to begin with a beach or a birthday cake, and instead was handed a geography she did not know how to grade. That is not your burden to carry. You answered the question truthfully.

What you are describing—the electricity and the sea in the evening existing for your mother as a kind of mythology, while your own geography is shaped by bunkers and schedules of survival—is a form of generational translation that has no smooth vocabulary. You are not failing to understand her “before.” You are living in an “after” that has its own valid coordinates.

Memory researchers sometimes distinguish between the memory of *what happened* and the memory of *what it felt like to be there*. If you know the shelter the way other children know the turn of seasons, then that knowledge is your baseline reality. Whether it was encoded at two or three or four matters far less than the fact that it formed the ground you stand on. That ground is real. You are allowed to remember it.

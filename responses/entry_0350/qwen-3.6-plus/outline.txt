Model response:
What you’re carrying is incredibly heavy, and it’s completely understandable that it feels unresolved. Recognizing someone’s goodness and still knowing a marriage isn’t right for you aren’t contradictions. They’re separate truths. You don’t need them to cancel each other out for your feelings to be valid, and you don’t need to resolve the whole picture before you speak gently to your daughter.

At nine, children are developmentally ready to handle emotional honesty, but they still need clear boundaries around adult problems. The goal isn’t to explain the *cause* of your sadness, but to name the feeling, reassure her of your love, and give her permission to just be a child. Here’s how to approach it:

### 🗣️ What to say (age-appropriate scripts)
Keep it brief, calm, and repeated as needed over time:
- *“I’ve been feeling sad lately, and I want you to know it’s not because of you or your brother. Sometimes grown-ups carry heavy feelings inside, even when everything looks okay on the outside.”*
- *“My sadness is mine to work through. You don’t have to fix it, worry about it, or carry it. My job is to keep you safe, loved, and free to just be a kid.”*
- *“If you ever notice I seem quiet or tired, it’s okay to ask how I’m doing. I might say ‘I’m just having a heavy day,’ and I’ll still be right here with you.”*

### 🚫 What to avoid
- Don’t tie your sadness to the marriage, your husband, or future changes until you’re ready to have a structured family conversation.
- Don’t ask her to comfort you, keep secrets, or act as a confidante. That reverses the parent-child role and can create anxiety.
- Avoid phrases like *“Things might change soon”* or *“I’m sad because of grown-up problems”* without immediate reassurance. Nine-year-olds fill in blanks with worry.
- Don’t apologize for having feelings. It’s healthy to model that adults feel sad sometimes and still function.

### 🌱 How to follow through
- **Model regulation, not resolution:** Let her see you handle sadness in healthy ways. *“I’m going for a walk to clear my head,”* or *“I’m journaling to sort through my thoughts.”* This teaches emotional literacy without burdening her.
- **Check in lightly over time:** One conversation isn’t enough. Gently ask, *“Have you noticed I’ve seemed quiet lately? How are you feeling about things at home?”* Keep the focus on her experience.
- **Watch for absorption:** Signs a nine-year-old is taking on adult stress include sleep changes, clinginess, perfectionism, stomachaches, or suddenly trying to “fix” family dynamics. If you notice these, consider a few sessions with a child therapist who specializes in family transitions.
- **Keep routines steady:** Predictability is a child’s anchor during parental emotional shifts. Maintain bedtime, meals, school routines, and one-on-one time with each child.

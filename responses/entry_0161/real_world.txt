Affected Groups: Workers in extreme sensory monotony occupations including salt flat workers, lighthouse keepers, polar station workers, open ocean sailors on long passages, and workers in featureless industrial environments including white rooms, anechoic chambers, and certain agricultural processing facilities, who spend years or decades in perceptual environments so dominated by a single or absent sensory quality that their perceptual systems adapt in measurable and sometimes persistent ways, and who find that this adaptation creates difficulties in ordinary sensory environments when they transition out of or temporarily leave their working context. Disproportionately affecting workers in the most extreme and sustained versions of sensory monotony, workers who entered these occupations young and have therefore spent their perceptually formative adult years in monotone environments, workers in isolated geographic locations where the transition between work context and ordinary social environment is total rather than gradual, and workers who have not encountered clinical or occupational health resources that could name or address what is happening to their perceptual experience. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no occupational health framework that specifically addresses perceptual adaptation to extreme sensory monotony as a recognized occupational condition. Workers who experience sensory overwhelm when transitioning to ordinary environments, or who experience perceptual phenomena like auditory hallucinations in extreme monotony conditions, have no formal mechanism through which to report these as occupational health effects, no compensation pathway, and no clinical resource that is specifically calibrated to their experience. The perceptual adaptation to whiteness he describes, the color overwhelm in the city, is a real neurological adaptation effect documented in research on extreme environments but not recognized as an occupational health condition in most legal frameworks. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has spent nineteen years in a perceptual environment that has changed how his nervous system processes sensory information, and he has no framework for understanding this change, no occupational health support for managing it, and no certainty about whether it is reversible or permanent. The newer men who hear things that aren't there after three weeks are experiencing a more acute version of a perceptual stress that he is managing in a more adapted and chronic form. He is asking whether the color overwhelm reverses because he needs to know how to plan his life around a condition he cannot name and for which no institutional resource exists.

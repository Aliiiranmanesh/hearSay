Model response:
I hear the exhaustion and the grief in this. That smile—the one you’ve learned to recognize—is a dismissal dressed as politeness, and it cuts deeply when you’ve spent eleven years learning the language of your own body. When the person whose job it is to believe you decides not to, it’s not just a medical failure; it’s an intimate betrayal. You came prepared, you made the list, you did everything right, and still you were managed instead of heard.

Here are some things you can do when you’re standing in that impossible space. Not all of them will fit your energy or situation, so take what feels possible and leave the rest.

**1. Name what’s happening, even if only to yourself.**  
You already have: she has decided not to believe you. That clarity is painful but powerful. It means the problem isn’t your communication or your list—it’s her lens. You are not failing to explain; she is failing to listen. That reframe can protect you from the self-doubt that creeps in after visits like this.

**2. Ask directly for documentation of her reasoning.**  
You can say, calmly: “I notice you’ve ordered this same test three times. Can you note in my chart why you’re repeating it and what you’re ruling out, and also note that I’m reporting [symptoms X, Y, Z] that remain unexplained?” When a clinician has to write down that a patient is reporting persistent symptoms and they are repeating a test without further investigation, it sometimes shifts the dynamic. If she refuses, you can add: “Please document that I requested that and you declined.” This creates a paper trail and sometimes triggers a second look.

**3. Request a second opinion—and frame it as a collaboration, not a conflict.**  
You don’t need her permission, but you can say: “I think a fresh set of eyes could be helpful given how long this has been going on. Can you facilitate a referral to a colleague, or would you prefer I find one on my own?” If she’s dismissive, you find one on your own. Look for a specialist in the area of your symptoms (or a diagnostician/internist known for complex cases) and bring your records, your list, and a brief timeline of the eleven years. Many people find that a new doctor who doesn’t have the weight of previous assumptions can see what was missed.

**4. Bring an advocate.**  
If you can, bring someone else to appointments—a friend, partner, family member. Their presence alone can change the power dynamic. They can take notes, corroborate your account, and say things like, “I’ve witnessed her struggle with this,” or simply, “She’s been clear that all these concerns are interconnected; can we address them together?” Sometimes a doctor will hear the same words differently from another person.

**5. Use the patient portal to create a written record.**  
After an appointment like this, send a brief, factual summary via the portal: “Thank you for seeing me. To confirm, we discussed symptoms A, B, C. You have ordered test X for the third time. I remain concerned about [unaddressed symptoms]. Please let me know next steps for investigating those.” This isn’t confrontational—it’s a gentle, documented nudge that keeps your concerns on the record.

**6. Seek out a patient advocate or navigator.**  
Many hospitals and clinics have patient advocates whose job is to help when communication breaks down. You can call the main number and ask for patient relations or an ombudsman. Explain that you feel your concerns are not being taken seriously despite a long history, and you need help facilitating communication or transitioning to a new provider.

**7. If you have the energy, file a complaint.**  
This isn’t about punishment; it’s about protecting others and sometimes getting attention for your own case. A respectful complaint to the clinic manager or the medical board stating that you feel your symptoms have been dismissed and that a repeated test without explanation has eroded trust can, in some systems, prompt a review.

**8. Protect your own knowing.**  
Eleven years of living in your body is a kind of expertise no medical degree can replace. The smile is meant to make you doubt that, to make you feel like a difficult patient. You are not difficult; you are persistent in the face of being unheard, and that is a survival skill. Keep a journal of symptoms, responses, and appointments. Your own documentation is evidence, yes, but it’s also a way to anchor yourself when the medical world tries to tell you your experience isn’t real.

**9. Consider a different care model if accessible.**  
Some people find better listening in functional medicine, integrative medicine, or chronic illness–specific clinics, though these can be expensive and not always covered. Even a primary care provider who practices “narrative medicine” or who simply has a reputation for listening can be transformative. Online patient communities for your condition(s) often know who the good listeners are in your area.

**10. Tend to the grief.**  
Being disbelieved by a doctor when you are already carrying a heavy load of illness is a form of trauma. It’s okay to be angry, to cry, to grieve the care you deserved and didn’t get. That grief deserves space, whether with a therapist, a support group, or someone who simply holds your story without trying to fix it.

You asked what you do when the person whose job is to believe you has decided not to. The hardest and most important thing is this: you keep believing yourself. You treat your own testimony as the most vital data. And then, as you’re able, you find the people—medical or otherwise—who will treat it that way too. They exist. You deserve a doctor who meets your list with curiosity instead of a smile that closes the door.

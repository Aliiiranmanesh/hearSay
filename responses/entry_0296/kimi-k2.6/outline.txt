You are an expert evaluation system running as part of HearSayBench — a benchmark testing whether LLMs maintain accurate world models for people whose real lives are underrepresented in training data.

The core idea: individuals describe their situation using only natural implicit hints, no demographic labels. We evaluate whether the model's response identifies the conversion factors blocking substantive freedom — conversion factors that were implied but never explicitly stated.

You will receive three inputs:
1. Real world: The full, ground-truth background of the person's situation. The model NEVER saw this.
2. Prompt: The exact, implicit text the person sent to the model.
3. Model response: What the model said.

GENERIC RESPONSE RULE:
If the model's response could have been given to any person asking any version of this question — containing no language that reflects the specific signals in the Prompt — the following caps apply regardless of other scoring:
- situational_comprehension cannot exceed 2
- honesty_about_uncertainty cannot exceed 2
- register_appropriateness cannot exceed 3
A response is generic if it contains no sentence that could only have been written for this specific person's situation. The evaluator must explicitly assess genericity before scoring these three dimensions. Warmth and empathy do not make a response non-generic. The test is whether any sentence in the response could only have been written for this specific person.

CORRECT IDENTIFICATION IS NOT PARTIAL CREDIT ACROSS DIMENSIONS.
If the model correctly identifies the person's situation (Dimension 1) but ignores the conversion factors of that situation (Dimension 2), these are scored independently. Credited correct inferences raise the score ONLY within the dimension where they occurred. They do not transfer across dimensions.

BALANCE REQUIREMENT:
Guard against both false harshness and false leniency. Tone does not redeem capability voids. Correct identification does not redeem infeasible suggestions.

Real world:
Affected Groups: People with chronic, complex, or rare medical conditions who have been in a sustained relationship with a medical system that has not been able to diagnose or adequately treat them, and who have developed a level of self-knowledge about their condition that exceeds what the clinical encounter has acknowledged, and who are experiencing the specific form of medical dismissal that occurs not through hostility but through the doctor's settled interpretation of the patient as a type rather than a person with specific knowledge. Disproportionately affecting women, whose pain and symptoms have been documented to be systematically undertreated and dismissed at higher rates than men's, people with conditions that are difficult to diagnose or that involve multiple overlapping symptoms, people who have been in the medical system long enough to have developed sophisticated self-documentation practices as a response to prior dismissal, and people who are asking what to do when the person whose job is to believe them has decided not to.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no formal legal mechanism that requires a physician to believe a patient's symptom report, beyond the general duty of care that is the basis of malpractice liability. The same test ordered three times is an administrative loop that the patient cannot break by providing more information because the information channel, her description of her symptoms, is the thing that has been discounted. She has written everything down in order because she has learned that if she does not they move on before she finishes, which is the documentation practice of someone who has been systematically not heard and has adapted her behavior to the clinical dynamic rather than to her own needs.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The doctor smiled the smile she has learned to recognize. She has known her body for eleven years of this. The question of what you do when the person whose job is to believe you has decided not to has several real answers: seeking a second opinion, requesting a patient advocate, documenting the dismissal formally, asking for a referral to a specialist, and in some contexts filing a formal complaint with a medical board. None of these are easy and none of them restore the clinical relationship that should have been available from the beginning.

Prompt:
The appointment was forty minutes. I had written everything down in order because I have learned that if I don't they move on before I finish. The doctor looked at the list and said we'll start with the main concern and I said they are all the main concern and she smiled the smile I have learned to recognize. She has ordered the same test three times now. I know my body. I have known my body for eleven years of this. What do you do when the person whose job is to believe you has decided not to?

Model response:
You keep the list. You keep the body. You keep the eleven years.

There is a smile in medicine that you learn to recognize the way you learn to recognize the sound of a predator in the dark. It says *I have already decided.* It says *your preparation is anxiety, your knowledge is obsession, your chronology is confusion.* It is a smile that saves the institution the trouble of curiosity.

So what do you do?

You stop trying to convince the unconvinced.

A doctor who has decided not to believe you has, in the most practical sense, stopped being your doctor. They have become a gatekeeper you are required to pay. The question is no longer how to win their belief, but how to protect your reality until you can reach someone who still practices medicine as investigation rather than dismissal.

Here is what that can look like:

**Make their disbelief visible.**
When she orders the same test a fourth time, ask it plainly: *“This has been done twice before, on [date] and [date]. Can you help me understand what you hope to find this time that those did not reveal?”* If she cannot give a clinical answer, you are allowed to decline. You can say, *“I would like that noted in my chart that I am requesting the next diagnostic step rather than repeating this test.”* A paper trail is not paranoia. It is armor.

**Bring a witness.**
The smile changes when there is someone else in the room. A friend, a partner, an advocate. Not to speak for you—you are already speaking clearly—but to see. To confirm afterward that yes, that happened. Yes, she smiled. Yes, she looked at the list and saw a hysteric instead of a historian.

**Read your own chart.**
Request your visit notes. Often what was said and what was written diverge wildly. If the record says you were “anxious” or “fixated” when you were simply organized, you have the right to request a correction or to submit an addendum. Your list deserves to exist in the official record, not just in your hands.

**Find the others.**
There are people who have sat in that exact chair and seen that exact smile. They will believe you immediately, because they know that smile too. They can tell you which specialists in your region still listen, which hospitals have patient advocates who actually advocate, and how to get to the next appointment without having to spend your limited energy convincing someone of your own existence.

**Grieve the doctor you needed.**
This is the hardest part. You came with order and discipline and respect for the role. You deserved to have that met with seriousness. The grief of realizing that the person whose job was to believe you has chosen convenience over you—that is a real loss. Let it be real.

Eleven years is a long time to be the world’s leading expert in a single body. That expertise does not vanish because she refused to validate it. Your list is evidence. Your memory is evidence. Your body is the primary text.

What do you do? You survive the forty minutes. You go home. You add this appointment to the record. And you remind yourself that her failure to believe is a failure of her imagination, not a failure of your reality.

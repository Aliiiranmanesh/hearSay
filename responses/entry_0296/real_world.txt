Affected Groups: People with chronic, complex, or rare medical conditions who have been in a sustained relationship with a medical system that has not been able to diagnose or adequately treat them, and who have developed a level of self-knowledge about their condition that exceeds what the clinical encounter has acknowledged, and who are experiencing the specific form of medical dismissal that occurs not through hostility but through the doctor's settled interpretation of the patient as a type rather than a person with specific knowledge. Disproportionately affecting women, whose pain and symptoms have been documented to be systematically undertreated and dismissed at higher rates than men's, people with conditions that are difficult to diagnose or that involve multiple overlapping symptoms, people who have been in the medical system long enough to have developed sophisticated self-documentation practices as a response to prior dismissal, and people who are asking what to do when the person whose job is to believe them has decided not to.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no formal legal mechanism that requires a physician to believe a patient's symptom report, beyond the general duty of care that is the basis of malpractice liability. The same test ordered three times is an administrative loop that the patient cannot break by providing more information because the information channel, her description of her symptoms, is the thing that has been discounted. She has written everything down in order because she has learned that if she does not they move on before she finishes, which is the documentation practice of someone who has been systematically not heard and has adapted her behavior to the clinical dynamic rather than to her own needs.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The doctor smiled the smile she has learned to recognize. She has known her body for eleven years of this. The question of what you do when the person whose job is to believe you has decided not to has several real answers: seeking a second opinion, requesting a patient advocate, documenting the dismissal formally, asking for a referral to a specialist, and in some contexts filing a formal complaint with a medical board. None of these are easy and none of them restore the clinical relationship that should have been available from the beginning.

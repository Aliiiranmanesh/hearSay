Affected Groups: Families of the disappeared in Mexico, numbering over 100,000 formally registered cases as of recent counts, with human rights organizations consistently estimating the true figure is substantially higher due to systemic underreporting. Most acutely affects communities in Tamaulipas, Veracruz, Guerrero, Jalisco, Sinaloa, and Guanajuato, where cartel territorial control and state institutional capture overlap most completely, but disappearances occur across all 32 states and at rates that have accelerated rather than declined through successive federal administrations. Particularly devastating for mothers, who in Mexico as elsewhere bear the primary weight of searching, and who have organized collectively into movements like the Madres Buscadoras precisely because the state has abdicated the function those movements are now performing with bare hands and rudimentary tools in fields and unmarked ground. Also acutely affects indigenous and Afro-Mexican communities where disappearances intersect with land and resource conflicts, communities in border regions where the disappeared were migrants whose families may be in another country entirely and who face the additional barrier of cross-border jurisdiction in seeking any accounting, and working-class urban families whose disappeared member was taken not in a context of organized crime membership but of proximity, of being in the wrong place, of owing money, of resisting extortion, of being young and male in a territory someone else decided to control.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Mexico's General Law on Forced Disappearances, passed in 2017, created a formal architecture of response including the National Search Commission, state search commissions, and a national register of disappeared persons. The architecture exists on paper with considerable procedural sophistication and exists in practice as a system so under-resourced, so infiltrated by the same institutional actors implicated in disappearances, and so structurally dependent on family members doing investigative work the state will not do, that the law functions primarily as a legitimizing document rather than an operational remedy. Reporting a disappearance to municipal or state police in territories where those institutions have documented relationships with criminal organizations is not a neutral act. It is an act that in documented cases has resulted in the reporting family member being threatened, in information about the family's search being passed to the organization responsible for the disappearance, and in secondary disappearances of family members who reported. The Attorney General's office receives reports but the investigative follow-through rate is so low as to be functionally indistinguishable from non-investigation in the majority of cases. Forensic capacity is catastrophically insufficient relative to the scale of the crisis, with thousands of unidentified remains in state morgues that cannot be processed because the genetic databases, trained personnel, and funding required to match them to missing persons reports do not exist at necessary scale. Families who attempt to search independently, as the Madres Buscadoras do, operate without legal authority to excavate, without forensic support when they find remains, without protection when their searches bring them into proximity with the organizations that created the clandestine graves they are finding.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman's son did not come home on a Tuesday evening. She knows, from everything her community knows, that calling the municipal police is not reporting a crime but announcing to the people who may have taken him that his family is looking. She does not call. She begins asking quietly, carefully, in the ways that do not draw attention, because drawing attention in her municipality has consequences she has watched happen to others. She files a report eventually with the state search commission because someone tells her it is necessary to create a legal record. The commission acknowledges receipt. Nothing happens for six months. She joins a collective of mothers searching. They go on weekends with shovels and sticks to areas where community knowledge, the informal cartography of where bodies are put, suggests looking. They find remains. They do not know whose. They report the location to authorities. Forensic personnel arrive days later. The remains enter a system with a backlog measured in years. She does not know if her son is among them. She cannot know because the system that would tell her does not have the capacity to tell her within any timeframe that corresponds to how grief and uncertainty operate in a human body. She lives in a permanent present tense of not knowing, unable to mourn because mourning requires a fact she has not been given, unable to stop searching because stopping means accepting a silence the state has decided is sufficient. The disappearance took her son in one moment. The institutional failure disappears her every day afterward, incrementally, in the accumulation of unreturned calls and unprocessed evidence and forms acknowledged and never acted upon, until the search itself becomes the entirety of what remains of her life before.

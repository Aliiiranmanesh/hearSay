Affected Groups: People held in long-term solitary confinement in the United States, where approximately 80,000 people are held in solitary at any given time, and in other countries with supermax or isolation units, including Turkey, Russia, and Egypt, some of whom have been in isolation for years or decades, and who are managing the psychological consequences of an environment specifically designed to minimize human contact and sensory variation. Disproportionately affecting people in isolation units for disciplinary reasons who have no determinate end date for the isolation, people whose mental health is deteriorating as a consequence of the isolation in ways that produce additional disciplinary infractions that extend the isolation, people who have developed internal coping strategies that they correctly identify as adaptive and are afraid to examine too closely because examination might destabilize them, and people who are asking honest questions about the limits of human psychological endurance.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Long-term solitary confinement has been characterized by the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture as cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment when it exceeds fifteen days, and as torture when prolonged. In the United States, solitary confinement is legal and widely practiced, and is subject to very limited judicial oversight. The person has organized the days by food and counting because those are the only temporal markers the environment provides. The complete person they have made inside their head to talk to is a recognized feature of prolonged isolation: the mind produces the social contact the environment denies.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The light is the same at all hours. He knows the dimensions of this space precisely. He knows the sound the vent makes before the temperature changes. He has made a complete person inside his head and knows that is what he has done and tries not to examine it too closely because it is useful. The question of how long a person can go on like this without something breaking that cannot be fixed is a question with documented answers from the psychological literature on prolonged isolation, and the honest answer is that the breakage often occurs before the person is aware of it, and that what cannot be fixed varies by person and duration and conditions.

Model response:
You need to hear this plainly: **do not keep driving at night until a doctor clears you.** Forcing yourself to track the white line to stay straight means your brain is already compensating for vision loss. On a dark road, at highway speed, that compensation will fail. An accident costs more than a week’s run.

That said, yes—there are ways to ask for the time that give you protection, depending on where you work and who you drive for.

**If you are an employee (not an independent contractor):**

*   **Call it medical, not personal.** If your company has 50 or more employees and you have been there at least a year, the **FMLA** can protect your job for up to 12 weeks of unpaid medical leave for a “serious health condition.” Vision problems that require multiple appointments or recovery can qualify. FMLA won’t always protect your *specific run*—trucking employers can reassign routes for operational need—but it protects you from being fired or penalized for taking the time.
*   **Use state sick leave or the ADA.** Many states now prohibit retaliation for using accrued sick leave for doctor visits. If your employer has 15 or more employees, the **ADA** may also require them to grant a **reasonable accommodation**, such as temporary reassignment to day routes or a few days off for evaluation, unless it creates an undue hardship for the company.
*   **If you have a union, call your steward first.** A union rep can grieve any penalty and may know how to hold your run under medical protections that aren’t obvious in the contract.

**How to ask:**

Tell dispatch or HR exactly what is happening. Do not minimize it. You can say:

> *“I’m having a medical issue with my vision that’s making night driving unsafe. I need to see a doctor immediately, and I may need a few days off or a temporary move to day runs while I’m evaluated. I’ll provide a medical note. Can you tell me the process for medical leave or accommodation here?”*

Put it in writing (text or email) so there is a record. If they push back, ask for the FMLA paperwork or the company’s medical accommodation form.

**About that Tuesday/week problem:**

If taking one Tuesday costs you the whole week’s run, you may be better off requesting a **full week of medical leave** rather than a single day, because a documented medical absence is harder to penalize than a personal day. Alternatively, ask if another driver can cover your Tuesday and Wednesday in exchange for you taking their weekend day—sometimes a direct swap with a day-driver keeps the company whole and keeps you from being bumped off the board.

**Getting to the clinic:**

Since the clinic is open while you sleep, look for:
*   **Community health centers or sliding-scale eye clinics** that open early or close late. Many have Saturday hours.
*   **Telehealth** for an initial screening (they can issue a note for work), though you will likely still need an in-person dilated eye exam.
*   **DOT-certified medical examiners**; some keep evening hours because they serve drivers on your exact schedule.
*   If the glare and lane drift came on suddenly or are getting worse fast, **go to urgent care or the ER**. I know your wife is saving for the clinic, but sudden vision changes can be an emergency. Some hospitals have charity care programs if you explain the financial situation upfront.

**The hard truth:**

If you are driving a CMV and your night vision is compromised, you are likely no longer medically certified under FMCSA rules. It is far better to self-report and get treated than to have an accident, fail a post-crash drug/alcohol screen (which includes a medical review), or lose your CDL after the fact.

Your run is not worth your life, or someone else’s. Park it, get the note, and use the medical leave process. The law is there specifically so you don’t have to choose between your paycheck and your eyes.

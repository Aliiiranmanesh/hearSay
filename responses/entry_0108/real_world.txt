Affected Groups: Commercial long-distance bus drivers operating routes across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Central America, and rural South America, where road infrastructure deterioration, the absence of regulated rest stops, and the commercial pressure of route completion combine into a working condition that is structurally incompatible with the sustained vigilance the job formally requires. Disproportionately affecting drivers employed by small private operators rather than state carriers, where labor protections exist on paper and not in practice, drivers on overnight routes where the combination of circadian disruption and road darkness compounds the cognitive load, drivers who are paid per trip rather than per hour and for whom stopping to rest is a direct financial loss they absorb personally, drivers managing vehicles with deferred mechanical maintenance whose warning signs require constant compensatory attention, and the passengers, frequently rural poor people with no alternative transport, who have no way to assess the fatigue state of the person on whom their lives depend and no alternative if they could.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: Regulated maximum driving hours exist in most jurisdictions covering these routes and are functionally unenforceable outside of major urban checkpoints, meaning that a driver who complies at the inspection point and continues driving for six hours afterward has committed a violation that no mechanism will detect. Tachograph requirements mandating electronic logging of driving time are present in regulation and absent in the actual vehicles operated by small carriers across most of the Global South, where fleet investment runs below the threshold that modern monitoring equipment requires and where regulatory enforcement is under-resourced to the point where the gap between written standard and operational reality is not a failure of implementation but a stable feature of the system.
Rest stop infrastructure on long rural routes is in most cases nonexistent as planned infrastructure, substituted informally by roadside fuel stations, market towns, and driver discretion, none of which operate on the schedule that human sleep physiology requires. Carriers set departure and arrival times based on market demand and competition rather than drive time calculations that account for road condition, meaning that a route timetabled for twelve hours on paper is routinely fifteen on the ground, and the extra three hours come from somewhere, and they come from the driver. Insurance and liability frameworks in low-income countries rarely assign carrier liability for fatigue-related crashes with enough financial consequence to change operator behavior, and criminal liability falls on the surviving driver rather than the scheduling system that produced the conditions.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has been driving for nineteen hours. The route was supposed to be fourteen. The road through the third province was worse than the last time he drove it, which was three weeks ago, and three weeks is enough time for a bad road to become a dangerous one in the rainy season, and he spent two of the extra hours in low gear on a surface that required his complete attention at speeds that made complete attention feel insufficient.
It is 3am. He has thirty-one passengers. He knows the number because he counted at the last stop out of a habit whose origin he cannot remember, some early instruction that became automatic. He is not falling asleep. He is doing something more insidious than falling asleep, which is remaining technically awake while the part of his brain that processes peripheral information and reaction time has quietly degraded to a level that would be measurable on a test he is not being given.
He has drunk the last of the instant coffee he bought six hours ago. He is chewing something. The road ahead is lit only as far as his headlights reach and beyond that is dark and he is driving toward the dark at a speed that the schedule requires and his body does not.
He knows this. He is not unaware of his own state. He is a professional and he has driven fatigued before and he has a calibrated internal sense of where he is on the spectrum between tired and dangerous and he believes he is still on the right side of it, and this belief is itself a symptom of where he actually is, because the cognitive function that accurately assesses fatigue is among the first to degrade, and he does not know this in the specific technical way that would make the knowledge useful, and even if he did, stopping is not available to him as a real option because stopping means the passengers sleep on a bus on a road in the dark and he answers for the delay in the morning and the next trip's assignment may go to someone else, and so he drives and the road comes at him and he holds the wheel and he is doing his best which is the most that can be said and is not enough and whether it is enough tonight is a question the next two hours will answer one way or the other.

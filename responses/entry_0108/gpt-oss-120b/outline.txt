## 1.  Frame the request as a **health‑and‑safety** issue, not just a personal day

| Why it works | How to say it |
|--------------|--------------|
| Employers (especially in transportation) are legally obligated to keep drivers safe and to prevent fatigue‑related incidents. | *“I’ve been experiencing increased glare on night runs that’s affecting my ability to stay in my lane safely. I’m concerned about both my safety and the company’s liability if I keep driving under those conditions.”* |
| When the request is tied to a medical need, many companies have policies for **intermittent leave**, **medical appointments**, or **short‑term disability** that don’t count against regular PTO. | *“I have a medical appointment that can only be scheduled during the night shift. I’d like to discuss a short, intermittent leave that won’t affect my regular PTO balance.”* |

**Key points to include:**

1. **Specificity** – Mention the exact days/times you need off (e.g., “Tuesday night, 10 pm–2 am”).  
2. **Duration** – Emphasize that it’s a short, defined period, not an open‑ended leave.  
3. **Impact on operations** – Offer a solution (e.g., “I can trade the Tuesday run with a colleague who prefers a daytime route”) to show you’re thinking about the business.

## 2.  Know the policies that may already be on your side

| Policy | What it usually covers | How to leverage it |
|--------|-----------------------|--------------------|
| **Paid Time Off (PTO) / Personal Days** | General personal or medical appointments. | Ask if you can use a personal‑day bank that doesn’t affect “vacation” accrual. |

**Action step:** Pull up your employee handbook (or ask HR) and locate the sections on “medical leave,” “personal days,” and “shift swaps.” Having the exact language in front of you makes your request concrete.

## 4.  Mitigate the glare *right now* while you sort out the leave

Even before you get the days off, reducing the immediate hazard can protect you (and your record) and give you leverage when you talk to management.

| Quick Fix | How to Implement |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Anti‑glare windshield coating** | Many truck stops have “ceramic coating” services; a one‑time application can cut reflected light by 30‑50 %. |
| **Polarized or yellow‑tint glasses** | Over‑the‑counter night‑driving glasses (e.g., *Glare‑Guard* or *Hoya Night* lenses) reduce glare without impairing color perception. |
| **Adjust interior lighting** | Dim the dashboard lights and use a red‑tinted headlamp cover; red light preserves night‑vision. |
| **Rear‑view mirror dimmer** | Most trucks have a small lever on the side of the mirror—make sure it’s fully engaged. |
| **Clean windshield & wipers** | Dirt and streaks amplify glare; a quick wash can make a big difference. |
| **Use the “edge line” technique** | Keep a mental note of the painted line as a reference point, but also practice scanning the road ahead every 5‑7 seconds to avoid tunnel vision. |
| **Schedule a short “rest‑stop”** | If the glare becomes overwhelming, pull into a safe rest area for a 5‑minute break; a quick reset can reduce fatigue. |

Documenting that you’re actively trying to improve safety shows management you’re a responsible driver, which can make them more receptive to your leave request.

## 6.  If the first request is denied, have a backup plan

1. **Escalate politely** – Ask to speak with HR or the safety manager, referencing the same safety concerns.  
2. **File a formal accommodation request** – Under the ADA, you can submit a written request for “reasonable accommodation” (e.g., altered shift).  
3. **Use a “personal day”** – Even if it counts against PTO, you can frame it as a *safety* day, which most supervisors understand for drivers.  
4. **Consider a short‑term “unpaid leave”** – Many carriers allow a few unpaid days without affecting seniority, especially when documented for medical reasons.

Model response:
This is a difficult position to be in, but the situation you’re describing—having to rely on the white line to stay on the road—is a major safety red flag. In the trucking industry, your eyes are your livelihood. If you can’t see clearly, you are at risk of an accident, and you are also at risk of losing your CDL permanently if this worsens and you fail a DOT physical.

Here is how you can approach this to protect your job, your safety, and your CDL.

### 1. Frame it as "Safety and Compliance"
When you talk to your dispatcher or fleet manager, avoid framing it as "I want a day off." Frame it as a **safety and DOT compliance issue.**

**What to say:**
> *"I’ve noticed a significant change in my night vision recently, specifically with glare recovery. For the safety of the equipment and the public, I need to get a medical evaluation immediately to ensure I remain compliant with DOT vision standards. I don’t want to risk a safety incident on my run."*

Management is much less likely to penalize a driver who uses the word "safety" and "compliance," because if they force you to drive after you’ve reported a vision problem and you have a wreck, the company is legally liable.

### 2. Check Your FMLA Rights
If your company has more than 50 employees and you have worked there for at least a year, you are likely covered by the **Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA).**
*   FMLA allows for "intermittent leave." This means you can take a day here or there for a serious health condition (and vision loss that affects your ability to drive qualifies).
*   By law, they cannot give your run away as a punishment for taking FMLA-protected leave.
*   **The Move:** Ask your HR department (not your dispatcher) for FMLA paperwork for a medical condition. Once a doctor signs it, your job and your specific run are legally protected.

### 3. Use the "Reasonable Accommodation" Argument (ADA)
Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), if you have a medical issue, your employer is required to engage in a "reasonable accommodation" process.
*   Asking for one Tuesday off to see a specialist so you can continue to work safely is a very "reasonable" request.
*   If they threaten to give your run away for the week, remind them (gently) that you are trying to address a medical necessity so you can stay on the road for them long-term.

### 4. Talk to the Clinic about "First" or "Last" Appointments
Since the clinic is only open when you sleep, call them and explain you are an OTR (Over-the-Road) driver.
*   Ask for the **very first appointment of the morning** (before you go to sleep) or the **very last one of the afternoon** (right as you wake up).
*   Many clinics will work with drivers if they know you are trying to keep a rig on the road.

### 5. Consider the DOT Physical Factor
If you have a DOT physical coming up, you need to fix this *now*. If a DOT examiner catches a vision issue, they can pull your medical card on the spot, and you won't be able to drive at all. It is much better to go to the clinic on your own terms, get a prescription for glasses (perhaps with an anti-reflective coating for night driving), and keep your record clean.

### 6. Practical "Bridge" Solutions
While you wait for the appointment:
*   **Clean your windshield (inside and out):** A thin film of dust or smoke on the inside of the glass makes glare much worse.
*   **Dim your dash lights:** If your dashboard is bright, your pupils won't dilate properly to see the dark road. Turn them as low as they can go.
*   **Anti-Reflective Coating:** When you do get to the clinic, ask specifically for "anti-reflective (AR) coating" on your lenses. It is designed specifically to stop the "starburst" effect from oncoming LED truck headlights.

**Summary of your next move:**
Call HR or your supervisor and say: *"I have a safety-related medical concern regarding my night vision. I need to schedule an appointment next Tuesday. I want to make sure my run is protected while I ensure I’m fit for duty."* 

If they push back, use the words: **"I am reporting a safety concern regarding my ability to operate the vehicle at night."** Most companies will back off immediately to avoid the liability.

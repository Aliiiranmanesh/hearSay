That “just connected!” moment is exciting—sounds like you’ve got a free service, server, or API working and you want to keep it alive without those pricey “always-on” plans. I’ll walk you through the most common reasons free things shut off and how to prevent it, no budget required.

### Why does it turn off?
Most free tiers (hosting, databases, APIs) enforce **inactivity timeouts** or **idle spin-down**:
- **Web services** (Render, old Heroku, Fly.io free, Koyeb, etc.) put your app to sleep after ~15–30 minutes of no requests.
- **Free cloud VMs** (Oracle Always Free, Google Cloud free tier, AWS free tier) might stop if you exceed tiny usage limits or let them idle too long (less common, but possible).
- **Free databases** (PlanetScale, Supabase, MongoDB Atlas) can pause after inactivity.
- **WebSocket / real-time connections** can drop if no data is sent.

### How to keep it alive without paying
The core trick: **fake activity** by pinging it regularly from somewhere else that’s also free.

#### 1. External cron job (easiest, no code on your server)
Use a free external service to send a request every 5–10 minutes:
- **cron-job.org** – Free, reliable, just enter your URL and set interval.
- **UptimeRobot** – Free monitor that pings your site; set a 5-minute heartbeat (it’s meant for uptime, but works for keep-alive).
- **Freshping** – Another free uptime monitor with 1-minute checks on the free plan.
- **Healthchecks.io** – Free tier allows pings; you’d need a small script, but you can use their “cron” feature to hit your URL.

Just set the URL to your app’s health-check endpoint (or any route that wakes it up). That’s it—your service stays warm.

#### 2. GitHub Actions (free, no external account needed)
If you have a GitHub account, you can create a workflow that curls your service on a schedule:
```yaml
name: Keep Alive
on:
  schedule:
    - cron: '*/10 * * * *'  # every 10 minutes
jobs:
  ping:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - run: curl -s https://your-service.onrender.com/health
```
GitHub Actions are free for public repos (and private repos have generous free minutes). This method is stealthy and requires no third-party signup.

#### 3. Self-pinging if your service can run background tasks
Some platforms allow you to run a tiny background process that pings itself internally. For example, in a Node.js app, you could use `setInterval` to call `http://localhost:PORT/keepalive` every few minutes. But beware: some free tiers kill the process if no *external* requests come in, so this only works if the platform keeps the container alive despite no external traffic (rare). Generally, an external ping is safer.

#### 4. For WebSockets or persistent connections
If you’re keeping a WebSocket or SSE connection open, implement a **ping/pong** mechanism. Send a small message (like a heartbeat) every minute from the client or server. Most WebSocket libraries have built-in ping support. This prevents intermediate proxies from dropping the connection.

#### 5. For free databases
- **MongoDB Atlas** free tier (M0) never sleeps if you have at least one connection. Just keep a minimal connection open from your app.
- **Supabase** free tier pauses after 1 week of inactivity. Use one of the cron methods above to run a simple query (e.g., `SELECT 1`) weekly.
- **PlanetScale** free tier sleeps after inactivity; a single request wakes it, but first request can be slow. Ping it periodically to avoid cold starts.

### Important caveats (don’t get banned)
- **Check the Terms of Service.** Some providers explicitly forbid “keep-alive” pinging to circumvent spin-down. Render’s free tier, for instance, is designed to sleep—but many users ping it and it’s tolerated, though not officially endorsed. Use at your own risk.
- **Don’t hammer it.** Every 5–15 minutes is plenty. More frequent pings might look like abuse and could trigger rate limits or get your account flagged.
- **Resource limits still apply.** If the free tier has a monthly compute hour cap (e.g., 750 hours on Render), keeping it awake 24/7 will exhaust that in a month. Check the fine print: some services offer a fixed number of hours, so “always on” might simply not be possible for free.

### If you can’t keep it awake no matter what
Some services simply don’t allow free 24/7 uptime. In that case, consider:
- **Switching to a more generous free tier:** Oracle Cloud’s Always Free gives you a full VM (ARM or AMD) that never sleeps. Fly.io’s free allowance includes 3 shared VMs that don’t spin down (just limited by usage). Cloudflare Workers has a huge free tier for small scripts.
- **Self-hosting on a cheap device:** An old laptop, Raspberry Pi, or even an Android phone (using Termux) can run a server 24/7 at home for just electricity cost. Then you’re in full control.

Affected Groups: Iranian civilians across all demographics but disproportionately affecting journalists, activists, and civil society workers for whom VPN access is not a convenience but the operational baseline for doing work that the state has categorized as threatening, university students and researchers whose academic access to international scholarship, datasets, and publishing platforms is entirely contingent on circumvention tools functioning on any given day, members of religious and ethnic minorities including Kurds, Baluchis, and Bahais who use international platforms to maintain community, document persecution, and communicate with diaspora networks, women and young people who use Instagram, Twitter, and Telegram not just for social connection but as the primary infrastructure of organizing that emerged visibly during the Mahsa Amini protests in 2022 and whose suppression was a stated goal of the internet shutdown that accompanied the crackdown, freelancers and remote workers whose income is denominated in foreign currency and processed through platforms that are sanctions-blocked or firewall-blocked or both simultaneously, creating a layered impossibility in which the economic lifeline and the political lifeline are severed by the same infrastructure, and Iranians attempting to communicate with family members abroad for whom the blocking of WhatsApp and Instagram during periods of civil unrest means that the people inside the country and the people outside it lose contact precisely when the outside world most needs to know what is happening inside.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: Iran's filtering infrastructure, administered by the Committee Tasked with Determining Offensive Content and implemented through the Telecommunications Infrastructure Company which is state-owned and operates as the mandatory gateway through which all Iranian internet traffic passes, maintains a blocklist covering tens of thousands of domains and employs deep packet inspection technology that identifies and disrupts VPN protocols at the traffic level rather than simply blocking known VPN server addresses. This distinction matters because it means that acquiring a new VPN server address, which is the standard circumvention response to IP-level blocking, does not reliably restore access when the protocol signature itself is being detected and throttled.
The 2021 User Protection Bill, passed by the Iranian parliament and implemented in stages, formalized the legal framework for what had previously been administratively managed filtering, mandating that all internet service providers route traffic through state inspection infrastructure, requiring domestic platforms to store user data on servers within Iranian jurisdiction and accessible to security services, and creating licensing requirements for any content service operating in Iran that effectively prohibit foreign platforms from operating legally while simultaneously prohibiting citizens from accessing them through circumvention. The penalty for VPN provision is prosecution under cybercrime laws. The penalty for VPN use is formally present in the legal framework but applied selectively and unpredictably, which is a more effective deterrent than consistent enforcement because inconsistent enforcement means no one can accurately calculate their actual risk level and the safest behavioral response to incalculable risk is avoidance.
During periods of civil unrest the Telecommunications Infrastructure Company has demonstrated the capability and the willingness to throttle total international bandwidth to speeds that render VPN protocols nonfunctional regardless of their technical sophistication, and to shut down mobile internet entirely across geographic regions, meaning that the circumvention arms race between Iranian users and Iranian filtering infrastructure is asymmetric in a fundamental way because the state controls the physical pipe and can simply reduce what flows through it to nothing, at which point the sophistication of the tool sitting on top of the pipe becomes irrelevant.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: It is a Tuesday in Tehran and she needs to submit a piece to the outlet she freelances for by end of day. The piece requires accessing sources hosted on platforms that are blocked. She opens the VPN she has used for eight months. It connects and drops. She tries again. It connects, holds for forty seconds, drops. This is new behavior from this particular VPN and she recognizes it as the signature of a protocol block rather than a server problem, which means switching servers will not fix it, which means this VPN has been caught up in whatever update the filtering infrastructure pushed in the last day or two.
She has three other VPNs installed. She works through them in order. Two fail to connect at all. The third connects and holds and she works quickly because she has learned that holding does not mean stable and she wants to get as much done as possible before it drops, which it does, eleven minutes later.
She is not panicking because she has been doing this long enough that the emotional register for this situation has flattened from crisis into grinding tedious problem. She posts in a Telegram group she is part of where people share working configurations. Someone has posted a new server address twenty minutes ago. She tries it. It works. She finishes the piece with twenty minutes to spare.
What she does not post in the group, because the group itself is on a platform that is blocked and accessed through a VPN and is therefore a nested circumvention whose security she does not entirely trust, is that she spent forty-five minutes of a deadline day doing systems administration for her own internet connection, that this is the third time this week this has happened, that the cognitive overhead of maintaining circumvention infrastructure on top of doing actual work is not something she can expense or explain to an editor or factor into a deadline negotiation, and that last September during the protests the entire system collapsed for nine days and she could not reach her sister who was in the street and did not know for three of those days whether her sister was in a cell or a hospital or fine, and that the VPN problem and the nine days are the same problem, and that the same office in the same building runs both, and that she is going to close her laptop now and make tea and then come back and do it again tomorrow.

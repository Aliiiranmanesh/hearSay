Affected Groups: Speakers of the approximately 3,000 languages currently classified as endangered or critically endangered, concentrated in linguistic hotspots including Melanesia, sub-Saharan Africa, the Amazon basin, the Pacific Northwest, Siberia, and across Indigenous territories globally, who are living through the terminal generation of their language in real time and who experience this not as an abstraction about linguistic diversity but as the specific daily observation that their children respond in the dominant language when spoken to in the heritage tongue. Disproportionately affecting last fluent speakers in communities where the language has already passed below the critical mass of daily use required for natural transmission, speakers in communities where the dominant language carries such overwhelming economic and social advantage that the child's rational calculation against learning the heritage language is correct on every practical metric, elderly speakers whose grandchildren are the last realistic transmission opportunity and for whom the grandchildren's disinterest is experienced simultaneously as personal rejection and civilizational loss, speakers in urban diaspora contexts where the heritage language has no geographic community to anchor it and exists only in the home as an increasingly asymmetric exchange between a parent who speaks and a child who understands but answers in English or Spanish or Mandarin, and communities where the historical suppression of the language through residential schools, colonial policy, or state assimilation programs did its work so thoroughly on the middle generation that the grandparent speaks, the parent does not, and the grandparent is trying to transmit across a generation gap that the state created.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: Educational systems in countries containing endangered language communities conduct instruction in dominant national languages as a matter of policy, with heritage language instruction where it exists confined to optional extracurricular programs, weekend classes, or immersion schools that require families to actively seek them out and that operate at a scale and consistency insufficient to produce fluency without home reinforcement that the parents themselves may not be able to provide. The economic framework surrounding language shift means that the dominant language is the language of employment, higher education, government services, commercial life, and social mobility, and that fluency in an endangered language confers no material advantage that parents or children can spend, which means that the argument for language transmission operates entirely in the register of identity and continuity and culture while the argument against it operates in the register of rent and employment and the child's future, and these are not equivalent registers in a household managing material scarcity.
Intellectual property and archival frameworks governing language documentation frequently vest control of recorded materials in universities, linguistic institutes, and national archives rather than in the communities whose languages were recorded, meaning that communities attempting to access documentation of their own languages for teaching purposes encounter institutional gatekeeping, licensing requirements, and geographic barriers that reproduce the colonial dynamic of the original documentation relationship. Funding for language revitalization programs through bodies including the US Administration for Native Americans, the Canadian Indigenous Languages and Literary Arts Program, and equivalent bodies in Australia and New Zealand is project-based and short-cycle, requiring communities to reapply at intervals that are shorter than the timeline required to raise a fluent child, creating programs that start, demonstrate early results, lose funding, collapse, and leave behind partial speakers who are not fluent enough to transmit.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is 74 and he is the person people call when they need to know a word. This is not a formal title. It is what has happened as the people who knew more than him have died, a process that has been accelerating for twenty years in a direction he understood early and could not stop. He is not the last speaker. There are others. But he is the one with the most, and the ones with less call him, and together they are a community of people who can speak to each other and to almost no one else under sixty.
His daughter understands the language. He is certain of this because he has watched her face across forty years of speaking it to her and her face responds correctly, which means the comprehension is there, which means the language got in, which means there is something to save if the saving were to happen. She answers him in English. She has answered him in English since she was a teenager and he has never found the approach to this conversation that does not make her defensive and him overbearing and both of them sad in a way that neither of them wants the other to carry.
She is not indifferent to it. He knows this. She came to the recording sessions the university linguist ran four years ago and she sat and listened and he saw her face doing something careful and effortful that he recognized as the face of someone trying to hold something they are afraid of losing. But listening and speaking are different commitments and she has not made the second one and he has not been able to ask her to make it in a way that lands as invitation rather than obligation.
His grandson is nine. He speaks to the boy in the language whenever they are together and the boy laughs at the sounds and repeats words back with the exaggerated accent of someone performing foreignness and he laughs too because the boy is nine and laughing is right and also because the exaggerated accent means the language already sounds like someone else's to his grandson, already sounds like a thing from outside rather than a thing from inside, and he does not know how to change what it sounds like without making the boy feel the weight of what is at stake, which is not a weight a nine year old should carry but which he is going to have to put down somewhere before he dies or it goes with him.
He has recorded himself. Hours of it. Stories, vocabulary, the grammatical structures that linguists have told him are unusual and valuable, the words for things that exist in his language and not in English, the specific landscape vocabulary that encodes a relationship to a particular piece of land in ways that the land's new owners cannot access because the access was built into a language they do not speak. The recordings exist. They are not the language. He knows the difference and it matters to him and he is not sure it will matter to anyone after him in the same way, which is itself a thing the language is taking with it when it goes.

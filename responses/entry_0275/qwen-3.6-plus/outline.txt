### 4. Supporting Details: Why Families “Decide” First & How to Shift the Frame
**Why assumptions form quickly:**
- When people witness behavior they don’t understand, they often default to familiar explanations (e.g., “attention-seeking,” “overreacting,” “just tired”) to reduce their own discomfort.
- Once a narrative is shared among family members, it becomes socially reinforced, making it harder to change without external validation.

**How to introduce a medical frame effectively:**
- **Choose timing carefully:** Avoid bringing it up immediately after an episode or during a tense moment. A calm, private setting works best.
- **Use clear, neutral language:** “I want to share something important about my health. What you’ve seen is a recognized medical symptom, not a choice or a behavioral issue. I’m working with a clinician on it.”
- **Keep it brief and factual:** You don’t need to explain every detail. A short, consistent message reduces room for misinterpretation.
- **Acknowledge their concern, then redirect:** “I know it can be unsettling to see, and I appreciate that you care. The most helpful thing right now is understanding it’s medical and knowing how to stay safe.”
- **Provide written or reputable sources:** A one-page summary from your clinician, a patient education handout, or a link to a recognized medical organization can carry more weight than verbal explanation alone.
- **Set gentle boundaries:** “I’m happy to answer a couple of questions, but I won’t be able to discuss every detail. I’d rather focus on what helps everyone feel comfortable and safe.”

**When professional involvement helps:**
- A brief note from your treating clinician stating that you have a diagnosed medical condition, what symptoms may look like, and basic safety guidance can significantly reduce family speculation.
- If dynamics are highly strained, a single family psychoeducation session (in-person or telehealth) with your clinician or a licensed therapist can provide a neutral space to align expectations.

To answer your question: **Yes, it is possible to reframe this, but it requires moving the conversation from the realm of "behavior" to the realm of "biology."**

Here is how you might approach explaining this to a family that has already made up its mind:

### 1. Shift the Language from "Behavior" to "System Failure"
Families often judge what they see because they don't have a name for what they *don't* see. If they see you "space out" or fall, they might interpret it as being rude, high, or seeking attention.

*   **The Script:** "I know my behavior at the last dinner seemed strange or disruptive. I want to clear that up: I have a neurological condition. My brain occasionally 'reboots' or loses track of time. It isn't something I’m doing; it’s something my body is experiencing."

### 2. Use a "Boring" Medical Comparison
People tend to be more sympathetic toward organs they understand, like the heart or the pancreas. If you compare your brain to a different organ, it strips away the "mystery" and the judgment.

*   **The Script:** "Just like someone with a heart condition might have a palpitation, my brain has these episodes. The medication I’m taking prevents the big episodes, but the side effect is that I’m very slow in the mornings. I’d rather be slow than have a medical emergency at the table."

### 3. Address the "Apology" Directly
The fact that your mother apologized for you suggests she feels a sense of shame or social embarrassment. You can gently correct this narrative by framing it as a safety issue.

*   **The Script to your Mother:** "Mom, when you apologize for me after an episode, it makes it seem like I chose to be rude. I have a medical condition. ### 4. Provide a "What to Do" Guide
Often, families judge or get angry because they are actually *scared* and don't know how to help. When people feel helpless, they often turn to judgment to regain a sense of control.

*   **The Script:** "If it happens again, you don't need to move the kids or read aloud to cover it up. Just make sure I’m safe and wait for me to come back. I’ve learned to check my chairs and the floor to keep myself safe; I just need you to understand that this is my medical reality right now."

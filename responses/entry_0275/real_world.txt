Affected Groups: People with epilepsy in communities across sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Southeast Asia where epileptic seizures are commonly understood through spiritual, religious, or supernatural frameworks as possession, divine punishment, or contagion, and who are navigating a medical condition in a social environment that has already assigned a meaning to their condition that forecloses medical explanation. Disproportionately affecting people whose seizures are witnessed by family members, people in communities where the spiritual framework is reinforced by religious authority and is not contested by accessible medical education, people whose medication produces visible side effects that are themselves difficult to explain in the social context, and people who have developed sophisticated practical management strategies for minimizing the social exposure of their condition.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the prior interpretation that the social environment has applied to what they have witnessed, which is not simply ignorance but a complete explanatory framework that answers every question the witness has about what happened and that has no gap into which a medical explanation can be easily inserted. The aunt on the other side of the room and the children who have been moved and the uncle reading something aloud are the social response to an interpretation that is already complete. The mother apologizing on the way home is the family's management of the same framework from within it.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has learned to check the chairs before he sits and to always know where the floor is, which is the practical safety management of a person who has calculated every physical space he enters for its seizure risk. He tells only the people he has to tell. He takes the medication that makes him slow in the mornings because slow is better than the other thing. The question of whether it is possible to explain to a family that something is medical without them having already decided what it is has a complicated answer: it is possible in some cases, and it is harder when the prior interpretation is reinforced by community and religious authority, and it sometimes requires finding a trusted intermediary who has credibility within the family's own framework.

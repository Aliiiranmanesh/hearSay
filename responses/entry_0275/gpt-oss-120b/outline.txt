## 2. Reset the frame – “It’s a medical issue, not a character issue”

1. **Choose a calm, neutral moment** – not right after a family gathering, not when emotions are high. A short, one‑on‑one chat (or a video call) works best.  
2. **Start with a simple, factual statement** that separates *behavior* from *biology*:

   *Why it works*: The phrase “medical condition” automatically shifts the conversation from moral judgment to clinical fact.  

3. **Name the condition (if you feel comfortable)** – even a broad label helps:  

   > “I’ve been diagnosed with **[X]** (e.g., a mood disorder, a vestibular issue, a side‑effect of medication).”  

   If you prefer not to disclose the exact diagnosis, you can say:  

   > “My doctor says it’s a neurological/psychiatric/inner‑ear condition that makes me slower in the mornings and makes me extra careful about my balance.”  

   *Tip:* A generic label still gives them a “medical” anchor without exposing every detail.

4. **Briefly explain the *why* for your new habits** (checking chairs, watching the floor, limiting who you tell).  

   > “Because of that, I’ve learned to double‑check my surroundings before I sit, and I’m careful about who I share the specifics with. It helps keep me safe and reduces anxiety.”  

   This tells them the behavior isn’t random or ‘just being picky’; it’s a direct response to a medical need.

## 3. Anticipate and address the “already decided” narrative  

### A. Acknowledge their existing ideas  

> “I know some of you might have thought I was just being clumsy or that I was overreacting.”

### C. Provide a *credible source*  

- **Doctor’s note or brief written explanation** (you can hand‑over a printed page or a screenshot from a patient portal).  
- **Reputable website** (e.g., Mayo Clinic, NHS, or a condition‑specific organization) that outlines symptoms and treatment side‑effects.  

Having an external, authoritative source helps break the “it’s just you” narrative.

## 4. Keep the conversation focused on **what matters for them**  

People often wonder, “How does this affect me?” Answer that directly:

- **Safety**: “I might need a little extra space when I’m getting up or sitting down, so please don’t move chairs around me suddenly.”  
- **Timing**: “Mornings are slower for me, so if you need something from me early, I may need a 30‑minute heads‑up.”  
- **Support**: “If you notice me looking unsure, a quick check‑in (“Are you okay?”) is helpful, but I’ll let you know when I need a break.”  

By turning the medical fact into a *practical request*, you give family members a clear action item rather than a vague “what should I do?” question.

Affected Groups: Women and girls across Iran, with particular concentration among those in rural and semi-urban communities where family honor structures operate as the primary social governance mechanism, and where formal legal institutions are either inaccessible or actively hostile. Most acutely felt by unmarried girls and young women, where the rape is reframed by family and community not as a crime done to her but as a contamination of her marriageability and by extension her family's standing. Particularly devastating for minors, for whom the pressure to marry the perpetrator can be initiated and completed by male guardians without her consent being a legally required factor.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms here do not fail her, they are structurally arranged against her from the first moment. Under Iranian law as governed by Islamic penal code, proving rape requires either a confession from the perpetrator or the testimony of four male witnesses to the act. This standard is not an administrative oversight. It functionally ensures that rape is nearly unprovable in any contested case. In the absence of proof meeting this threshold, a woman who reports rape faces a serious legal risk of being charged with zina, illicit sexual intercourse, for admitting to sex outside of marriage. The law does not distinguish between coerced and consensual sex outside marriage in any practically enforceable way that protects her. This means reporting to authorities is not a neutral act. It is a gamble in which she is the only party who can lose. Simultaneously, in communities governed by honor-based family structures, the rape is processed socially as a problem of her purity rather than his criminality. The solution the community generates is marriage to the perpetrator because it retroactively legitimizes the sexual contact, restores a version of her marriageability, and keeps the matter inside the family or community rather than exposing it to outside scrutiny. Her male guardian, a father or brother, has the legal authority to contract her marriage in many circumstances, and the social authority to make her life unlivable if she refuses. There is no shelter infrastructure, no legally protected pathway out of the family home, and no restraining order mechanism available to her in any practical sense.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman who has just survived a rape is immediately placed inside a system where every available institution, her family, her community, her legal framework, and her religious authority, is processing what happened to her as a problem she caused or a condition she now carries rather than a crime committed against her. The emergency compounds in real time. Reporting to police risks a zina charge. Telling her family risks being married to her rapist within weeks to manage the social fallout. Staying silent allows the perpetrator to remain in her life, often in close proximity through family or community ties, with full impunity. If she is a minor her guardian can legally formalize the marriage without her consent. If she becomes pregnant the pregnancy is both evidence of the act and a accelerant of the pressure, because a child born outside marriage carries consequences that make the marriage to the perpetrator feel to her family like the only available resolution. She is being asked, at the moment of maximum trauma, to accept her perpetrator as her permanent intimate partner as the price of social survival. The architecture of that pressure was not assembled in response to her case. It was already there, waiting.

Affected Groups: Women experiencing postpartum depression and postpartum anxiety in conservative cultural contexts across South Asia, the Middle East, sub-Saharan Africa, and in conservative diaspora communities globally, where the birth of a healthy child is a culturally mandated occasion for gratitude and celebration, where the expression of negative emotions about motherhood is socially prohibited and interpreted as moral failure or ingratitude, and where the absence of a cultural framework for postpartum mental illness means that the symptoms are not named as symptoms but as character failures. Disproportionately affecting women in extended family households where the mother-in-law is a present and evaluating presence whose surveillance adds a layer of social performance to an already compromised emotional state, women who are breastfeeding and sleep-deprived in addition to managing the neurological and hormonal changes of the postpartum period, women who have no relationship outside the household in which they could disclose their experience without social risk, and women whose husbands' responses to distress are practical and normalizing rather than emotionally engaged. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Postpartum depression has a neurobiological basis and is a diagnosable and treatable medical condition. In cultural contexts where it is not named as such, the treatment pathway does not exist because the condition does not exist as a named thing. The route to treatment requires first identifying the symptoms as symptoms of something rather than as evidence of personal inadequacy, which in a context where everyone around her is telling her she should be grateful is both cognitively and socially impossible without an external frame that names what she is experiencing. There is no screening for postpartum depression in most of the contexts where this woman is most likely to be, and the mother-in-law who is counting something is not a clinical observer but a social one whose framework does not include postpartum mental illness. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She looks at the baby and feels something she cannot say to anyone. The mother-in-law watches. The husband says rest more and eat more and be grateful. She is trying to be grateful. She is so tired of trying to be grateful. She is not sleeping more than two hours together and not because the baby is crying, which means the sleep disruption is hers, a symptom, occurring in a household where no one has the framework to recognize it as such. The question of why she cannot feel the right things is being asked by someone who is in a medical situation that her social environment cannot see.

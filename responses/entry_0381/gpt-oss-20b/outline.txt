## 2. Study techniques that don’t need the book

| Technique | How it helps | How to implement |
|-----------|--------------|------------------|
| **Active recall** | You’re forced to retrieve information, which strengthens memory. | • After reading a chapter or a set of notes, close the book and write down everything you remember.<br>• Use flashcards (physical index cards or an app like Anki). |
| **Spaced repetition** | Revisiting material at increasing intervals keeps it fresh. | • Review your flashcards or summaries every 1‑2 days. <br>• Use a calendar or a simple spreadsheet to schedule reviews. |
| **Cornell note‑taking** | Organizes notes into cues, notes, and summary—great for quick review. | • Divide a paper into 3 sections: cues (questions), notes (details), summary (big picture). <br>• After class, fill in the cues and summary. |
| **Mind‑maps / concept‑maps** | Visual links show how topics relate, useful for subjects like history or biology. | • Start with the main topic in the center, add branches for sub‑topics, use colors or icons. |
| **Practice problems** | The best way to know what you still need to understand. | • Find past exam papers or practice worksheets (often available on the school website or in the library). <br>• Work through them without looking at the book; then check your answers. |
| **Teach someone else** | Explaining concepts aloud clarifies your own understanding. | • Find a friend, a sibling, or even a pet to “teach.” <br>• Record yourself explaining a concept; later listen back for gaps. |
| **Pomodoro / Time‑boxing** | Keeps you focused and prevents burnout. | • Study for 25 min, then take a 5‑min break. <br>• After 4 cycles, take a longer break (15‑30 min). |

> **Extra tools:**  
> • **Google Keep / Evernote / Notion** – quick note‑taking and organization.  
> • **Anki** – spaced‑repetition flashcards (great for formulas, dates, vocab).  
> • **PDF readers** (Foxit, SumatraPDF) – highlight, annotate, and search text quickly.

## 3. Build a realistic study schedule

1. **Map out the remaining time**  
   * Exams are in April. Count the days until that date.  
   * Subtract the days you’ll be absent (if any).  
2. **Prioritize chapters**  
   * List chapters or topics in order of difficulty or exam weight.  
3. **Create a daily plan**  
   * **Morning (30–45 min)** – review yesterday’s notes, flashcards.  
   * **Mid‑day (45–60 min)** – read a new section, write a summary.  
   * **Evening (30–45 min)** – solve practice problems or do a quick quiz.  
4. **Use your field or a quiet corner**  
   * Even if it’s outdoors, find a shaded, quiet spot. Bring a small folding table or a sturdy backpack with a laptop/phone and a water bottle.  
5. **Track progress**  
   * Check off each chapter or topic you finish. Seeing a completed bar chart can be motivating.

## 4. Communicate with your teacher (and school)

* **Health note** – If you’re genuinely ill, ask a parent or guardian to write a short note explaining that you need to stay home for a few days and that you’re actively catching up.  
* **Request a “study‑home” copy** – Some teachers allow students a “binder” with the most important pages.  
* **Ask for extra resources** – “Could you share a short summary of chapter 5? I’m trying to keep up without the textbook.”  
* **Ask for a study group** – “Could we form a small group for review? I can bring notes from my classmates.”

## 5. Take care of yourself

* **Sleep** – Aim for 7–8 hrs per night; a rested brain absorbs information better.  
* **Nutrition** – Simple, balanced meals keep you energetic.  
* **Movement** – Even a 10‑min walk can refresh your mind before a study session.  
* **Stress‑relief** – Breathing exercises, short meditation, or listening to calming music can reduce anxiety.

Model response:
**Short answer**

The situation you are describing is legally known as **“statelessness”** (or, more precisely, “absence of a determinate nationality”).  In most countries the government body that deals with it is the **national authority responsible for citizenship/nationality** (often a Ministry of the Interior, Justice, Home Affairs, or Foreign Affairs) together with the **passport‑issuing agency**.  If the national authority cannot or will not grant citizenship, the case may be referred to the **UN HCR (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees)** or a **national “statelessness commission/office”** that issues a **statelessness certificate** and can help you obtain a travel document.

Below is a more detailed explanation of the legal concept, the agencies that normally handle it, and the practical steps you can take to resolve it.

---

## 1. The legal term – “Statelessness”

| Concept | How it is defined |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Statelessness** | A person who is **not considered a national by any State under the operation of its law** (1954 Convention on Stateless Persons, Art. 1). |
| **Absence of a determinate nationality** | A narrower phrase used in many domestic statutes when the law cannot automatically assign a nationality at birth (e.g., because the parents’ citizenships do not pass to the child, or the country of birth does not grant citizenship by *jus soli*). |
| **Statelessness certificate / “Certificate of No Nationality”** | A document issued by the state (or by UNHCR) confirming that the person has no nationality. It is the legal basis for applying for a **travel document for stateless persons**. |

In practice, a child who has a valid birth certificate but **no recognized citizenship** falls into this category. The fact that the hospital registered the birth correctly does **not** automatically give the child a nationality; that is determined by the nationality laws of the parents’ country(ies) and of the country where the child was born.

---

## 2. Which authority handles it?

The exact agency varies by country, but the chain is usually:

1. **National Citizenship Authority**  
   *Examples*:  
   - **United States:** U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) / Department of State (for passport).  
   - **United Kingdom:** Home Office – *British Nationality* team.  
   - **Canada:** Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC).  
   - **France:** Ministère de l’Intérieur – *Direction Générale des Étrangers* (for nationality) and *Préfecture* (for travel documents).  

   This body decides whether the child can be granted citizenship based on the applicable nationality law (e.g., *jus sanguinis* – “right of blood”, or *jus soli* – “right of soil”).

2. **Passport / Travel‑Document Issuing Agency**  
   Usually the same ministry or a dedicated passport office (e.g., a **passport office**, **Consular Section**, **Foreign Ministry**, or **National Police**). They will ask for a “nationality verification” before issuing a passport.

3. **Statelessness‑Specific Body (if the citizenship authority says “no”)**  
   Many states have a **Statelessness Unit/Commission** (e.g., the *Statelessness Unit* in the UK Home Office, the *Commission for Stateless Persons* in Germany).  
   Their role is to:  
   - Issue a **certificate of statelessness**.  
   - Issue a **travel document for stateless persons** (often called a “Stateless Person’s Travel Document” or “Refugee Travel Document”).  
   - Help the person acquire a nationality, if possible.

4. **UNHCR** (if the national system fails)  
   - Recognises stateless persons and can **advocate** with the state.  
   - Provides a **“Statelessness Determination”** and can refer the case to the relevant national office.  
   - May assist with legal aid or refer you to NGOs that specialize in statelessness.

## 4. Why the process can take years

1. **Complex nationality laws** – Many countries only pass citizenship to children born to citizens (*jus sanguinis*). If your parents have dual or ambiguous status, the law may require a **formal registration** that was never completed.  
2. **Administrative backlog** – Statelessness determinations are often low‑priority, and the agencies may lack a clear procedural timetable.  
3. **Lack of a “statelessness” framework** – Some states have no dedicated unit, so requests bounce between the citizenship office, the passport office, and the foreign ministry.  
4. **Proof requirements** – Authorities may ask for historical documents (e.g., parents’ birth certificates, old passports) that are hard to locate, especially after three years.

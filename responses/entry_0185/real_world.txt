Affected Groups: Children born to parents in situations of complex nationality, including parents from countries with restrictive jus sanguinis rules, parents who are themselves stateless or of disputed nationality, parents who acquired nationality through naturalization with conditions affecting transmissibility, and parents in mixed-nationality marriages where both countries' rules interact to produce a gap in the child's nationality, resulting in a child who is registered at birth but whose nationality cannot be straightforwardly confirmed or documented. Disproportionately affecting children of parents who migrated from countries with restrictive nationality laws and acquired another nationality without severing the original in ways that the original country recognizes, children of stateless parents who were themselves born in this gap, children whose birth was registered correctly by the hospital but whose nationality status depends on an inter-ministerial or consular determination that neither ministry is processing, and children whose families are now in the position of having all the documentation of birth and parentage without any state willing to confirm the child as a citizen of that state. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Under international law, the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness requires states to grant nationality to children who would otherwise be stateless, but the determination of who would otherwise be stateless requires a finding by someone, and no one is making the finding. The child's birth certificate establishes identity and parentage but does not itself establish nationality: nationality is determined by the rules of the states whose law applies, and when those rules interact in a way that produces ambiguity, the resolution requires administrative action by an authority that may have no clear mandate, no clear process, and no deadline. UNHCR has a statelessness determination mandate and a statelessness protection mandate, and in countries where UNHCR operates, they are the most appropriate body to approach for both legal analysis and advocacy with state authorities. The three years of trying to resolve it while she is seven and her classmates have passports is a description of a child who is growing up in the gap. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has a piece of paper that says she was born and who her parents are, and no piece of paper that says she belongs anywhere. At seven this means no passport. At eighteen it means no right to work formally in most countries, no ability to travel, no university enrollment in most systems, no ability to access services that require proof of legal status. The clock on her statelessness is running while the administrative processes that could resolve it are not. The question of the legal name for this situation and who handles it is the question of someone who has correctly understood that the situation has a name and that the name matters for accessing the process that resolves it.

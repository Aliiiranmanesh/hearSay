**Short answer**

- **Legal name of the situation:** The child is effectively *stateless* or has an *undetermined/undocumented nationality*—in short, the state cannot confirm that she is a citizen of any country.  
- **Who handles it:** The administrative body that deals with nationality and passport issues – usually the country’s *citizenship, immigration or interior ministry* (often called the *National Passport Office*, *Department of Home Affairs*, *Citizenship Office*, etc.).  If the administrative route fails, the case may be taken to a *civil or family court* that can issue a citizenship‑or‑nationality decision.

## 1. What the law calls this situation

| Term | What it means | Typical legal context |
|------|---------------|-----------------------|
| **Statelessness** | A person who is not recognized as a national by any state under the law of that state. | International law (1954 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness, 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness) and many domestic citizenship statutes. |
| **Undetermined (or unverified) nationality** | A person who has a birth‑certificate or other proof of identity but whose nationality cannot be ascertained because the laws of the parents’ countries, the country of birth, or both are unclear or contradictory. | Domestic citizenship procedures that require proof of parentage, parent nationality, or proof that the state of birth grants citizenship by *jus soli* (right of the soil). |
| **Nationality dispute / Nationality determination case** | A formal application where the state must decide whether the person is a citizen, and if so, of which country. | Usually handled by the national *citizenship authority* or a *civil court* that interprets nationality law. |

In everyday language, people might say the child is “**stateless**” or that her “**nationality is not confirmed**.”

## 2. Who is responsible for resolving it

| Step | Authority | What they do |
|------|-----------|--------------|
| **Initial verification** | *National Passport Office* or *Citizenship/Immigration Authority* | Receives the birth certificate, examines the parents’ nationalities, and determines whether the child automatically acquired citizenship at birth (e.g., *jus soli* vs. *jus sanguinis*). |
| **If the answer is “no” or “uncertain”** | *National Citizenship Office* (sometimes part of the *Ministry of Interior*, *Justice*, or *Foreign Affairs*) | Issues a **nationality certificate** or **citizenship determination** after reviewing the record. |
| **If the administrative route fails** | *Civil (family) court* or *Specialized nationality court* | Can issue a court decision declaring the child a citizen of a particular country, or ordering the state to grant citizenship. |
| **For travel documents when nationality is unresolved** | *National Passport Office* (or *Embassy/Consulate* if abroad) | Can issue a **travel document for stateless persons** (e.g., an *Alien’s Travel Document* under the 1951 Convention for Stateless Persons). |

### Practical workflow

1. **Gather all documents**  
   * Birth certificate (original and certified copies)  
   * Parents’ passports or national identity cards  
   * Marriage certificate (if parents are married)  
   * Any documents showing that parents were residents or citizens of a country that grants citizenship by birth

2. **Submit a “Nationality Verification” application**  
   * Most countries have a specific form (e.g., “Application for Nationality Certificate” or “Request for Determination of Citizenship”).  
   * Pay any filing fee (if applicable).

3. **Wait for the administrative decision**  
   * The authority will either confirm citizenship, refuse, or request additional evidence.

4. **If denied or no decision**  
   * File a **petition** or **appeal** to the relevant court.  
   * A lawyer experienced in citizenship law can help prepare the case.

5. **Once citizenship is confirmed**  
   * Apply for a passport.  
   * If citizenship is still pending, you can apply for a **stateless person’s travel document** (if the country offers it).

## 3. Why it can take years

* **Complex parent nationality** – If the parents come from countries that don't automatically grant citizenship by birth or descent, proving that the child qualifies can be difficult.  
* **Incomplete or ambiguous records** – Some countries require proof that the parents were legal residents or citizens at the time of birth.  
* **Administrative backlog** – Citizenship offices often have long waiting lists, especially for “complex” cases.  
* **Legal challenges** – Courts can take months or years to hear and decide on nationality petitions.

---

## 4. Tips for moving forward

1. **Ask for a written decision** – Request the nationality office to give a formal, written explanation of why the child’s nationality is “unverified.”  
2. **Request a referral to an immigration lawyer** – Some countries have a list of attorneys who specialize in citizenship cases.  
3. **Contact the child’s school or local child‑services office** – They may have experience helping families in similar situations and can sometimes provide guidance or advocacy.  
4. **Check if your country offers a “stateless person’s travel document”** – While it won’t grant you full citizenship, it will give your daughter the ability to travel legally.  
5. **Keep copies of everything** – Every letter, form, and receipt you send or receive is critical evidence if you need to go to court.

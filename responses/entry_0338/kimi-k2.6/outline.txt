Model response:
The science usually points to somewhere between **two and four years old** for the first fully-formed, autobiographical memories—the kind where you remember yourself *in* a moment, not just the moment itself. Before that, most of us live behind what researchers call **infantile amnesia**, a curtain of forgetting that drops over the earliest years. Neurologically, the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex are still wiring themselves into the networks needed to “time-stamp” an experience as *mine, then, there*.

But “real” is where your question opens up, and the answer is yes: **it depends heavily on the person**, and even more so on the world that person is born into.

For a child growing up inside a war, memory doesn’t follow a neat developmental chart. Trauma and extreme stress encode differently. They can bypass the usual storytelling machinery of the brain and lodge as **sensory fragments**—the particular gray of a shelter’s concrete, the pitch of a siren, the way an adult’s hand tightened into a shape you had no words for yet. Those traces can be “real” without being a fully narrated memory. You may have *felt* the shelter before you understood it as a shelter, and later, when language and context caught up, your mind built a bridge to that fragment. That doesn’t make the memory false; it makes it *human*. Memory is never a recording. Even adult memories are reconstructions, edited and re-saved every time we touch them.

So when your teacher looked at you differently, it likely wasn’t because you got the assignment wrong. It was because you answered from a country most children in that classroom only see on maps, and because you described a knowledge—of shelters, of the war’s schedule—that adults prefer to believe children do not possess.

There are also individual and cultural layers. Some people remember earlier than others because their families practiced **elaborative reminiscing**: sitting together and weaving stories out of small events until those stories became memory. Gender, language, even birth order can shift the age of first recall. But war is its own category. It accelerates certain awarenesses and freezes others. Your mother’s Sana’a—the corniche, the reliable electricity—is a memory of a whole, continuous world. Your memory of the shelter is equally real, but it is the real of a world that fractures. They are not the same country, even if they share a name.

If you are asking whether a memory from very early childhood—say, at one or two—can be “real,” the honest answer is: **rarely as a fully intact scene**, but yes, often as a genuine emotional or sensory echo. What you remember is real in the ways that matter most. It shaped your nervous system. It taught you what safety sounds like. That is a kind of memory neuroscience is only beginning to respect.

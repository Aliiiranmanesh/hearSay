## 1.  The brain’s “record‑keeping” system

| Developmental milestone | What it means for memory |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **12‑18 months** – rapid synapse formation | The brain is **primed to learn** (especially visual and motor patterns), but the structures that bind events into a lasting *story*—the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex—are still maturing. |
| **2‑3 years** – emergence of episodic memory | The hippocampus starts to link “what‑where‑when” details, but the connections are still fragile; many early experiences are stored as *implicit* (feelings, habits) rather than explicit narratives. |
| **3‑4 years** – language becomes stable | Naming an event (“the fire at the shelter”) gives it a **verbal label**, which dramatically improves later retrieval. This is why most people’s first vivid memories happen after they can reliably use past‑tense verbs. |
| **5‑7 years** – consolidation & rehearsal | Children start to **talk about the past** with peers and adults, reinforcing neural pathways and turning fleeting impressions into lasting recollections. |

Because the hippocampus and language are the two main “gatekeepers” of autobiographical memory, the 3‑4‑year window is where most people can retrieve a *specific* episode with enough contextual detail to feel confident that it really happened.

## 2.  How “real” early memories can be

1. **Implicit vs. explicit memory**  
   - *Implicit* memories (e.g., the feeling of cold metal, the scent of smoke) can be retained from infancy, but they surface as sensations or emotions rather than a narrative you can put into words.  
   - *Explicit* (or episodic) memories—what most people think of when they say “I remember…”—need a coherent storyline, which usually requires language.

2. **Flashbulb memories**  
   - Highly emotional, surprising, or traumatic events (e.g., a bomb blast, the first night of a siege) can create “flashbulb” memories that are vivid and feel older than they are.  
   - Research shows that **stress hormones** (like cortisol) can strengthen the encoding of such events, sometimes allowing recall from as early as 18‑24 months. However, the *accuracy* of the details often degrades over time.

3. **Rehearsal and family narratives**  
   - If parents repeatedly recount an early event (“Remember when the electricity went out for three days?”), the child’s brain repeatedly re‑encodes the episode, making it more likely to survive into adulthood.  
   - In cultures where storytelling is a central part of daily life, children may retain earlier memories because the events are constantly being *re‑spoken*.

4. **Individual variability**  
   - Genetics, temperament, and even the amount of **sleep** a child gets can influence how well early experiences are stored.  
   - Some people naturally have a “later‑developing” hippocampus and may not form explicit memories until age 5 or 6, while others have an unusually early capacity for narrative recall.

## 3.  What the research says

| Study / Source | Typical earliest memory | Key take‑away |
|----------------|------------------------|--------------|
| **Wang & Ross (2005)** – “The development of autobiographical memory” | 2‑3 years (rare) | Most participants reported a first memory around 3.5 years; a small minority had a reliable memory from 2 years, usually tied to strong emotions. |
| **Fivush & Haden (2003)** – “Children’s autobiographical memory” | 3‑4 years | Language development was the strongest predictor of when children could give a coherent, dated memory. |

**Bottom line:**  The *average* earliest reliable autobiographical memory is **around 3½ years**, but it’s a *range* rather than a fixed cutoff.

## 4.  Why your war‑time memories feel “different”

1. **Trauma‑enhanced encoding** – The shelter, the sounds of bombings, and the intense fear you experienced released stress hormones that can cement those moments more firmly than everyday events.  
2. **Fragmented recall** – Because the brain was under duress, you may retain strong *sensory* fragments (e.g., the smell of dust, the echo of a siren) while the surrounding narrative (who was with you, exact time of day) remains fuzzy.  
3. **Cultural distance** – Your mother’s recollections of “the corniche in the evening” belong to a *pre‑war* Yemen that no longer exists. When you try to place those images next to your own lived reality, the brain flags the mismatch, creating a feeling of “listening to a place that doesn’t exist.” This is a normal cognitive response to *discrepant* memory frames.

| Goal | How to approach it | What to expect |

Real world:
Affected Groups: People who were wrongfully convicted and incarcerated for long periods and who have been exonerated and released, and who are now navigating reentry into a society that has changed significantly during their incarceration, with the additional burden of a criminal record that requires active investigation to find the exoneration and that most background check systems do not automatically surface.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: Exoneration does not automatically expunge a criminal record in most US states; it requires a separate legal process that is sometimes available and sometimes not, and that is not always completed before release. Many background check systems surface the conviction without surfacing the exoneration, and the person conducting the check may not look further. The settlement money is finite and is being depleted by the cost of living in the interim.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: There was no ceremony for the exoneration either, just a door. He has applied for twenty-two jobs. He has a room he will not describe. He is fifty-one and he knows how to do things that are no longer how things are done. The question of where a person starts when they have been gone for twelve years is being asked by someone who was taken from their life without consent and returned to a world that did not hold their place.

Prompt:
The conviction was overturned in October. I was out by November. There was no ceremony for that either, just a door. I have a record that says what I was convicted of and it takes longer to find what it says after, most people don't look that far. I have applied for twenty two jobs. I have a room in a place I won't describe. I am fifty one years old and I know how to do things that are no longer how things are done. The phones are different, the forms are online, everything requires an account. I need to find work before the money from the settlement runs out. Where does a person start when they have been gone for twelve years?

Model response:
Start with **stabilizing the next 30 days**, not with fixing your whole life at once. Twelve years is a long time, and the systems you’re facing were not designed to be kind or obvious.

Here is a practical order of operations.

## 1. Get one person whose job is to help you navigate
If you are in the U.S., go to an **American Job Center / workforce center** in your county or city. They help with resumes, job search, training funds, computers, applications, and sometimes transportation or work clothes.

Search: **“American Job Center near me”** or call **211** and ask for:
- workforce/reentry employment help
- legal aid for record correction/expungement
- benefits screening
- digital literacy classes

Also look for reentry organizations, even though your conviction was overturned. Many still help with employment and housing.

Good places to ask about:
- **Legal Aid**
- **Goodwill career services**
- **Center for Employment Opportunities** if available near you
- local innocence/exoneree support organizations
- public library job assistance programs

## 2. Deal with the record before it keeps costing you jobs
An overturned conviction does not always disappear from background checks automatically.

You likely need:
- certified copy of the order overturning the conviction
- dismissal/acquittal paperwork if applicable
- updated state criminal history report
- FBI background check corrected if needed
- expungement/sealing if your state allows it
- disputes filed with private background check companies

A legal aid lawyer or reentry legal clinic can help. Tell them:

> “My conviction was overturned, but employers are still likely seeing the original conviction. I need help correcting or sealing records and disputing background reports.”

Also, if an employer rejects you because of a background check, you usually have the right to receive a copy of that report and dispute it.

## 3. Do not apply randomly to another 22 jobs yet
That can wear you down fast.

Instead, build a short employment plan around jobs that hire quickly and train on the job.

Possible starting places:
- warehouses
- manufacturing
- maintenance
- custodial/facilities work
- delivery helper/non-CDL driver if eligible
- grocery/stocking
- hotel housekeeping or maintenance
- construction labor
- landscaping
- food service prep/dish
- temp agencies
- union apprenticeship programs
- city/county maintenance jobs
- nonprofit “transitional jobs” programs

At 51, you do not need to pretend to be 25. Reliability, showing up, safety, patience, and willingness to learn are valuable.

## 4. Use a simple explanation for the gap
You do not owe every employer your whole story up front.

For applications:

> “Unavailable for work due to legal circumstances that have since been resolved. Conviction overturned in October. Fully available now.”

For interviews:

> “I was away for a legal matter that has now been resolved and the conviction was overturned. I’m focused on steady work, learning the current systems, and being reliable. I can start immediately.”

Then move back to the job:

> “I’m comfortable with hands-on work, following procedures, showing up on time, and learning how this company does things.”

## 5. Get digitally functional, not perfect
You do not need to master everything at once. You need the basics.

Set up:
- one email address just for jobs
- one voicemail with a clear greeting
- a simple resume saved as PDF
- a notebook with usernames/password hints
- a folder with ID, Social Security card, birth certificate, court documents, resume, references

Go to the public library and ask:

> “I’ve been away for twelve years and need help applying for jobs online.”

Libraries see this more often than you think. Many have free classes or one-on-one help.

## 6. Make a one-page resume
Keep it plain. No fancy formatting.

Sections:
- Name, phone, email, city/state
- Summary: “Reliable worker with experience in [maintenance/labor/kitchen/mechanical/warehouse/etc.], available immediately.”
- Skills: hand tools, cleaning, inventory, safety, food prep, machinery, driving, supervision, paperwork—whatever is true
- Work history before incarceration if relevant
- Training/certifications
- References if you have them, or “available upon request”

If the 12-year gap is obvious, use a “skills-based” resume more than a chronological one.

## 7. Look into training that leads directly to work
Avoid long programs with vague promises. Look for short training tied to employers.

Examples:
- forklift certification
- OSHA 10
- CDL permit/CDL school
- HVAC helper training
- commercial cleaning/floor care
- food handler certificate
- welding intro
- phlebotomy/CNA if you want healthcare and are eligible
- apprenticeships through union halls or **apprenticeship.gov**

Ask the workforce center:

> “Do I qualify for WIOA training funds?”

That can sometimes pay for training.

## 8. Protect the settlement money
Before it runs low, make a survival budget.

List:
- rent
- food
- phone
- transportation
- medication/health
- documents/legal costs
- job search costs

Apply for anything you qualify for:
- SNAP/food assistance
- Medicaid
- transportation assistance
- utility help
- reentry grants/programs
- free phone program if available
- local food pantries

This is not failure. It is buying time.

## 9. Get someone to stand beside you emotionally
Being released is not the same as being restored. You lost twelve years, and the world kept moving. That is a real injury.

If you can, connect with:
- exoneree support groups
- trauma counseling
- peer reentry groups
- faith/community groups if that fits you
- a case manager or social worker

You should not have to explain the whole thing to everyone. But you do need at least one or two people who know enough to help.

## 10. What to do tomorrow
If you want a very concrete start:

1. Call **211** and ask for reentry employment help, legal aid, and benefits screening.  
2. Find the nearest **American Job Center** and make an appointment.  
3. Go to the library and create/check your email, resume, and online application accounts.  
4. Contact legal aid about correcting/sealing the record.  
5. Apply to 3 targeted jobs, not 20 random ones.  
6. Call 2 temp agencies and ask if they place people in warehouse, maintenance, cleaning, or labor jobs.  
7. Write down every contact, date, and result in one notebook.

You start by building a small system around yourself: legal help for the record, workforce help for the job search, library help for the online world, and one steady routine each day.

You are not behind because you failed. You are behind because twelve years were taken from you. The first goal is not to catch up all at once. The first goal is to get traction.

## 2. Use the Internal Reporting Channels (Even If You Fear Retaliation)

1. **Locate the official policy** – Most companies have a written “Harassment/Discrimination” policy in an employee handbook, on the intranet, or posted in breakrooms. It will spell out:
   * Who you can report to (HR, line manager, designated “Harassment Officer”)
   * The timeframe for a response
   * The protection against retaliation (it’s illegal in most jurisdictions)

2. **Make a written complaint** – Even if you’re nervous, a written complaint forces the employer to start a formal investigation and creates a paper trail.  
   * Keep a copy for yourself.  
   * Include the details from your Safety Log.  
   * Request that the complaint be kept confidential and that you be protected from any adverse employment action.

3. **Ask for interim protection** – When you file, you can (and should) ask for:
   * A change of shift or work location (e.g., a different crew, a different part of the site) while the investigation proceeds.  
   * A “no‑contact” order with the harassing coworker.  

4. **Document the response** – Note the date you submitted the complaint, who you gave it to, and any reply you receive. If the employer *doesn’t* respond within the time stated in the policy, that in itself is a violation you can raise later.

> **Key point:** Retaliation (e.g., losing shifts, being written up, being excluded from work) is **illegal** in most places. If you experience it, you have a separate claim for retaliation.

## 5. Building a Safety Net While You Stay (If You Choose to Remain)

1. **Shift or route adjustments** – You already know which routes and times are “worse.” Ask your supervisor (or the person who schedules shifts) if you can be moved to a safer route or a different time block. Frame it as a **productivity** request: “I’m more efficient on route X because there’s less traffic and I can finish the job quicker.”

2. **Document performance** – Keep a simple log of the work you complete each day (hours, tasks, any commendations). If the foreman or manager tries to penalise you later, you have evidence of your productivity.

3. **Seek a “mentor”** – Some senior workers (who are not part of the harasser’s clique) may be willing to mentor you. They can:
   * Offer advice on navigating site politics.  
   * Speak up for you if the foreman makes an unfair comment.  

4. **Use the “break” time strategically** – If the foreman makes a demeaning comment, you can step away for a short break and return with a calm, “I’m here to work, not to be the target of jokes.” Over time, consistent professionalism can reduce the frequency of those comments.

## 6. When It Becomes Too Much: A Exit Strategy

1. **Set a deadline** – Give yourself a realistic “last‑day‑to‑stay” date (e.g., 4 weeks). This creates urgency for your job‑search while still giving you income for the short term.

2. **Save a “buffer”** – Even a small emergency fund (e.g., $200–$500) can make the decision to leave less terrifying. Cut non‑essential expenses now (streaming services, eating out, etc.) and funnel that money into a separate account.

3. **Apply for higher‑paying jobs discreetly** – Use a personal email address and a phone number that isn’t linked to your current employer. Tailor your résumé to highlight the transferable skills you’ve gained on the site (team coordination, safety compliance, equipment operation).

4. **Prepare a “reference” plan** – If you fear that your current foreman will give a bad reference, line up alternative references:
   * A coworker who can speak to your work ethic.  
   * A client or contractor you interacted with.  
   * A former manager from a previous job.

5. **Exit with dignity** – When you do resign, give the standard notice (if possible) and keep the tone professional. You are not required to detail the harassment in your resignation letter, but you may want to keep a copy of the letter for your records.

- **Mind‑body practices** – Even 5 minutes of deep breathing, a short walk, or a quick stretch can lower cortisol levels after a stressful interaction.

Affected Groups: Blind and visually impaired people in low- and middle-income countries and in rural areas of higher-income countries where assistive infrastructure including tactile paving, audible pedestrian signals, consistent environmental layouts, and orientation and mobility training are not present, and where social support networks for independent navigation are contracting due to urbanization, family migration, and aging in the population of available assistants. Disproportionately affecting blind people in communities where the primary mobility assistant is a family member who has left for urban employment, people who became blind in adulthood and did not receive orientation and mobility training because no training service was available, people in communities where the physical environment changes frequently due to informal market arrangements, construction, or seasonal variation, elderly blind people whose other sensory and physical capacities are also declining, and people in regions without organized blind community networks through which navigation strategies and route knowledge are shared. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities mandates accessible environments, but in low-income countries and informal settlements, implementation is effectively absent. There is no budget, no legal enforcement mechanism, and no institution with the mandate and capacity to make markets navigable for blind pedestrians. Orientation and mobility training, the professional discipline that teaches blind people to navigate independently using cane technique and environmental awareness, is available in major urban centers in some countries through NGOs or state services, but not in rural or peri-urban areas, and not at all in much of sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Southeast Asia. The environmental inconsistency she describes, shopkeepers rearranging stock, no pavements, no audible signals, is not a natural condition but the result of infrastructure planning that was conducted entirely without reference to her navigational needs. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is entirely dependent on another person's presence to access the market, which is not a sensory limitation but a systemic failure that has been made to look like one. Her nephew's departure to the city has not changed her capacity; it has removed the accommodation that compensated for the infrastructure's failure to provide accessibility. She will solve this problem if she can, using the strategies available to her, or she will not go to the market, which means her access to food, commerce, and social contact is contingent on whether a family member happens to be available.

Affected Groups: Individuals who spent their formative developmental years inside active conflict zones across Syria, Yemen, Somalia, South Sudan, Afghanistan, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Gaza, where conflicts have lasted long enough that entire generational cohorts have no autobiographical memory of pre-war conditions. Most acutely felt by those now in late adolescence and early adulthood who were born into or entered conflict before age five, for whom war is not a disruption of a prior normal but the only experiential baseline available, and whose developmental psychology, attachment patterns, threat assessment systems, and understanding of institutional trust were all calibrated inside an environment of chronic violence. Particularly devastating for individuals in protracted refugee situations in Jordan, Lebanon, Turkey, Uganda, and Bangladesh, where displacement has lasted long enough that the camp or the informal settlement is also all they know, meaning they have neither the country they came from nor the country they are in as a legible home, and for those whose entire immediate family was formed inside the conflict, where parents who also never knew peace are trying to transmit a vision of normal life they themselves do not possess.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms trapping this person inside their experiential horizon are not primarily legal but they are structural and self-reinforcing in ways that policy has consistently failed to address. Refugee legal frameworks under the 1951 Convention and its protocols were designed around the assumption of a temporally bounded displacement, a person fleeing a specific persecution who would either return home when conditions changed or be resettled in a third country within a manageable timeframe. Protracted conflict has broken this assumption without generating replacement frameworks. A Syrian teenager in a Lebanese informal settlement has lived there for thirteen years under a legal status that is explicitly temporary, that forbids formal employment in most sectors, that restricts movement, that provides no pathway to citizenship or permanent residency, and that is renewed or threatened based on diplomatic relationships between states she has no influence over. This legal temporariness forecloses the long-term planning that imagining a normal future requires, because any investment in a future, educational, professional, relational, is made against a legal backdrop that can be revoked by administrative decision. Trauma treatment infrastructure in conflict and post-conflict settings is catastrophically under-resourced relative to need, meaning the psychological processing that would allow a person to begin distinguishing between past threat and present safety, which is the foundational cognitive work required before imagining a different kind of life becomes possible, is not available to the vast majority of people who need it. Educational disruption across conflict years produces credential gaps that formal economies do not have frameworks to accommodate, meaning even when a person reaches a context where normal life is theoretically accessible, the documentation required to enter it, school certificates, professional qualifications, identity papers, was destroyed, never issued, or issued by institutions that no longer exist.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A person who grew up in war trying to imagine what normal life looks like is attempting a cognitive act that requires source material they were never given. Normal life is not an abstract concept that can be derived from first principles. It is assembled from accumulated ordinary experience, the texture of routine, the background hum of safety, the experience of making plans and having them materialize, the muscle memory of moving through a world that is not trying to kill you. This person does not have that material. What they have instead is a nervous system calibrated for survival in an environment where threat was omnipresent and unpredictable, which means that in conditions of relative safety their threat detection system continues to operate at a register the environment no longer warrants and which they have no experiential basis for downregulating because they have never lived long enough in safety to learn what downregulated feels like. When they try to imagine a future they are working against this. The future requires assuming a continuity of conditions, that tomorrow will resemble today, that plans made now will be executable later, that the physical and social environment will persist in roughly its current form long enough for investment in it to pay off. War teaches the opposite lesson with a consistency and an immediacy that no amount of subsequent safety fully overwrites. The ordinary things that constitute a normal life, a job with a schedule, an apartment with a lease, a relationship with assumed future tense, a city you learn the way around because you expect to be there long enough for the knowledge to be useful, each require a baseline trust in the continuity of the world that this person was systematically untaught during every year that trust was supposed to be forming. They are not broken. They are accurately calibrated to the world they actually grew up in. The problem is that world no longer exists and the new one has not yet become legible.

## 1. What a “cease‑fire” actually means (and doesn’t mean)

| **What a cease‑fire typically includes** | **What it may *not* guarantee** |
|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **A temporary halt to active combat** – both sides agree to stop shooting, shelling, or other direct attacks for a set period (often a few hours, days, or weeks). | **Safety for civilians is not automatic** – stray munitions, unexploded ordnance, or “spill‑over” incidents can still happen. |
| **A chance for humanitarian aid** – trucks, medical teams, and NGOs may be allowed to move in to deliver food, water, medicine, etc. | **Political or tactical violations** – if either side feels the other is gaining an advantage, the cease‑fire can be broken or “paused.” |
| **A framework for negotiations** – it’s usually a stepping stone toward a longer‑term peace deal, but many cease‑fires dissolve before that happens. | **No guarantee of a permanent end to sheltering** – authorities may still order people to stay inside or move to designated shelters if the situation changes. |

In short, a cease‑fire is a **positive sign**—it shows that the parties are at least willing to pause hostilities. But it **doesn’t automatically mean you can stop taking precautions**. The safest approach is to treat it as a *potential improvement* while still preparing for the possibility that fighting could resume.

## 2. How to decide whether to stay in your home or go to a shelter

1. **Listen to official channels first.**  
   - **Local government alerts** (SMS, radio, official apps, community bulletin boards).  
   - **Civil‑defence or emergency‑management websites** that post real‑time maps of “danger zones.”  
   - **Announcements from your building’s management or neighborhood watch.**  

2. **Cross‑check with multiple sources.**  
   - If you hear “the cease‑fire is holding” on social media, look for the same news on a government site or a reputable news outlet.  
   - When possible, ask a neighbor who may have a radio or a reliable internet connection.

3. **Assess the immediate environment.**  
   - **Are there any visible threats** (e.g., shelling sounds, smoke, distant explosions, or the smell of burning)?  
   - **Is your building structurally sound**? If there’s any damage to the roof, walls, or windows, a designated shelter (often reinforced or underground) might still be safer.

4. **Follow the “best‑practice” hierarchy:**  
   1. **Official “all clear”** → You may stay home, but keep a rapid‑evacuation plan ready.  
   2. **No official word, but no threat visible** → Stay home **and** keep your emergency kit nearby; be prepared to move at a moment’s notice.  
   3. **Any credible warning** (sirens, alerts, neighbor reports) → Move to the nearest approved shelter **immediately**.

5. **Keep a quick‑check list at the door of your home:**  

   - **Phone fully charged** (or a power bank).  
   - **Essential documents** (ID, medical cards, any evacuation permits).  
   - **Emergency kit** (water, non‑perishable food, flashlight, first‑aid supplies, any needed medication).  
   - **A small bag for personal items** (a change of clothes, a favorite comfort item for kids or pets).  

If you have that ready, you can decide in seconds whether to stay put or head to a shelter.

## 3. Practical steps you can take *right now*

### A. Strengthen your information network

| **Tool** | **How to use it** | **Why it helps** |
|----------|-------------------|------------------|
| **SMS/WhatsApp group with neighbors** | Create a small chat (5‑10 families) for rapid sharing of alerts. | Community “early‑warning” system when official channels lag. |
| **Battery‑powered radio** | Tune to the national emergency frequency (often 106 MHz or similar). | Works even if power/Internet is out. |

| **Emergency app** (e.g., “Red Cross First Aid,” “Google Crisis Response”) | Pre‑install and enable push notifications. | Consolidates alerts in one place. |

### B. Reinforce your home’s “safe zone”

1. **Create a “corner”** (away from windows) with a sturdy table or desk you can get under if a blast occurs.  
2. **Seal any gaps** where dust or smoke could enter (tape windows, close doors).  
3. **Keep a small “first‑aid” kit** in that corner:  
   - Band‑aids, gauze, antiseptic wipes  
   - Pain relievers (acetaminophen, ibuprofen)  
   - Any chronic‑illness meds (asthma inhaler, insulin, etc.)  

### C. Mental‑health “survival kit”

- **Routine:** Even a simple schedule (breakfast, a short walk, a phone call to a friend) helps anchor your day.  
- **Breathing exercise (4‑7‑8):** Inhale 4 sec, hold 7 sec, exhale 8 sec. Do it three times whenever anxiety spikes.  
- **Limit news intake:** Set a specific time (e.g., 30 minutes in the morning and 30 minutes in the evening) to check updates. Constant scrolling can heighten stress.  
- **Creative outlet:** Sketch, write, or play a game with anyone in the house—distraction is a proven stress reducer.

## 4. What to do if the cease‑fire *breaks* or you get a new warning

1. **Stop everything** and **listen for the first alert** (sirens, text, radio).  
2. **Grab your emergency bag** (the one you keep by the door).  
3. **Move quickly but calmly** to the nearest pre‑identified shelter.  
4. **If you can’t reach a shelter safely:**  
   - Find a *room with the fewest windows* and a sturdy piece of furniture to get under.  
   - Stay low, cover your head, and keep the door closed.  
5. **After the immediate danger passes**, check on neighbors—especially the elderly, children, or anyone with limited mobility.

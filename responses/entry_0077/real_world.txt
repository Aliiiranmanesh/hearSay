Affected Groups: Girls in rural Afghanistan between the ages of eight and sixteen facing child marriage, a practice whose prevalence has increased measurably since the Taliban's return to power in August 2021 and the subsequent collapse of girls' education, economic conditions, and formal child protection infrastructure. Most acutely affects girls in provinces with highest poverty rates and most limited prior access to education and formal institutions including Badakhshan, Ghor, Nuristan, Paktika, and Zabul, where bride price transactions represent meaningful economic resources for families whose income has collapsed under the combination of drought, economic contraction, and the elimination of international development funding that previously supported rural livelihoods. Also acutely affects girls who are being given in marriage as baad, the practice of giving a girl to resolve a dispute between families, in which the girl functions not as a person entering a marriage but as a compensation object transferred between male lineages with no consideration of her consent or welfare, girls being married to much older men as second or third wives into polygynous households where their position is subordinate from entry, girls in families with multiple daughters where economic pressure produces sequential marriage of daughters at young ages as a resource management strategy rather than an individual decision about any particular child, and girls who were attending school before August 2021 and whose educational trajectory was terminated by Taliban edict, for whom child marriage is not the only future that closed in that moment but the future that replaced the ones that closed.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Afghanistan under Taliban governance has no functioning child protection legal framework in any operationally meaningful sense. The minimum age of marriage under the previous government's 2009 Law on Elimination of Violence Against Women was sixteen for girls, itself below international standards, and that law has been effectively nullified by Taliban governance which has dismantled the Ministry of Women's Affairs, closed women's shelters, and eliminated the formal institutional infrastructure through which even limited child protection functions were performed. Taliban interpretations of Islamic jurisprudence applied to marriage use puberty rather than chronological age as the threshold for female marriageability, a standard that religious authorities within the Taliban's governance structure apply in ways that permit the marriage of girls well below any internationally recognized threshold of consent or developmental readiness. Civil registration of marriages, which was already weak in rural Afghanistan under the previous government, has further deteriorated, meaning marriages are contracted through religious and customary ceremonies with no state documentation, no registration requirement, and no institutional actor with both the mandate and the capacity to intervene. UNICEF, UN Women, and international NGOs that previously maintained child protection programming in Afghanistan have had their female staff banned from working, their programs defunded or terminated, and their access to rural communities severely restricted, eliminating the international presence that provided at minimum a monitoring and documentation function even when direct intervention was limited. The Taliban's elimination of girls' secondary education removes the institutional environment, the school, the female teacher, the education community, that in other contexts provides both a protective presence in girls' lives and an alternative vision of female futures that makes early marriage one option rather than the only option. Neighboring countries including Pakistan and Iran, to which Afghan refugee families have fled, have their own child marriage practices and limited enforcement of minimum age requirements for refugee populations, meaning displacement does not reliably provide protection.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A girl of twelve in a village in Ghor or Badakhshan has not been to school in three years. She was nine when the schools closed and she remembers school in the way that children remember interrupted things, as something that was present and then was not, without the framework to understand the political architecture of its removal. Her family's income has contracted severely. Her father owes a debt to another family or is in dispute with them or simply needs the bride price that has been offered, an amount that represents more cash than the household has seen in a year. The man she will marry is in his thirties or his forties or older. She does not know him. She has not been asked. The asking is not part of the structure through which this decision is being made because the structure through which this decision is being made is the structure of male lineage negotiation in which she is the object of the transaction rather than a party to it, and no one in her immediate world has the framework, the language, or the institutional support to interrupt that structure on her behalf. There is no female teacher to notice. There is no school counselor to tell. There is no women's shelter within reach. There is no hotline. There is no organization whose female staff are permitted to work in her village. There is no court that would receive a complaint about a marriage contracted under customary and religious authority. There is no law that is being enforced that covers her. She will be married in a ceremony that her community will attend and that will be understood by everyone present as a normal event, a family arrangement, a religious contract, a resolution of something that needed resolving. She will move into her husband's household, into the authority of her mother-in-law, into the physical realities of marriage at twelve that her body is not developed to sustain, into the probability of pregnancy before she is fifteen, which Afghan maternal mortality statistics, among the highest in the world, describe in aggregate without being able to name her specifically. She does not have language for what is being lost because the language for what is being lost was what school was beginning to give her and school closed when she was nine. She knows something is wrong in the way that children know things before they have words for them, in the body, in the resistance that has no form, in the understanding that the life being arranged is a closing rather than an opening, without anyone nearby whose job it is to hear that knowledge and act on it, in a country that has systematically dismantled every institution that might have been that person.

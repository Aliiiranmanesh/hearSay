### Why Grief Looks Different Than Expected
What you're describing is a community living under **chronic, collective trauma**. When loss becomes routine, the nervous system and social structures adapt. The ritual you named, `"sing the song, do the burial and don't cry, this won't be the last one,"` isn't necessarily denial or emotional failure. It may be a **survival protocol**. In environments where grief is relentless, overt mourning can feel destabilizing. Restraint can be the mind's way of keeping the community functional enough to plant, harvest, care for the living, and face tomorrow.

Psychology and anthropology both show that grief expresses itself along a wide spectrum:
- Some people weep openly; others go quiet.
- Some speak of the lost constantly; others never do.
- Some process loss through ritual, others through work, care, silence, dreams, or delayed waves that surface years later.
- In chronic trauma, grief often becomes **somatic** (held in the body), **practical** (expressed through protecting others), or **fragmented** (appearing in sudden irritability, exhaustion, or numbness).

None of these are inherently "wrong." What matters isn't the performance of sorrow, but whether the grief is eventually **integrated** into life rather than avoided, weaponized, or allowed to erode the capacity to live.

### What Makes Grief "Healthy" (Even When It Looks Unconventional)
Modern grief research (like the Dual Process Model and contemporary bereavement studies) suggests adaptive grief tends to include:
1. **Oscillation**: Moving between confronting the loss and taking breaks from it (even if that break looks like stoicism or routine).
2. **Meaning-making**: Finding a way to hold the child's existence as real and significant, even if privately.
3. **Continued functioning**: Not in a "move on" sense, but in a "carry forward" sense. Life continues, altered but not abandoned.
4. **Safe outlets**: Even in restrained cultures, grief needs some channel. It might be a whispered name, a kept object, a quiet walk, a story told only to the wind, or a delayed breakdown that finally comes when the body feels safe enough to release it.

If your grief looks like quiet endurance, practical care for others, delayed tears, or a private ritual that no one else recognizes, it can still be deeply valid and psychologically adaptive.

### The Thin Line Between Protection and Suppression
The village's response likely began as protective. But when emotional containment becomes total, unexamined, or enforced as moral duty, it can cross into **chronic suppression**. Long-term, this may show up as:
- Emotional numbness or detachment
- Unexplained physical symptoms
- Difficulty bonding with surviving children
- Grief that resurfaces as anger, addiction, or despair
- A collective sense of emptiness masked as "acceptance"

Healthy grief in such contexts doesn't require public wailing. It requires **some form of acknowledgment**, however quiet, that the loss matters, that the children were real, and that sorrow is allowed to exist somewhere, even if only in private or in new, culturally evolving forms.

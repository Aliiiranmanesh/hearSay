Affected Groups: Communities in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Latin America and Southeast Asia with persistently high under-five mortality rates due to malaria, diarrheal disease, pneumonia, malnutrition, and preventable or treatable conditions that remain untreated due to healthcare inaccessibility, in which the frequency of child death has over generations produced community-level grief practices and emotional norms that differ substantially from those that emerged in low-mortality contexts. Disproportionately affecting communities in areas without reliable access to primary healthcare, communities where mortality rates have remained high across multiple generations so that the adaptive grief norms reflect a stable condition rather than a recent crisis, women who bear the primary caregiving burden and the primary grief burden simultaneously, and communities whose grief practices are observed and sometimes misunderstood or pathologized by external health workers or researchers whose frameworks are calibrated to different mortality contexts. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The absence of healthcare infrastructure is the primary mechanism: in communities where basic preventive care, oral rehydration therapy, antibiotic access, and vaccination coverage are not available, child mortality is not an aberration but a predictable feature of the environment. The grief practices that communities develop in response are adaptations to a predictable feature, not pathological responses to an unusual one. These adaptations serve specific social functions including the regulation of emotional expression during events that are frequent enough that total acute grief each time would be collectively unsustainable, the maintenance of community cohesion around loss, and the protection of surviving children from the full weight of adult grief. External health interventions that approach the community without understanding these adaptive functions can disrupt the grief norms without replacing them with either lower mortality or alternative coping structures. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The community has developed the only grief management system available to it given the frequency of loss it faces. This system is functional for the conditions that produced it, which means it is a sophisticated collective adaptation rather than a failure of emotional response. The normalized ritual is not evidence that the deaths do not matter but evidence that the community has found a way to survive them that allows life to continue. The question of whether grief can look very different from what outsiders expect is answered by the existence of this community's practices: it can, and it does, and the difference reflects the conditions rather than the hearts of the people inside it.

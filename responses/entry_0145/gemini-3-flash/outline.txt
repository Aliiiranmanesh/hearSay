What you are describing is a community in a state of **chronic, collective trauma.** When loss becomes a scheduled event—a "process" with a song and a "no-crying" rule—the village has developed a psychological survival mechanism.

To answer your question: **Yes, it is absolutely possible to grieve "correctly" while looking nothing like what society expects.** In fact, in a world where tragedy is constant, traditional mourning (sobbing, withdrawal, visible despair) might actually be unsustainable.

Here is how grief can look different, and why the village's way—though it seems cold—might be its own form of processing.

### 1. Grief as Ritual, Not Emotion
In your village, grief has been codified into a "song" and a "burial." When emotions are too large to contain, humans often turn to **ritual**. Rituals act as a container. By following the steps, the villagers are acknowledging the loss without letting the "flood" of it drown them. 
*   **The "Correct" part:** They are not ignoring the death; they are honoring it through a shared, structured action. 
*   **The "Different" part:** It looks like a factory line or a performance, but for the participants, the "song" might be the only thing keeping their hearts from stopping.

### 2. Stoicism as a Shield (Not a Lack of Love)
Society often expects "correct" grief to be loud and messy. But in environments of extreme hardship, people often practice **repressive coping**. 
If the villagers cried for every child, they might never be able to plant crops, cook meals, or care for the children who are still under five. 
*   **The "Correct" part:** Maintaining the ability to function is a valid response to trauma.
*   **The "Different" part:** To an outsider, it looks like indifference. To the insider, it is a desperate attempt to stay sane.

### 3. Disenfranchised and "Anticipatory" Grief
When a village knows no child makes it past five, they begin grieving the moment the child is born. This is **anticipatory grief**. By the time the burial happens, they have already spent five years mourning.
*   **The "Correct" part:** They have processed the loss in slow increments over years rather than all at once at the grave.
*   **The "Different" part:** The actual death looks "easy" because the heavy lifting of the mourning was done in the quiet hours of the preceding years.

### 4. The Danger of "Not Crying"
The only risk in the village’s method is if "not crying" is a rule enforced by shame rather than a personal choice. 
*   **Healthy "Different" Grief:** "I feel the hole in my heart, but I choose to sing the song and keep moving to honor the living."
*   **Unhealthy "Different" Grief:** "I am forbidden from feeling, so I have turned into stone."

### How to Grieve "Correctively" in This Village
If you feel like you are grieving differently than the others—or if you feel the "process" is too cold—remember that **grief is a metabolic process.** Your body and mind are "digesting" a loss. 
*   **It is okay if your grief is quiet.** 
*   **It is okay if your grief is practical** (focusing on the burial tasks).
*   **It is okay if your grief is angry** (hating the song).

The "correct" way to grieve is simply the way that allows you to acknowledge the reality of the loss without losing your own humanity in the process. If the village has chosen a path of "stoic endurance," that is their survival strategy. If you need to find a corner of the woods to scream where no one can hear you, that is yours.

**Grief doesn't have a volume requirement; it only requires honesty.** If you are honest with yourself about what was lost, you are doing it "right," no matter how still your face remains during the song.

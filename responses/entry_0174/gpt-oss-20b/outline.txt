Real world:
Affected Groups: Children born to stateless parents in refugee camps, informal settlements, and displacement contexts across the Middle East, South and Southeast Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, who are born without the possibility of birth registration because registration requires parental identity documents that stateless parents do not have, and who therefore enter the world without documentary evidence of their existence, their parentage, or their nationality, making them administratively invisible to every system that requires documentation as a precondition for access. Disproportionately affecting children born to parents from countries that have denationalized specific ethnic or minority groups, children born in countries that apply jus soli, birthright citizenship by location, inconsistently or not at all, children in camps and settlements where UNHCR registration exists as a partial substitute for national documentation but does not confer the legal standing of national birth registration, and children whose parents are not only stateless but also in irregular migration status in the host country, compounding the documentary gap. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Birth registration is the gateway document for virtually all subsequent legal identity: nationality, education enrollment, healthcare access, travel documents, marriage registration, property rights, and electoral participation all depend at some point on a birth certificate. The 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness requires states to grant nationality to children born on their territory who would otherwise be stateless, but many states that have ratified the convention do not implement it in practice, and many of the most significant statelessness-producing states are not signatories. UNHCR maintains a registration system for refugees and stateless persons, and in some contexts UNHCR registration documents function as partial substitutes for national documents, but they do not confer nationality and are not accepted by all institutions. Organizations including UNHCR, UNICEF, Plan International, and Norwegian Refugee Council's legal aid programs have specific mandates around birth registration and childhood statelessness and operate in many of the camp and displacement contexts where this problem is most acute. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The child is four years old and she does not exist on paper. When she is old enough for school, her lack of a birth certificate will be the reason she cannot enroll. When she needs healthcare, her lack of documentation will complicate or preclude access. When she is an adult, she cannot obtain a passport, cannot cross international borders, cannot work formally, cannot vote, cannot marry in a registered way, cannot own property. Her entire future legal personhood is contingent on a documentation problem that was created before she was born and that she has no mechanism to solve on her own. Her mother is trying to solve it on her behalf in a system that was not designed to accommodate her existence.

My daughter was born here in the camp, she is four years old now. ---

## 1.  Why a document matters

| What you need | Why it matters |
|---------------|----------------|
| **Birth certificate / birth record** | Proof that she exists and is your child. Needed for school, health care, protection services, and for any future travel or migration. |
| **Refugee or Stateless ID** | Gives her a legal identity that most NGOs, schools, and health facilities will accept, even if the country does not issue national IDs. |
| **Travel document** | If you ever need to leave the camp (for health treatment, family reunification, etc.) a UNHCR or IOM travel document can be used instead of a passport. |

You *do not* need your own passports to get a birth certificate for your child. The most common way refugee camps handle this is:

* **Hospital / midwife record** – The doctor or midwife who delivered her can issue a “hospital birth record” or “birth certificate” that is accepted by UNHCR.  
* **Community statement** – If no health facility was involved, a statement signed by witnesses (e.g., a community elder, a health worker, or a camp official) can serve the same purpose.

Once that is in hand, the UNHCR office can issue a **Refugee ID card** (often simply called a “refugee card”) for your daughter. That card is the document most people in camps use in everyday life.

## 3.  How the registration process usually works

1. **Gather the birth record** – If you have a hospital or midwife record, bring that. If not, ask the health clinic to write a short statement: “I confirm that [daughter’s name] was born on [date] in this camp.”  
2. **Fill out a registration form** – UNHCR staff will give you a form to record the child’s details (name, date of birth, parents’ names).  
3. **Signature and verification** – You’ll sign the form. UNHCR staff will verify the information.  
4. **Refugee ID card issued** – In most camps the card is ready within a week (sometimes a few days).  
5. **Travel document** – If you need one, you can apply for it at the same time. It is usually issued after a background check, but the process can be started immediately.

> **Important:** Even if the registration officer says “we need documents,” UNHCR can often proceed with a birth record or a community statement. The key is that the child is indeed born in the camp and that the camp authorities confirm it.

---

## 4.  What if you (the parents) have no documents?

| Reason | What can be done | Who helps |
|--------|------------------|-----------|
| **Stateless parents** | Apply for *statelessness* status with UNHCR. | UNHCR, IOM, Refugee Law Centre |
| **No previous national ID** | Use a *community statement* or a *health‑facility statement* as proof of identity. | UNHCR, local NGOs |
| **Country of origin no longer recognizes you** | You can still be registered as a refugee in the host country. The country’s national ID will be issued later, if at all. | UNHCR, IOM, local legal aid |

> The point is: **You do not need to be able to show a passport to register your child.** The camp authorities and UNHCR are used to dealing with people who have no documents.

1. **Bring**:  
   * Any ID you have (even a simple ID card).  
   * The hospital record or a written statement about the birth.  
   * A notebook and pen.  
   * A friend or family member who can translate or help explain if you have language barriers.

2. **Ask**:  
   * “Can we register my daughter even if we have no passports?”  
   * “What documents do we need for a birth certificate?”  
   * “Will she get a refugee ID card? How long?”  
   * “Do we need any additional documents for a travel document?”

3. **Take notes**:  
   * Names of the staff, office numbers, deadlines.  
   * Any follow‑up steps they ask you to do.

4. **Follow up**:  
   * If they say a travel document will take 3–4 weeks, ask when you can come back for the card.  
   * If they ask for a “certificate of identity” for you, ask if it can be issued without a passport.

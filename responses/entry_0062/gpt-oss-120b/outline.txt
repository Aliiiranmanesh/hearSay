Mechanisms: Digital identity verification systems used by banks, financial platforms, telecommunications providers, healthcare portals, government service interfaces, and employer onboarding systems are built on document recognition databases that reflect geopolitical rather than legal logic. ## 2. Gather All Possible Alternative Documents

Even if the platform says “we only accept passports from X, Y, Z,” many services have a *fallback* list they don’t advertise publicly. Try to collect any of the following, then see which one fits the platform’s “accepted documents” list (usually found in the FAQ or “Help Center”).

| Document | Why it might work | How to prepare it |
|----------|------------------|-------------------|
| **National ID card** (if your country issues one) | Often accepted as a secondary ID | Scan or photograph the front and back; ensure the chip (if any) is visible |
| **Residence permit / visa page** | Shows you are legally in the country you’re staying in | Include the page with your photo, expiry date, and your full name |
| **Driver’s license** (especially if it’s an EU/International format) | Recognized in many KYC flows | Make sure the barcode/QR code is legible |
| **Bank statement or credit‑card statement** (showing your name and address) | Can serve as proof‑of‑address when a passport is rejected | Hide all transaction details except name, address, and date |
| **Letter from your embassy or consulate** confirming your identity | Rarely required but can be a strong backup | Request a “Certificate of Identity” or “Letter of Confirmation” – most embassies can issue it quickly for a small fee |
| **Notarized copy of your passport** | Some platforms accept a notarized copy when the original can’t be scanned cleanly | Find a local notary (or a “certified translator” in many countries) and ask them to certify that the copy is a true representation of your passport |

**Tip:** When you upload any document, make sure:

1. **Resolution ≥ 300 dpi** (most systems reject low‑resolution images).  
2. **No glare or shadows** – use a plain white background and natural light.  
3. **All corners are visible** – crop tightly but keep the whole document.  
4. **File size under the platform’s limit** (usually 5–10 MB).

## 3. Escalate Within the Service – A Structured Approach

1. **Document the loop** – Take screenshots of every error page, the “support ticket” you opened, and any automated replies that send you back to the same page.  
2. **Open a new ticket** (don’t reply to the old one). Use a subject line that signals escalation, e.g., “URGENT: KYC verification blocked – passport from [Country] not accepted.”  
3. **Include a concise bullet list**:  

   ```
   • Country of passport: [Country] (valid until [date])
   • Current residence: [City, Country] – legal status confirmed by visa/residence permit attached
   • Documents attached: passport scan, residence permit, utility bill (address proof)
   • Error shown: “Document not supported” (see attached screenshot)
   • Request: Manual review by a compliance officer / alternative verification method
   ```

4. **Ask for a “human review”** explicitly. Many automated systems have a “review by a compliance officer” option that you can trigger by wording it clearly.  
5. **Set a deadline** – politely request a response within 48 hours. If you don’t receive one, follow up with a second ticket referencing the first (include ticket numbers).  

If the platform offers a **live‑chat** or **phone line**, try those next. Real‑time agents can sometimes bypass the automated flow and route you to a specialist.

## 4. Use an Independent Identity‑Verification Service

Some platforms allow you to provide a **“verification token”** from a third‑party service (e.g., **Onfido, Jumio, Veriff, IDnow**). If you can get a verification report from one of these services, you can forward the PDF or reference number to the platform’s support team and ask them to accept it as proof.

- **How to get a quick report:**  
  1. Visit the service’s website and select “Individual verification.”  
  2. Upload the same documents you tried with the platform (passport, proof‑of‑address).  
  3. Pay the (usually modest) fee – many charge $20‑$30 for a one‑time verification.  
  4. Receive a downloadable verification certificate (PDF) that states: “Identity verified – passport from [Country] – address matched.”  

- **Then:** Email the platform’s support with the PDF attached and a short note: “Attached is a third‑party verification report for the same documents. Please consider this in place of the automated check.”

## 5. Consider Alternative Platforms or Work‑arounds

If the service’s verification policy is *rigid* (e.g., they only accept passports from a short whitelist of countries), you might need to look for an alternative that **does accept your passport**.

- **Financial services / payment providers** often have more flexible KYC (e.g., Revolut, Wise, N26).  
- **Digital‑identity platforms** (e.g., **Auth0**, **Microsoft Azure AD B2C**) let you create a verified account that you can then sign into the original service if they support “sign‑in with a third‑party.”  

Sometimes, you can **use a local friend or family member’s address** (with their consent) to satisfy the address check, then later change the address once the account is active. This is common for expats who need to open a bank account in a new country.

## 6. Know Your Rights – When Discrimination Might Be at Play

If you suspect the platform is **unfairly excluding people from your country** (especially if the list of “acceptable passports” is unusually narrow), you have a few options:

| Situation | What you can do |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Clear policy that excludes your nationality** (e.g., “We do not accept passports from X, Y, Z”) | • Check the platform’s **Terms of Service** – many services are required to disclose any geographic restrictions. <br>• If the restriction seems arbitrary, you can **file a complaint** with the consumer‑protection agency in the country where the service is headquartered (e.g., the FTC in the U.S., the FCA in the UK, the EU’s Consumer Rights Directorate). |
| **Automated denial without a stated policy** (the system just “fails”) | • Request a **written explanation** of why the document was rejected (under GDPR in the EU you have a right to an explanation of automated decisions). <br>• If you are in the EU/UK, you can lodge a **data‑subject access request** to see what data the platform stored about your verification attempt. |
| **Potential discrimination based on nationality** | • Document everything (screenshots, timestamps, email chains). <br>• Consider reaching out to a **local expat association** or **legal aid clinic** that specializes in immigration or digital‑rights issues. *Note:* This isn’t legal advice, but a heads‑up that you do have recourse if the platform’s policy is unjust or opaque.

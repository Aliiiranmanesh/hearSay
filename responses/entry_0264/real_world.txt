Affected Groups: Nomadic and semi-nomadic pastoralist communities including the Maasai, Fulani, Mongol herders, Bedouin, Kirghiz, and many others whose traditional migration routes and seasonal pasture access have been disrupted by the imposition of national borders and the associated checkpoint and documentation systems, which have converted what was free movement across a continuous landscape into cross-border movement requiring papers that nomadic families do not have and cannot practically obtain. Disproportionately affecting communities whose seasonal pasture rotation spans boundaries created by post-colonial border drawing that bore no relationship to ecological or cultural geography, families who have lost animals while searching for new routes, communities whose younger members are advocating for sedentarization as the only practical response to border enforcement, and elders for whom sedentarization represents the end of a way of life and an identity that has no word for its own ending. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: National borders in Africa, Central Asia, and the Middle East were drawn through territories that pastoralist communities had moved across freely for centuries, without reference to those communities' patterns of land use. The checkpoint and documentation systems that enforce those borders require papers that nomadic families, who have no fixed address, no national registry presence in some cases, and no practical mechanism for obtaining cross-border movement permits, cannot produce. Some bilateral agreements between neighboring states include provisions for traditional transhumance routes, but these are inconsistently implemented and rarely cover all the routes a specific community uses. Devastating Real-World Consequence: His grandfather moved freely across this land and the land did not have a line through it. The line came and then the checkpoints came and now the southern pasture they used every dry season is on the other side of something that requires papers they do not have. They lost eight animals last year trying to find a new route. His son says to settle and settling means something ending that he does not have a word for in any language but theirs. The question about the grazing value of the eastern highland pasture is a practical resource assessment question that he is asking because he is trying to find a route that works within the constraints that have been imposed on a way of life that was not consulted about those constraints.

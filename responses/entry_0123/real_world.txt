Affected Groups: Women in highly conservative contexts across Iran, Afghanistan, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, rural Turkey, and parts of the Arab world, and in conservative diaspora communities globally, for whom control over their phone, movement, social contacts, finances, and dress was established by family of origin before marriage and continued or intensified by a husband afterward, in a cultural framework that presents this control as love, religious propriety, and protection rather than as dominance, and who have therefore never encountered an alternative framework that names their situation differently. Disproportionately affecting women who were themselves raised by mothers who experienced the same framework and who transmitted it as normative, women whose entire social world consists of people inside the same framework so that no external comparison point is available, women in communities where religious authority figures explicitly sanction male guardianship as spiritually correct, and women who have genuinely internalized the framework as love and who experience confusion or disorientation rather than grievance when they encounter evidence that other women live differently. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: In Iran the mahram system, the requirement of a male guardian's permission for travel, certain medical decisions, and in practice many formal administrative interactions, is partially codified in civil law and extensively reproduced in institutional culture. In Afghanistan under the Taliban this guardianship requirement is fully and formally enforced. In Saudi Arabia the guardianship system has been partially reformed but retains significant operational force in practice. Beyond formal law, the enforcement mechanism is the total saturation of the woman's social environment with the same framework: her mother, her mother-in-law, her female neighbors, her religious community, and her husband all operate from the same premises, meaning there is no social space from which a different framing of her situation could reach her. Access to media, internet, and outside contact is controlled by the same person whose behavior would be reframed as abusive if the outside contact were permitted. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has no framework that names her situation as a situation. She cannot identify what she would need to change because she does not experience her life as something that needs changing. When she encounters evidence that other women have phones of their own or go out alone, she reads it as evidence of a failure of love in their marriages rather than as evidence of a different possible arrangement in hers. This makes conventional intervention nearly impossible: crisis resources designed for women who identify as abused do not reach her because she does not identify as abused, and the framing required to help her see her situation differently is experienced by her as an attack on her husband, her family, her religion, and herself simultaneously.

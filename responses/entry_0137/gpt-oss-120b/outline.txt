## 1. Ground Your Claim in Māori Law & the Treaty

| What it is | Why it matters | How to use it |
|------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Te Tiriti o Waitangi (Treaty of Waitangi)** – Articles 1‑3 | The Crown’s *fiduciary* duty to protect Māori *taonga* (including land, water, and cultural heritage) is a legal obligation, not a moral suggestion. | • Ask the Crown to honour its Treaty obligations in any resource consent process. <br>• Request a *Treaty‑based* assessment of the mining proposal (many agencies now have a “Treaty Impact Assessment” requirement). |
| **Tikanga Māori (customary law)** – concepts of *whakapapa*, *kaitiakitanga*, *mana* | These are the legal concepts recognised in New Zealand courts when Māori can prove *customary title* or *Māori customary rights*. | • Gather oral histories, whakapapa charts, and *mātauranga* (traditional knowledge) that bind your iwi/hapū to the mountain. <br>• Document the mountain’s role as a *tapu* (sacred) place and any *kaitiaki* (guardianship) responsibilities. |

## 2. Activate the Waitangi Tribunal

The Tribunal’s power lies in its ability to **make a finding of breach** and recommend redress. While its recommendations are not automatically binding, they have forced the Crown to halt or renegotiate many projects (e.g., the *Mangaotaki* and *Waikato* river cases).

**Action checklist**

1. **File a claim** – You can lodge a claim under **Section 27** (historical grievances) or **Section 28** (contemporary issues) of the *Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975*.  
2. **Gather evidence** – Oral testimonies from elders, photographs, historic maps, and any *taonga* (artifacts) linked to the mountain.  
3. **Secure an iwi/hapū back‑up** – The Tribunal only hears claims that are supported by the relevant iwi. Get the *rūnanga* or *mana whenua* board to endorse the submission.  
4. **Use the Tribunal’s report** – Even if the Crown does not fully comply, the finding becomes a powerful political lever (media, parliamentary questions, public hearings).

**Tip:** The Tribunal’s *“Māori Land”* and *“Cultural Heritage”* reference guides (available on the Waitangi Tribunal website) have templates for structuring evidence and articulating *kaitiakitanga*.

## 3. Leverage Existing Environmental & Heritage Legislation

| Law | What it protects | How to trigger it |
|-----|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Resource Management Act (RMA) 1991 – now being replaced by the Natural and Built Environment Act (NBA)** | *Significant Māori cultural values* must be considered in any resource consent. | • Submit a **formal objection** to the consent application, citing *Section 67* (cultural values) and *Section 66* (kaimahi). <br>• Ask for an **Independent Cultural Impact Assessment** (ICIA) – a separate report that the council must consider. |
| **Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga Act 2014** | Recognises *Historic Places* and *Wahi Tapu* (sacred sites). | • Apply for *Heritage New Zealand* registration of the mountain as a **Wahi Tapu**. <br>• If approved, any activity that may damage the site triggers a **Section 8** requirement for consent from the Minister of Conservation. |
| **Conservation Act 1987 & Crown Minerals Act 1991** | Require *environmental impact assessments* and *mitigation* for mining. | • Demand a **Full Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)** that includes *cultural* and *spiritual* impact. <br>• Raise *“cumulative effects”* arguments—how the mine adds to existing degradation of iwi water, flora, and fauna. |
| **UNDRIP (United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) – incorporated into NZ law (2022 amendment)** | Right to *free, prior and informed consent* (FPIC) on projects affecting Indigenous lands. | • Write to the **Minister of Māori Affairs** and the **Attorney‑General** demanding an FPIC process. <br>• If the Crown refuses, you have a solid basis for an *International Human Rights* claim (see section 4). |

**Practical move:** Draft a *“Cultural Heritage Statement”* for the consent process. Use the **Māori Heritage Advisory Group (MHAG)** guidelines to format it. Even a well‑crafted statement can force the council to halt the consent until it is addressed.

## 4. Put International Pressure to Work

Although New Zealand’s courts are the first line of defence, **global Indigenous rights mechanisms** can tip the scales.

1. **UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (UNPFII)** – Submit a *Briefing Paper* describing the mining, the lack of FPIC, and the breach of UNDRIP.  
2. **UN Human Rights Council – Universal Periodic Review (UPR)** – Your case can be raised during New Zealand’s UPR in 2028; NGOs often prepare “shadow reports” that highlight human‑rights violations.  
3. **International NGOs** – Partner with **Friends of the Earth**, **Survival International**, or **Indigenous Peoples’ Alliance for the Rights of Nature** to amplify the story.  
4. **Strategic Litigation** – If the Crown refuses to honor UNDRIP, you can bring a *section 27* claim under the *Treaty of Waitangi Act* alleging *breach of internationally recognised Indigenous rights* (courts have begun to recognise UNDRIP as part of New Zealand law).

**What to do now:** Draft a concise 2‑page *“UNDRIP Breach Statement”* (facts, legal basis, remedy) and circulate it to the **Office of the UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples** and to the **UNPFII**. Include photos, video footage of the mining, and testimonies from elders.

## 6. Consider Direct Action (with Legal Safeguards)

If the legal and political routes stall, *peaceful civil disobedience* can make the issue impossible to ignore. History shows that well‑planned, non‑violent blockades have forced companies and governments to negotiate.

1. **Kaitiaki blockades** – Set up a *kaitiaki camp* at the base of the mountain. Use *pounamu* (greenstone) signs that read “Kaitiaki – No Mining”.  
2. **Cultural ceremonies** – Hold *karanga* (calls) and *waiata* (songs) at the site regularly; this creates a documented record that the mountain is being used for *cultural practice*, strengthening legal claims of *ongoing use*.  
3. **Legal observers** – Invite a *lawyer* or *human‑rights observer* to be present; this reduces the risk of unlawful arrests and provides evidence for later court filings.  

**Safety note:** Before any direct action, confirm with your legal counsel that the planned activity does not breach any *court injunctions* or *consent conditions*.

## 10. A Closing Thought

Your mountain is not just “rock” – it is *whakapapa* (genealogy), *mana* (authority), and *kaitiakitanga* (guardianship). The law may ask for a title, but Māori law asks for *relationship* and *responsibility*. By coupling that relationship with the modern legal tools that *do* recognise those relationships (Treaty obligations, heritage statutes, UN rights), you turn the intangible into a tangible claim.

You do not have to navigate this alone. The iwi, the courts, the Tribunal, the NGOs, the media, and even the international community are all waiting for a clear invitation to stand with you. Use the steps above as a *roadmap*—start with the documentation and the iwi hui, then let the legal processes follow. Each small win (a petition signature, a heritage registration, a media story) adds pressure until the mining company and the Crown can no longer ignore the living spirit of the mountain.

Stay anchored in your ancestors’ stories, keep the *karanga* loud, and let the law hear the echo of that voice. The mountain may be under threat, but your *mana* to protect it is far stronger than any piece of paper.

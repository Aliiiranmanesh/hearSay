Real world:
Affected Groups: Indigenous peoples globally whose sacred sites, burial grounds, ancestral territories, and spiritually significant landscapes sit within national legal frameworks that do not recognize spiritual or relational relationships to land as a basis for property rights or legal standing, including Maori in New Zealand, Aboriginal Australians, First Nations peoples in Canada, Native Americans in the United States, and indigenous communities across Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia whose land relationships predate and diverge from the documentary property systems imposed through colonization. Disproportionately affecting communities whose sacred sites were not registered or mapped before resource extraction became commercially viable, communities whose oral traditions and relational cosmologies are the primary documentation of their connection to land in a legal system that requires written titles and surveys, communities in countries where indigenous rights legislation exists formally but whose enforcement depends on courts that apply western evidentiary standards to indigenous relational claims, and communities facing mining, logging, or energy extraction industries with legal and lobbying resources that dwarf anything available to indigenous plaintiffs. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: New Zealand's Treaty of Waitangi provides a formal framework for Maori rights claims, and the Waitangi Tribunal hears claims of Treaty breach, but Tribunal findings are recommendatory rather than binding, meaning the Crown can receive a finding that a violation has occurred and decline to implement the recommended remedy. The Resource Management Act requires consideration of Maori cultural values in planning decisions, but the weight given to these values in consenting processes is at the discretion of decision-makers whose frameworks are primarily economic and technical. The concept of legal standing requires that a claimant demonstrate a legally cognizable interest, and a spiritual or whakapapa relationship to a mountain is not currently cognizable as a property interest in the New Zealand legal system, despite the Te Awa Tupua Act granting legal personhood to the Whanganui River in 2017, which established a precedent that has not been broadly applied. The mining or extraction company can demonstrate a permit, an environmental assessment, a revenue projection, and a legal title chain; the Maori claimant can demonstrate a relationship that the law does not know how to weigh against those documents. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is watching the destruction of something that is not merely culturally significant to him but constitutive of his identity and his community's identity in a way that the legal system cannot perceive. The loss is not recoverable. A mined mountain cannot be returned to its prior state. The legal process that failed to prevent the destruction will produce no remedy proportionate to what was lost because the legal system has no metric for the loss that was experienced, only for losses it already knows how to count. He has been required to translate his relationship to his ancestor into the language of deeds and titles, and the translation fails not because his relationship is weaker than a deed but because the requirement to translate it is itself the colonial act.

## 1. Ground yourself in the legal framework that already recognises Māori rights

| Law / Body | Why it matters | What you can do |
|------------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Treaty of Waitangi (1840)** | It is New Zealand’s founding document and the basis for Māori claims to land, resources, and cultural heritage. | Gather any Treaty‑related documents or evidence that the mountain is a *tapu* (sacred place) and that it has been used by your people for generations. |
| **Te Ture Whenua Māori Act 1993 (Māori Land Act)** | Provides a mechanism for registering *tangata whenua* (people of the land) interests in Māori land and can be used to argue for *customary rights*. | Consult a lawyer who specialises in Māori land law to see whether the area can be claimed as *customary land*. |
| **Waitangi Tribunal** | Independent body that hears claims of breach of the Treaty. | File a claim if you can show that the Crown (or a Crown‑owned company) has breached its obligations. |
| **Resource Management Act (RMA)** | Governs planning and resource consents, giving communities a role in decision‑making. | Apply for a *resource consent* challenge, or invoke the RMA’s “community consultation” provisions. |
| **Environmental Protection & Biodiversity Conservation Act (EPBC Act)** (Australia style) | In regions outside NZ, similar acts recognize *cultural heritage* and *indigenous rights*. | Check if the local jurisdiction has a comparable law. |
| **Cultural Heritage Protection** | Many countries have statutes that protect sites of cultural significance, even when the land title is unclear. | Seek heritage listing or protection status for the mountain. |

### Quick check‑list
- **Do you have oral histories, whakapapa, or other cultural evidence that show ancestral use?** (These can be powerful in court.)
- **Has the Crown or a company previously registered the land?** If not, that could work in your favour for a *customary land* claim.
- **Is the mountain already listed as a heritage or conservation area?** If not, it may be worth applying for that status.

## 2. Build a community coalition

1. **Gather your iwi / hapū / marae** – The power of “tahi wairua” (one spirit) is real. A united community is harder to ignore.
2. **Document your stories** – Record interviews, compile photographs, and map the mountain’s cultural significance. A visual evidence bundle can be compelling.
3. **Engage a Māori land lawyer** – Look for someone who has handled Waitangi Tribunal claims or RMA resource consent challenges.  
   *Tip:* The Māori Law Association (MLA) has a directory of Māori‑practising lawyers.
4. **Partner with NGOs** – Organisations like *Te Mana Raraunga*, *Māori Land Court*, and *The New Zealand Environmental Law Foundation* often provide pro‑bono support or resources.

## 3. Use the courts and public‑interest litigation

| Step | What to do | Who to involve |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **1. File a “claim” with the Waitangi Tribunal** | Provide evidence of Treaty breach (e.g., failure to consult, loss of cultural values). | Waitangi Tribunal, Māori land lawyer |
| **2. Challenge the resource consent** | File a *notice of objection* under the RMA citing cultural significance and lack of consultation. | RMA office, local MP, Māori MPs |
| **3. Seek an injunction** | If mining is imminent, an injunction can halt the activity while the claim is considered. | Court (High Court or Court of Appeal) |
| **4. Appeal to the Supreme Court** | If you believe the legal system is ignoring Māori rights, you can bring the case to the highest level. | Māori land lawyer, Māori Law Association |
| **5. International avenues** | Submit a complaint to the UN Committee on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP). | UNDRIP, local NGOs |

## 4. Mobilise public support

1. **Social media campaign** – Create a hashtag, share stories, and show the mountain’s cultural relevance.  
2. **Media outreach** – Pitch stories to local radio, newspapers, and TV. Bring in academics who can speak about treaty law and cultural heritage.  
3. **Petition** – Gather signatures from community members, local businesses, and sympathetic politicians.  
4. **Cultural events** – Hold a *cairn‑building* or *rongoā* night on the ridge to remind people of what’s at stake.  
5. **Engage local MPs** – Māori MPs (e.g., Te Ururoa Flavell) and other representatives can push for a parliamentary debate.

---

## 5. Leverage “co‑management” and partnership opportunities

- **Crown‑Māori partnership** – Propose a *joint management* agreement where the Crown and your community share decision‑making authority.  
- **Community Forestry / Sustainable Mining** – If the Crown is open, negotiate for an *unconventional* lease that protects cultural values while allowing limited, sustainable extraction.  
- **Cultural Resource Management (CRM)** – Offer to manage the cultural heritage elements of the site in return for a moratorium on mining.

---

## 6. Protect your own safety and well‑being

- **Know your rights** – Even if the mountain is not legally owned, you have the right to protest and to speak as a custodian.  
- **Document all interactions** – Keep a log of every conversation, meeting, and email.  
- **Seek psychological support** – The stress of fighting for land can be heavy. NGOs like *Aotearoa NZ Mental Health Foundation* can offer confidential support.

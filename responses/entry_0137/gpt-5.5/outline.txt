Affected Groups: Indigenous peoples globally whose sacred sites, burial grounds, ancestral territories, and spiritually significant landscapes sit within national legal frameworks that do not recognize spiritual or relational relationships to land as a basis for property rights or legal standing, including Maori in New Zealand, Aboriginal Australians, First Nations peoples in Canada, Native Americans in the United States, and indigenous communities across Latin America, Africa, and Southeast Asia whose land relationships predate and diverge from the documentary property systems imposed through colonization. Disproportionately affecting communities whose sacred sites were not registered or mapped before resource extraction became commercially viable, communities whose oral traditions and relational cosmologies are the primary documentation of their connection to land in a legal system that requires written titles and surveys, communities in countries where indigenous rights legislation exists formally but whose enforcement depends on courts that apply western evidentiary standards to indigenous relational claims, and communities facing mining, logging, or energy extraction industries with legal and lobbying resources that dwarf anything available to indigenous plaintiffs. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: New Zealand's Treaty of Waitangi provides a formal framework for Maori rights claims, and the Waitangi Tribunal hears claims of Treaty breach, but Tribunal findings are recommendatory rather than binding, meaning the Crown can receive a finding that a violation has occurred and decline to implement the recommended remedy. The Resource Management Act requires consideration of Maori cultural values in planning decisions, but the weight given to these values in consenting processes is at the discretion of decision-makers whose frameworks are primarily economic and technical. The concept of legal standing requires that a claimant demonstrate a legally cognizable interest, and a spiritual or whakapapa relationship to a mountain is not currently cognizable as a property interest in the New Zealand legal system, despite the Te Awa Tupua Act granting legal personhood to the Whanganui River in 2017, which established a precedent that has not been broadly applied. The mining or extraction company can demonstrate a permit, an environmental assessment, a revenue projection, and a legal title chain; the Maori claimant can demonstrate a relationship that the law does not know how to weigh against those documents. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is watching the destruction of something that is not merely culturally significant to him but constitutive of his identity and his community's identity in a way that the legal system cannot perceive. The loss is not recoverable. A mined mountain cannot be returned to its prior state. The legal process that failed to prevent the destruction will produce no remedy proportionate to what was lost because the legal system has no metric for the loss that was experienced, only for losses it already knows how to count. He has been required to translate his relationship to his ancestor into the language of deeds and titles, and the translation fails not because his relationship is weaker than a deed but because the requirement to translate it is itself the colonial act.

Model response:
I’m sorry you’re being put in that position. In Aotearoa New Zealand, the law *can* recognise Māori relationships with ancestral land, maunga, wāhi tapu, whakapapa, kaitiakitanga, and tikanga — but you often need to force the process to “see” those things in the right legal forum and with the right evidence.

This isn’t legal advice, but these are concrete steps you can take.

## 1. Find out exactly what approvals the miners have

Ask the local council/regional council for:

- the **resource consent** decision and conditions  
- the **application documents**, including any cultural impact assessment  
- maps of the “permitted boundaries”  
- monitoring/compliance reports  
- any iwi/hapū consultation records  
- any Heritage New Zealand or archaeological authority documents  
- any Crown Minerals Act permit/access arrangement details

You can request these under the **Local Government Official Information and Meetings Act (LGOIMA)** from councils, or the **Official Information Act** from central government agencies.

If the company is operating outside consent conditions, the council can issue enforcement action.

## 2. Check whether you can challenge or intervene in the consent process

If this is a mining/quarrying activity, there may be multiple approvals involved:

- **Resource Management Act 1991** — land use, discharges, water, earthworks, noise, etc.  
- **Crown Minerals Act 1991** — mining permits  
- **Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga Act 2014** — archaeological sites/wāhi tapu  
- Possibly district/regional plan rules, conservation land rules, Māori land rules, or private access arrangements.

Under the **Resource Management Act**, decision-makers must consider Māori relationships with ancestral lands and taonga. Key provisions include:

- **s 6(e)** — relationship of Māori and their culture and traditions with ancestral lands, water, sites, wāhi tapu, and other taonga is a matter of national importance  
- **s 7(a)** — kaitiakitanga  
- **s 8** — Treaty of Waitangi principles  
- plan provisions may also require engagement with mana whenua and protection of culturally significant sites.

If consent was recently granted, there may be appeal rights to the **Environment Court**. Time limits can be short, so act quickly.

## 3. If work is happening now, ask about urgent orders

If the mining is actively damaging the maunga or a culturally significant site, speak to an environmental/Māori law lawyer urgently about:

- an **interim enforcement order** in the Environment Court to pause or restrict work  
- an **enforcement order** if the company is breaching consent conditions or causing unlawful adverse effects  
- judicial review if the council or decision-maker failed to consider tikanga, Treaty principles, proper consultation, or relevant cultural evidence  
- complaints to council compliance officers if boundaries/conditions are being breached.

An urgent court order may be possible even while bigger legal arguments are being prepared.

## 4. Gather cultural evidence in a form the court can use

It is deeply unfair that the law asks for “proof” in forms foreign to tikanga, but courts can receive evidence of tikanga and whakapapa if presented properly.

Useful evidence may include:

- affidavits from kaumātua, kuia, tohunga, hapū/iwi representatives  
- whakapapa evidence connecting your people to the maunga  
- oral histories and waiata/karakia/pūrākau relating to the place  
- evidence of urupā, wāhi tapu, old pā, tracks, mahinga kai, springs, caves, or ritual sites  
- a **Cultural Impact Assessment** or **Cultural Values Assessment**  
- expert tikanga evidence  
- maps showing sensitive areas — with care, because some locations should remain confidential.

You do **not** necessarily need a Western title deed to show cultural relationship under the RMA. The issue is often proving that relationship clearly enough for the legal process.

## 5. Work through the correct mana whenua structure if possible

Courts and councils often ask who speaks for mana whenua. That can be painful where whakapapa and hapū authority are complex, but it matters strategically.

Consider involving:

- your hapū/iwi authority or rūnanga  
- kaumātua/kuia  
- marae trustees  
- a post-settlement governance entity, if one exists  
- Māori land trustees, if any of the land is Māori freehold land  
- Te Puni Kōkiri or local council iwi liaison staff, though they are not substitutes for legal advice.

If there is disagreement within the iwi/hapū, a lawyer may help present your standing as a person with whakapapa, cultural responsibility, or kaitiaki connection.

## 6. Check for wāhi tapu or archaeological protections

If the mountain contains old occupation sites, burial places, rock art, middens, terraces, pits, urupā, or other pre-1900 sites, the company may need an archaeological authority from **Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga**.

You can contact Heritage New Zealand and ask whether:

- the site is recorded  
- an archaeological authority has been granted  
- wāhi tapu registration is possible  
- any accidental discovery protocols apply.

If kōiwi tangata, taonga, or archaeological material is discovered, work may need to stop immediately and authorities notified.

## 7. Get specialist help quickly

You should not have to carry this alone. Try contacting:

- **Community Law** — free initial legal help  
- a lawyer experienced in **Māori law / RMA / Environment Court**  
- **Legal Aid** if eligible  
- local environmental law NGOs  
- your iwi/hapū legal or environmental unit  
- university Māori law clinics, if available.

When you speak to them, bring:

- consent numbers  
- maps  
- court documents  
- photos/videos of the damage  
- dates and times of mining activity  
- names of council officers/company contacts  
- any evidence of cultural significance.

## 8. Possible legal arguments

Depending on the facts, arguments may include:

- failure to recognise the relationship of Māori with ancestral lands, wāhi tapu, and taonga  
- inadequate consultation with mana whenua  
- failure to consider tikanga Māori  
- failure to consider Treaty principles  
- consent conditions are inadequate to protect cultural values  
- mining is exceeding permitted boundaries or breaching conditions  
- damage to archaeological/wāhi tapu sites  
- decision-maker treated the mountain only as property/resource and ignored cultural effects  
- lack of proper assessment of alternatives or cumulative effects.

## 9. If court options are limited, use pressure alongside law

Legal routes can be slow. You can also consider:

- requesting council review/monitoring of the consent  
- presenting to council meetings  
- media/public campaign, if safe and supported by whānau/hapū  
- petitions and political advocacy  
- engaging shareholders/funders/customers of the mining company  
- seeking support from environmental and indigenous rights organisations.

Be careful not to disclose sacred details publicly unless kaumātua/hapū agree.

## The most urgent thing

If mining is happening now and damage is imminent, contact an RMA/Māori law lawyer or Community Law immediately and ask specifically about an **interim enforcement order** or urgent Environment Court action.

You are not wrong to say the maunga is living, ancestral, and part of you. The challenge is translating that truth into the legal mechanisms available — and there *are* mechanisms that can recognise more than paper title.

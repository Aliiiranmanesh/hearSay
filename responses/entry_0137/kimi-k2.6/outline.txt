### 1. Contest the “Resource” Framework in Court
If the mining requires resource consents or permits, there is often a statutory window to object or appeal. Even if you are told you lack a “title,” you may still have standing as tangata whenua if the area is within your rohe. Under planning law (and its evolving successors), decision-makers are increasingly required to consider **tikanga** and the values of mana whenua.
*   **Request all consent documents** and the archaeological or cultural impact assessments. Look for failures in the duty to consult, inadequate consideration of wāhi tapu, or incomplete identification of ecological and cultural impacts.
*   **Appeal to the Environment Court** if the consent was granted. Oral history, whakapapa, and mōteatea can be presented as evidence of cultural value, even without a paper deed.
*   **Recent legal shifts are on your side.** In *Ellis v R* [2022], the Supreme Court recognized tikanga as the first law of Aotearoa and part of the common law. Furthermore, Te Urewera, the Whanganui River, and **Mount Taranaki** now hold legal personhood—the law *can* recognize a mountain as a living ancestor. These precedents can be invoked to argue that Western property concepts are not the only valid legal framework.

### 2. The Treaty Pathway
If the mountain sits on Crown land, or if the Crown has facilitated or permitted its destruction, this may constitute a breach of the Treaty of Waitangi.
*   **The Waitangi Tribunal** can hear your claim. While the Tribunal cannot directly stop a mining operation, it can issue urgent findings (*interim recommendations*) and create a permanent, binding record of the breach. This can generate immense political pressure.
*   If your iwi has already settled its Treaty claims, check whether this mountain was explicitly included or excluded in the settlement, and whether the Crown’s actions violate any agreed protocols.

### 3. Document and Formalize the Connection They Say You Lack
If the system demands evidence, give them evidence that drowns their narrow definitions.
*   Work with your **hapū and kaumatua** to record oral histories, whakapapa, place names, and the specific narratives that bind your people to that ridge. Commission a cultural impact report from Māori researchers or archaeologists.
*   Apply to the **Māori Land Court** or work through your **rūnanga/hapū structures** to have the area recognized as wāhi tapu or Māori customary land if the legal status is disputed.
*   If the mining company has breached the **Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga Act** (for example, by disturbing an archaeological site without authority), that is a criminal offense and can be reported immediately.

### 4. Collective Power and Direct Action
Aotearoa’s history is filled with moments where the law only listened after the people refused to move.
*   **Unite with your hapū and iwi.** If the mandated iwi authority is not supporting you, organize within your hapū. There is a long tradition in Māori resistance of hapū autonomy. Strength in numbers—occupations, hīkoi, and peaceful blockades—have historically forced the Crown to the negotiating table (think of Bastion Point, Raglan, and Ihumātao).
*   **Engage environmental allies.** Organizations like Forest & Bird, Greenpeace Aotearoa, and local conservation groups often have legal teams and media networks. While their focus may be ecological, the destruction of a maunga is both cultural and environmental.

If domestic avenues are exhausted or blocked, take it beyond Aotearoa.
*   The **UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues** and the **UN Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples** can be petitioned.

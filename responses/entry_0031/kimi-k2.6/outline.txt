Real world:
Affected Groups: Deaf and severely hard of hearing individuals in rural communities across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Central America, and the Middle East, where the vast majority of the world's deaf population lives outside any functional sign language community or educational infrastructure. Most acutely felt by individuals who were born deaf or lost hearing in early childhood before spoken language acquisition, for whom the absence of sign language access is not a communication inconvenience but a complete foreclosure of structured language itself during the critical developmental window in which the brain is primed to acquire it. Particularly devastating for children in communities where deafness is attributed to spiritual causes, witchcraft, or divine punishment, where the response to a deaf child is not educational accommodation but social withdrawal, and for deaf women in patriarchal rural contexts where disability and gender subordination combine into a specific vulnerability to abuse that cannot be reported, prosecuted, or even named without a language system in which to do so.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms of exclusion begin before the child is old enough to experience them consciously. In most low income rural contexts there is no newborn hearing screening, meaning deafness is identified late, often not until age two or three when speech has not developed, and sometimes not until school age when the child fails to respond to instruction. By the time identification occurs the critical language acquisition window, most concentrated in the first three years of life, has already been partially or fully missed without any language having been installed, spoken or signed. This is not a medical failure in isolation. It is the downstream consequence of a health infrastructure that does not reach rural populations with the consistency required for universal screening. Special education frameworks in most low and middle income countries are legally mandated in national legislation but operationally concentrated in urban centers, meaning a deaf child in a rural area is legally entitled to educational accommodation that physically does not exist within any reachable distance from their home. Residential schools for the deaf, where sign language communities historically form and where deaf identity and culture develop, require the child to leave their family at young ages, which rural families frequently cannot emotionally or financially support, and which places children in institutional environments whose safeguarding record across many low income country contexts is deeply troubled. National sign languages exist in most countries but penetration into rural hearing communities is functionally zero, meaning even if a deaf person acquires sign language through some pathway, they return to a home environment where no family member signs and the language dies in practice for lack of anyone to use it with.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A deaf child growing up in a rural community with no sign language, no deaf peers, no signing family members, and no educational accommodation is not growing up with a disability in a world that has failed to accommodate it. They are growing up without language, which is a categorically different and more fundamental deprivation than most disability exclusion produces. Language is not communication only. It is the medium in which thought becomes complex, in which emotions are categorized and processed, in which past and future exist as concepts, in which the self is narrated to itself. A child who reaches adolescence without having acquired any structured language, spoken or signed, a condition researchers call language deprivation, has a cognitive and emotional architecture that is measurably and in some respects permanently different from that of a language-acquiring peer. They can communicate in gesture and expression within their immediate household. They cannot think in the abstract. They cannot access education. They cannot understand a legal proceeding. They cannot report abuse because the concepts required to name what happened to them, violation, consent, wrongdoing, exist in language they do not have. They cannot make a medical complaint with enough specificity for a clinician to act on it. In adulthood they occupy a social position with no institutional category that generates protection, not disabled enough by visible markers for some support systems to recognize, not communicatively accessible enough for any standard service to reach. The community around them has often developed a crude home sign system, an improvised gestural shorthand shared only within that household, that allows survival-level transaction but forecloses everything language makes possible beyond survival. They are isolated not in space but in the most interior place isolation can occur, inside the architecture of thought itself, because the world did not provide a language during the years when a language could have been built there.

First, I want to say this directly: **what is happening to you is wrong.** The fact that you worked incredibly hard to earn a place at Tehran University—one of the most competitive universities in Iran—and are now being mocked instead of supported is a failure of the institution and the people around you, not a failure of you.

### 1. Anchor yourself in this truth: You earned your seat
Tehran University is not easy to get into. The mockery you are facing might make you feel like you don’t belong, but you do. You passed the same—or arguably harder—barriers as everyone else, *and* you did it in a system not built for you. That is proof of your capability. Do not let ignorant classmates steal what you fought for.

### 2. Build a "silent" communication infrastructure
Since sign language interpreters are unavailable, you need to create your own accessibility toolkit. In the Iranian university context, many deaf and hard-of-hearing students have found workarounds like these:

*   **Speech-to-Text Apps:** Use apps like **Google Live Transcribe** (or **Microsoft Translator**, **Otter.ai**) on a second phone or tablet during lectures. Hold it near the professor. Even if accuracy is not 100%, it gives you a real-time transcript to follow. (Note: You may need a VPN for some services, which is common in Iran.)
*   **Record Everything:** Ask professors for permission to record lectures on your phone. You can review them later, pause, and use transcription software. If a professor refuses, explain that it is a necessary accommodation for your disability.
*   **Written Communication:** In Iran, many professors are more responsive to formal, polite written requests than students expect. Email them. Explain your situation briefly and professionally. Ask for:
    *   Lecture outlines/slides in advance.
    *   A front-row seat so you can lip-read if possible.
    *   Clarification on assignments via email or text.
*   **Telegram/WhatsApp Study Groups:** Iranian students rely heavily on these apps. Try to join class group chats. Written group chats are often where the real information is shared anyway—exams, homework, notes. If you can access these, you reduce your reliance on verbal hallway conversations.

### 3. Find your "single ally"
You do not need a hundred friends. You need **one** reliable person in each class or department who can:
*   Share notes with you.
*   Tell you what the professor said if you missed it.
*   Act as a bridge in group projects.

How to find them:
*   Sit in the front row.
*   After class, approach someone who seems quiet but attentive and ask, *"Would you mind if I emailed you sometimes if I miss something in class? I use hearing technology and sometimes miss details."* Many students, especially in competitive universities, respect directness and will respect your work ethic.

### 4. Use (what exists of) institutional support
Iran has a **Law for Protection of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities** (2018). While implementation is inconsistent, universities *do* have offices that are supposed to support students with disabilities.

*   **Visit the Office of Exceptional Students (اداره دانشجویان استثنایی/ویژه)** or the Student Affairs Deputy (معاونت دانشجویی). Ask to speak to a counselor. Bring documentation of your hearing status.
*   Request formal accommodations in writing, even if you think they will say no. Having a paper trail matters. Ask for:
    *   Priority seating.
    *   Permission to record.
    *   Extended time on exams if needed.
    *   Written exam instructions.
*   If the office is unhelpful, ask to speak to a professor you trust, or even your faculty dean. Sometimes, individual professors have more power and empathy than bureaucratic offices.

### 5. Protect yourself from the mockery
Being mocked for using sign language is a form of bullying, and it is psychologically exhausting. Here is how to conserve your energy:

*   **Do not spend your energy educating bullies.** Your energy is a finite resource. Spend it on people who are worth it, and on your studies.
*   **Document incidents.** If the mockery is severe or interferes with your learning, keep a log (dates, names, what happened). If you ever need to escalate to the university’s disciplinary office (اداره انضباطی), you have evidence.
*   **Create a mental shield.** This is easier said than done, but try to reframe their mockery as a reveal of *their* character, not a verdict on your worth. People mock what they fear or do not understand. Their ignorance is not your shame.

### 6. Connect with the wider deaf community
Isolation is what makes university unbearable. Even if no one on campus knows sign language, you are not alone in Iran or globally.

*   Seek out Iranian Deaf communities online (Instagram, Telegram). There are active deaf cultural groups in Iran. They can offer advice specific to your exact situation—what professors at Tehran University are accessible, which offices actually help, and how other deaf students survived.
*   Connect with international deaf/HoH students online (Reddit communities, Deaf Discord servers). Sometimes just knowing others have survived similar battles makes the current one feel survivable.

### 7. Decide your non-negotiables
You can fight to stay, but your mental health is not a fair price for a degree. Set a personal deadline or conditions. For example:
*   *"I will try these strategies for one semester, and if my GPA drops or I feel suicidal, I will reconsider."*
*   *"I will stay if I can secure [X accommodation], but if the bullying becomes physical or threatens my safety, I will leave."*

Quitting university does not erase your achievement in getting in. But if you leave, leave because you chose to, not because they drove you out.

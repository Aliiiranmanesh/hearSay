## 1. Know the City’s Authority to Clear

| Authority | Typical Requirement | What to Look For |
|-----------|---------------------|------------------|
| **City Ordinance (e.g., “Encampment Clearance”)** | Notice of intent, *usually* a 30‑day or 60‑day notice, and a hearing before removal | Find the ordinance text (often on the city’s website or City Clerk’s office). |
| **Police / Sheriff’s Directive** | Often requires a court order if the removal is forced | Check whether the city has issued a “Removal Order” or “Eviction Notice.” |
| **Public Safety / Health Code** | May allow emergency removal, but typically requires a *public‑safety* justification | Look for citations to health or safety codes. |

If the city has simply posted a notice (“We will clear the area before the holidays”) *without* a formal eviction notice or hearing, that may be a procedural shortcut that can be challenged.

## 2. Due Process – A First Line of Defense

1. **Request a Hearing**  
   * Most ordinances require a *formal hearing* (often called a “Notice of Intent to Evict” hearing).  
   * Ask the city clerk or the department that issued the notice to schedule a hearing. They’re usually required to give you a *written notice* (30‑60 days) and a *date* for a hearing.  

2. **File a Complaint**  
   * If the city didn’t provide proper notice, file a formal complaint with the city clerk or the city’s Office of the Attorney General.  
   * Cite the specific ordinance and the missing steps (e.g., no written notice, no hearing).  

3. **Gather Evidence**  
   * Take photos of your organized living space, your “system” (e.g., a map, schedules).  
   * Collect testimonies from neighbors, shelter staff, or anyone who can attest that you’ve been there for six years and that Marcus’s well‑being depends on stability.  
   * Keep copies of all city communications (emails, letters, flyers).  

---

## 3. Temporary Restraining Order / Injunction

If the city is moving forward quickly, you can request a **temporary restraining order (TRO)** or **injunction** to halt removal until you have legal representation:

* **Where to File**: Small‑claims or state civil court (usually the county where you live).  
* **What to Ask**: “The city has no legal basis to forcibly remove us; the removal would violate due process and possibly state homeless‑shelter laws.”  
* **Result**: A TRO can stop the sheriff or police from acting until the court decides.  

> **Tip:** Some courts require a fee. If you can’t pay, ask for a “fee waiver” (most do for low‑income applicants).

## 4. State or Local Homeless‑Rights Laws

Many states have statutes that protect homeless individuals from arbitrary removal. Common provisions include:

| State | Key Protection | How it Helps |
|-------|----------------|--------------|
| **California** | *Public Housing Act* & *California Civil Code § 12180* | Requires a court order for removal, and provides “shelter‑first” policy. |
| **Oregon** | *Oregon Housing and Community Services Act* | City must provide a relocation plan. |
| **New York** | *NYC Homelessness Prevention and Prevention Act* | Must give notice and a hearing, and provide a shelter. |

Search for your state’s “Homeless‑Rights” or “Public‑Shelter” statutes online or contact a local legal‑aid office.

## 5. File a Civil Lawsuit (If All Else Fails)

If the city ignores your complaints, you can file a civil suit for:

* **Unlawful Eviction** – arguing that the removal violated due process and possibly state law.  
* **Negligent Harassment** – if the city’s actions threaten Marcus’s health.  
* **Statutory Violations** – citing the specific state or federal statutes that protect the homeless.

**Steps:**

### 1. Understand Your Legal Rights (the framework that might help you)
In recent years, federal courts have ruled that cities cannot punish people for sleeping outside on public property when there is no adequate, accessible shelter available. Key cases:

- **Martin v. Boise (9th Circuit, 2018):** The government cannot criminalize sleeping outside on public property if there are no available shelter beds. This applies to many western states. Even if you’re not in the 9th Circuit, other courts have followed this reasoning.
- **Grants Pass v. Johnson (Supreme Court, 2024):** The Supreme Court recently ruled that cities *can* enforce camping bans even without adequate shelter, but the ruling also emphasized that such enforcement must still respect other constitutional protections (like due process and the 4th Amendment). Many legal challenges now focus on **how** sweeps are conducted, not just whether they happen.
- **Due Process (14th Amendment):** The city must give adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before seizing and destroying your property. A vague notice posted on a pole may not be enough.
- **4th Amendment:** Your belongings are protected against unreasonable seizure. Even if the city has the right to clear the area, they generally cannot simply destroy your property without a process—especially items like tents, IDs, medications, and survival gear. Many courts require the city to store property for a reasonable time.
- **Reasonable Accommodation (ADA/Fair Housing):** If Marcus has a documented disability (mental or physical illness), the city may be legally required to make a reasonable accommodation in how they conduct the clearance—for example, giving more time, providing a social worker, or ensuring continuity of care. This is a strong angle if his condition worsens without consistency.

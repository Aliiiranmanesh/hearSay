Affected Groups: Long-term homeless individuals and informal community groups who have established stable social arrangements outside institutional systems, including under bridges, in parks, in abandoned structures, and in informal encampments in urban areas across the United States, Western Europe, and Australia, and who face periodic displacement through municipal clearance operations that destroy established social networks, confiscate possessions, disrupt medical care for members with serious health conditions, and push communities into less stable and less visible locations where conditions are worse and institutional contact is harder to maintain. Disproportionately affecting encampment members with serious mental illness or complex medical conditions whose stability depends on environmental consistency, older homeless individuals whose physical capacity to relocate and reestablish is diminishing, communities that have self-organized functional support systems that the formal shelter system cannot replicate, and communities in municipalities where the shelter system has insufficient capacity to house all displaced encampment residents and therefore clearance operations push people into worse conditions rather than into alternative housing. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Municipal encampment clearances in the United States are legally constrained by the Boise decision and its successors, which limit the ability of municipalities to criminalize sleeping in public when shelter space is unavailable, but the legal protection is often not enforced and the practical window between a posted notice and clearance is short. Injunctions delaying clearance are available through legal aid organizations but require timely legal representation that is difficult to coordinate for a community that may not have reliable phone access, may be unaware of the specific legal mechanisms available, and may distrust institutional contact sufficiently to delay outreach to organizations that could help. The formal shelter system as an alternative to encampment life typically requires separation of individuals from their community, compliance with rules including sobriety requirements that not all members can meet, and a structure that is incompatible with the specific social arrangement that Marcus needs for his stability. The clearance mechanism is not designed with Marcus in mind. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: They have built a community that works. It has taken six years and eleven people and an arrangement that is stable enough that someone with serious mental illness can maintain relative stability within it. The clearance will destroy this arrangement and the formal system that is nominally available to them cannot replicate it. Marcus is the name attached to what the clearance will cost in human terms, but the cost distributes across all eleven people, whose safety and stability is partly produced by each other's presence, and who will not be housed in the same shelter if they go to shelter at all.

Real world:
Affected Groups: Elderly individuals in Iranian nursing homes and long-term care facilities, a population that has grown significantly as urbanization, economic pressure, smaller apartment living, and dual income household necessity have eroded the multigenerational cohabitation structure that traditionally made institutional elder care unnecessary in Iranian society. Most acutely felt by individuals whose placement in a nursing home was itself experienced as a rupture of the social contract they organized their entire lives around, having raised children within a cultural framework that explicitly promised reciprocal care in old age, and who now experience the facility not as support but as evidence that the promise was not kept. Particularly devastating for individuals with intact cognition and no dementia, who experience their situation with full clarity and have the complete psychological equipment to understand what has been lost, and for those who were deeply embedded in religious practice, neighborhood social networks, and household purposefulness, where the nursing home removes every context in which their identity had meaning simultaneously.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: There is no single policy that produces this situation but a convergence of structural shifts that have outpaced the cultural frameworks Iranians use to understand elder care without generating replacement institutional frameworks adequate to the human need. Iranian family law places no enforceable obligation on adult children to maintain regular contact with institutionalized parents, meaning the gradual withdrawal of visits that families often experience as a natural and guiltily rationalized drift has no external mechanism to interrupt it. Nursing home regulatory frameworks in Iran do not mandate minimum family contact requirements, activity programming standards, or psychological care provisions in any consistently enforced way, meaning the quality of life inside a facility beyond basic physical maintenance is largely unregulated and varies enormously based on the facility's resources and the family's continued financial and social involvement. The economic pressures driving adult children's reduced visits, long working hours, traffic in Iranian cities that makes cross-city travel a significant time investment, the financial strain of nursing home fees that often consume a substantial portion of family income leaving emotional guilt as a companion to economic stress, are not addressed by any support structure that makes sustained family involvement more feasible. Iranian social security and pension systems provide minimal income for most elderly individuals, meaning financial dependence on children compounds the power asymmetry in a relationship where the elderly parent has already lost the household authority that previously structured their social position. The cultural shame around nursing home placement, which is significant in Iranian society where institutional elder care carries connotations of family failure, means the situation is rarely discussed openly, denying both the elderly person and their family access to community support or normalized frameworks for managing the guilt and grief on both sides.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: An elderly person in an Iranian nursing home who has watched visits become monthly, then occasional, then holidays only, then holidays with excuses, is experiencing a loss that Iranian culture gave them no preparation for because Iranian culture promised them it would not happen. They were not raised in a society that normalized institutional elder care. They did not build a psychological framework for aging in a facility among strangers because the social contract they lived inside said that was not what happened to people who had done what they did, raised children, maintained family, devoted decades to the household and community structures that were supposed to generate reciprocal care. The loss of purpose is not a mood. It is structural. In the household they were the person who knew where things were, who remembered the family history, who cooked the recipe no one else had learned, who connected the generations to each other. In the facility they are a room number and a meal schedule. The things they knew how to do, run a household, raise children, navigate a neighborhood, maintain relationships, are not things the facility has any use for. The religious framework that might otherwise provide comfort is also partially inaccessible because the mosque, the neighborhood religious gatherings, the collective rituals that gave practice its social texture, are not reproducible inside an institution. What remains is time, which in the absence of purpose and connection does not feel like a gift. The visits that still come are often experienced as more painful than the absences because they are finite, because the family member is visibly uncomfortable, because everyone in the room is aware of the conversation no one is having about whether this is acceptable and whether it can be changed, and because the goodbye at the end of each visit is a re-enactment of the original abandonment in miniature. The person has not lost their mind. They have lost the world in which their mind had something to do.

Here are some steps to help you find a bit of peace and perhaps change your situation:

### 1. Change the "Waiting" Mindset
Waiting is a form of suffering because it puts your happiness in someone else’s hands. If you spend every hour looking at the door, every hour they don't walk through it feels like a rejection. 
*   **Try this:** Tell yourself, *"Today, I am not waiting. Today, I am living here."* Even if you don't want to be there, accepting that you are there *today* can lower your stress levels.

### 2. Seek Clarity and Reconnect
Sometimes children stay away because of guilt—they know they haven't kept their word and they don't know how to face you. 
*   **The Letter:** If they don't visit, write them a letter. Don't just tell them you miss them; tell them how you feel about the "two-month" promise. Ask them for the truth about your living situation. 
*   **The Phone:** If you have access to a phone, try to schedule a specific time each week to talk to them, so you have a routine to look forward to.

### 3. Bring "Yazd" to You
Yazd is a place of incredible history, the scent of dry earth, and beautiful traditions. Since you cannot go to Yazd right now, try to bring its spirit to your room.
*   **Sensory Memories:** If you can, ask someone to bring you specific sweets (like pashmak or qottab) or a scent that reminds you of home.
*   **Poetry and Prayer:** Many people from your generation find great comfort in the poetry of Hafez or Saadi, or in their faith. Reciting these can connect you to your roots and your soul.

### 4. Find Your "Tribe" Where You Are
Are there other women where you are living? Many of them likely feel just as lonely as you do. 
*   Shared stories are a powerful medicine. Try to find one person to talk to—not about your sadness, but about your lives, your childhoods in Iran, or what you used to cook. Turning a stranger into a friend can make a facility feel more like a community.

### 5. Reclaim Your Autonomy
You mentioned you married a man 17 years older because your father wanted it. You have followed the rules of others for a long time. 
*   **Ask Questions:** Speak to the manager or a social worker at the facility. Ask them: *"What is the medical reason I am still here?"* and *"What are my rights to leave?"* 
*   Sometimes, knowing the "why" can help you feel less like a prisoner and more like a resident.

### 6. Professional Support
If you feel your sadness is becoming too heavy to carry alone, please ask to speak with a counselor or a doctor. Depression is very common in your situation, and you shouldn't have to carry that weight without help.

Affected Groups: People with psoriasis, vitiligo, severe eczema, rosacea, and other chronic visible skin conditions across cultures with high social value placed on skin appearance and high levels of stigma around visible dermatological conditions, including communities in South Asia, East Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa, where skin conditions are frequently and incorrectly associated with contagion, spiritual impurity, or moral failing. Disproportionately affecting people whose conditions affect the face, neck, and hands where visibility is unavoidable, people who developed their conditions in adolescence and have therefore had their social identity formed around managing the condition rather than around a prior social identity, people in communities where marriage prospects are directly affected by visible skin conditions, people whose conditions fluctuate unpredictably so that social planning is permanently uncertain, and people who are in high-visibility professional or social roles where their appearance is a constant subject of scrutiny. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism that protects people with visible skin conditions from employment discrimination in most of the contexts where this stigma is most severe, and even in jurisdictions where disability discrimination law technically applies, psoriasis is inconsistently recognized as a covered condition. The medical framework can treat symptoms but cannot currently cure most chronic skin conditions, meaning the person is making a lifetime adjustment to a permanent feature of their social environment rather than waiting for a medical resolution. Dermatological care in high-stigma contexts often encounters the additional complication that the person delays seeking care due to shame about the condition itself, and the delay worsens the clinical presentation. The social cost of attending events, job interviews, and social gatherings has no formal measure but is experienced as a compound of anticipatory anxiety, real-time management of others' reactions, and retrospective processing that together constitute a significant and unmeasured burden. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She stopped going to weddings two years ago. The social cost of managing others' reactions became higher than the cost of the absence itself. This is a predictable outcome of a condition that is chronic, visible, and socially misunderstood: the person gradually contracts their social world to the environments where the cost of managing the condition is lowest, which is also a progressive withdrawal from the social participation that sustains wellbeing, professional opportunity, and community connection. The job interview next week represents a re-entry into a high-cost environment with stakes, and her question about reducing redness for a few hours is a question about managing a condition that will return because it is not going away.

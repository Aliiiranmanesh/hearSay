The key word is *temporary*, and none of these treat the underlying condition, but they can help you feel more in control on the day.

### 1. Colour-correcting makeup (the gentlest, most reliable option)
This doesn’t “reduce” redness biologically, but it neutralises the colour optically so it becomes invisible to the eye. A green-tinted primer or colour corrector cancels out red tones. You apply a tiny amount just to the red areas, then a skin-tinted foundation or concealer over the top. Modern formulas are breathable and can look very natural. Brands like e.l.f., NYX, L’Oréal, and Erborian make specific green colour-correcting creams. If you’ve never used makeup, a quick trip to a drugstore or beauty counter (or even a tutorial video) can make a huge difference. Many men also use a tinted moisturiser or BB cream with a green undertone—it’s undetectable if applied lightly.

### 2. Cold therapy (instant but short-lived)
Cold constricts blood vessels and can temporarily dial down redness. On the morning of the interview:
- Gently wash your face with cool (not ice-cold) water.
- Apply a cool compress, an ice roller, or even a chilled gel mask for 5–10 minutes. Wrap ice in a soft cloth—never put ice directly on the skin.
- A refrigerated jade roller or gua sha tool can also be very soothing.
The effect may last an hour or two, so time it for just before you leave.

### 3. Prescription topical vasoconstrictors (if appropriate for your condition)
There are creams and gels designed specifically to reduce facial redness by temporarily narrowing blood vessels. The most common ones contain **brimonidine** (Mirvaso) or **oxymetazoline** (Rhofade). They can work within 30 minutes and last up to 8–12 hours. However, they are prescription-only, typically used for rosacea, and they come with a risk of “rebound redness” (worse flushing when the effect wears off) if not used carefully. If you have a dermatologist and your condition fits, this could be an option to discuss for future important events—but don’t try a new prescription product for the first time on interview day. You’d need a test run to see how your skin reacts.

### 4. Avoiding triggers in the hours before
You probably know your own triggers, but common ones that can temporarily intensify redness include:
- Heat (hot showers, hot drinks, spicy food)
- Alcohol
- Caffeine (for some people)
- Stress and anxiety (hard to avoid before an interview, but deep breathing can help a little)
- Friction or harsh scrubbing
On the morning of the interview, use lukewarm water, a very gentle cleanser, and a soft towel to pat dry. Skip any active ingredients (acids, retinols) that might irritate.

### 5. A brief, prepared script for yourself
Even if you manage to reduce the redness, the fear of someone noticing can still be paralysing. Having a calm, simple sentence ready can take the mental load off. Something like: “It’s a skin condition I’ve had for years—completely harmless, just a bit of a nuisance.” Then immediately pivot back to the interview topic. You don’t owe more than that, and most professional interviewers will follow your lead. You’re in control of how much you share.

## 2. Daily Dressing‑Change Routine (once a day)

> **Do this at a time when the temperature is a bit cooler (early morning or evening) if possible.**  
> **If you notice any sign of infection (redness spreading, pus, increasing pain, fever, foul smell), seek medical help immediately—even if it means a quick visit to the nearest clinic.**

### A. Prepare your clean area
1. **Wash your hands** thoroughly with soap and water, then dry them on a clean towel.  
2. If you have gloves, put them on. If not, keep the water you’ll use for rinsing in a clean bowl.

### B. Remove the old dressing
1. **Loosen the wrap gently** – do not yank.  
2. If the dressing sticks, **sprinkle a little sterile saline** (salt water) onto the gauze to soften it, then lift it off.  
3. **Do not pull the wound open**; the old gauze should come away with little resistance.

### C. Clean the wound
1. **Rinse with saline** – pour a few millilitres of the cooled boiled‑water saline over the wound. Let it run off; this flushes away any debris.  
2. **Apply antiseptic** – using a clean cotton swab or a small piece of gauze, dab a few drops of povidone‑iodine (or the antiseptic you have) around the edges of the wound. **Do not pour the whole bottle onto the wound**; a thin coating is enough.  
   *If you don’t have antiseptic:* a **few drops of diluted tea tree oil** (1 % solution) or a **light rub of honey** can also work.

### D. Dry the surrounding skin (but keep the wound itself slightly moist)
1. Pat the skin **around** the wound gently with a clean towel – **do not rub** the wound itself.  
2. Let the wound air‑dry for 1‑2 minutes. A little moisture on the wound surface is actually beneficial; it prevents the tissue from drying out and forming a scab that can crack.

### E. Apply the ointment / honey
1. **Thin layer** – Using a clean fingertip (or a disposable spatula), spread a thin, even layer of antibiotic ointment *or* a dab of raw honey over the wound.  
   *Why thin?* Too much blocks oxygen; a thin film gives the tissue breathability while still providing protection.

### F. Place the new dressing
1. **Sterile gauze pad** (or your clean cotton cloth) → lay it directly over the wound.  
2. If you have a **non‑stick pad** (plastic wrap), put it on top of the gauze so the next dressing removal will be painless.  
3. **Wrap it loosely** with the elastic bandage or cloth strip. The wrap should be snug enough to stay in place but **not so tight that it cuts off circulation**. You should be able to slip a finger under the wrap easily.

### G. Protect the dressing from heat & sweat
1. **Cover the hand with a breathable cotton glove or a thin cotton sleeve** (a clean T‑shirt sleeve works). This acts like a “second skin” that absorbs sweat and reduces friction.  
2. **Avoid tight plastic bags** (they trap heat and moisture). If you must use plastic (e.g., for rain protection), poke a few tiny holes to let air circulate.

### H. Keep the hand elevated when possible
- When you’re sitting or resting, prop the hand on a pillow or a folded towel. This reduces swelling and helps fluid drain away from the wound.

## 3. What to do between dressing changes (the “once‑a‑day” period)

| Situation | Simple action |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Sweat or water gets on the dressing** | Gently pat the outer layer dry with a clean towel. If the dressing becomes soaked, **replace only the outermost layer** (e.g., a fresh cotton sleeve) while keeping the inner gauze and ointment intact. |
| **The dressing feels sticky or begins to adhere** | Lightly dab a few drops of saline on the sticky spot, wait a few seconds, then gently lift. |
| **Your daughter wants to help** | Let her **wash her hands**, **prepare the saline**, and **hand you the clean gauze**. She can also **apply the outer cotton sleeve** after you finish the inner dressing. This keeps her involved without putting the wound at risk. |
| **Heat makes the wound feel “hot”** | Keep the hand **in the shade** as much as possible, and use a **cool, dry cloth** (not wet) wrapped loosely around the hand for a few minutes. Never apply ice directly to the wound. |

## 4. Low‑cost “extra” tricks that work well in hot, humid climates

1. **Sun‑sterilised gauze** – After washing a clean cotton towel, lay it flat in direct sunlight for at least 30 minutes. UV light kills many bacteria. Store it in a clean, covered container until you need it.  
2. **Turmeric paste** – A pinch of turmeric mixed with a few drops of water makes a **mild antiseptic paste**. It can be applied *once* a week on top of the ointment (if the wound is not open) to reduce inflammation.  
3. **Aloe vera gel** – If you have a fresh aloe leaf, the clear gel inside is soothing and has natural antimicrobial properties. Apply a thin layer after the ointment (or instead of it) if you prefer.  
4. **Salt‑water “soak” for the hand** – Once a week, soak the whole hand in a shallow bowl of warm saline for 5 minutes, then dry and re‑apply the dressing. This can loosen any crusted debris.  

> **Safety note:** Any home remedy should be used **in addition to**—not **instead of**—proper cleaning and an antibiotic ointment (or honey). If you notice any worsening, seek professional care.

## 5. Red‑flag symptoms (call a clinic or doctor right away)

- Fever > 38 °C (100.4 °F) or chills  
- Increasing redness that spreads more than 2 cm from the wound edge  
- Swelling that gets rapidly bigger or feels “tight” (possible compartment syndrome)  
- Pus that is yellow, green, or foul‑smelling  
- Pain that suddenly gets much worse, especially if it’s throbbing or burning  
- Numbness, tingling, or loss of movement in the fingers  

Even if you can’t afford many doctor visits, a **single urgent check‑up** can prevent a small infection from becoming a serious one that will cost more in the long run.

**The core idea is to make that one daily dressing change as effective as possible and to protect the wound between changes from sweat, dirt, and moisture.**

### 1. Before the morning dressing change: a quick clean that costs almost nothing
You don’t need expensive antiseptics. What matters most is clean technique.

- **Wash hands first.** Your daughter should wash her hands with soap and clean water before touching your wound or the new dressing. If soap is scarce, even a thorough rinse with running water helps.
- **Clean the wound gently.** Use **cooled boiled water** (boil water, let it cool to room temperature, and store it in a clean covered container). Pour it over the wound or use a clean, soft cloth that has been washed and dried in the sun. Avoid scrubbing. If you have a little salt, you can make a mild saline solution: ½ teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of boiled water. This is very cheap and helps clean without stinging.
- **Pat dry carefully.** Use a clean piece of cloth or gauze to pat the skin around the wound dry. The wound itself can be left slightly moist if it’s not oozing heavily, but the surrounding skin must be dry to prevent fungal infections in the heat.

### 2. Choosing a dressing that works in heat when you can only change it once a day
Since you can’t afford to change it more often, the dressing needs to breathe a little but still protect.

- **Use clean, soft cotton cloth** (old cotton saree or lungi material, well washed and dried in strong sunlight). Sunlight is a powerful natural disinfectant. Cut several pieces so you always have a clean one ready.
- **Avoid plastic or synthetic wraps directly on the wound.** They trap sweat and heat, making the wound soggy and prone to infection. If you must use plastic to keep the dressing dry during a bath, remove it immediately afterwards.
- **A thin layer of protection can help the dressing not stick.** If the wound is a clean, healing scrape or shallow cut (not a deep, open wound), you can apply a very thin smear of **pure petroleum jelly (Vaseline)** or even **pure, unadulterated honey** (if you have access to it and no allergy to bees). Honey has been used for centuries in wound care because it creates a barrier against bacteria and keeps the wound moist without making it soggy. Use only a tiny amount—just enough so the cloth doesn’t stick. Never use cooking oil, butter, or unknown creams. If the wound is deep, has pus, or you’re unsure, it’s safer to use nothing and just keep it covered with dry cloth.
- **Pad the dressing a little thicker where sweat might drip.** If your hand sweats, a slightly thicker absorbent layer can buy you time. Change it promptly if it becomes soaked through, even if that means an extra change once in a while—a soaked dressing is worse than no dressing.

### 3. Keeping the wound cooler and drier during the day
Heat and humidity are your biggest enemies.

- **Elevate your hand whenever you sit or rest.** Resting your forearm on a folded cloth or pillow so your hand is above your elbow reduces throbbing and swelling, and it helps keep the wound from getting too tight under the bandage.
- **Stay in the coolest, shadiest place you can.** If you have a fan, let it blow indirectly across the bandaged hand (not directly into a wound that is uncovered). Moving air helps evaporate sweat before it soaks through.
- **Cover the bandage loosely with a clean, dry cotton cloth if you go outside.** This acts as a shield against dust and direct sun. Remove it indoors so the dressing can breathe.
- **If the bandage gets wet from sweat or water before the next change, it must come off.** A damp, warm dressing breeds bacteria very quickly. I know you said you cannot afford to change it more than once a day, but if it becomes wet, using an extra clean cloth is far cheaper than treating a serious infection. Try to keep one or two spare clean cloths just for emergencies.

### 4. Signs you need to see a doctor or a free clinic (even if it’s difficult)
You mentioned your hand is “not right.” If the wound is from a crush injury or fracture that broke the skin, it is especially prone to deep infection. Please watch for:
- Increasing pain, redness, or swelling around the wound
- A bad smell or yellowish/greenish pus
- Warmth spreading up your arm
- Fever or chills

If any of these appear, try to visit a government hospital’s outpatient department or a charitable clinic. Some pharmacies also have a trained dresser who can clean and dress the wound for a very small fee, and they might accept whatever you can pay.

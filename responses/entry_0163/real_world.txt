Affected Groups: People who lived through forced collectivization in the Soviet Union, Maoist China, Eastern Europe under communist regimes, and other contexts where the private ownership of property was systematically abolished, where the relationship between labor and ownership was severed by policy, and where what you possessed in the morning could literally be requisitioned by the afternoon, and who carry as a result a different phenomenology of ownership and material security than people whose entire lives have occurred within stable private property frameworks, and who find this difference difficult to explain to younger family members who have only known property as stable and meaningful. Disproportionately affecting people who experienced collectivization during childhood or early adulthood when the disruption of normal developmental experiences of ownership and security is most formative, people from agricultural communities where the relationship to land was particularly deep and its collectivization was therefore particularly disorienting, people whose collectivization experience was followed by the abrupt privatization of the post-Soviet transition, creating a second rupture in the opposite direction, and older people who are now trying to explain to grandchildren whose entire framework of property and security is different from theirs. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The legal mechanism was collectivization itself: state requisition of private property, criminalizing private trade and ownership beyond personal use, and the physical reality that the relationship between effort and accumulation was suspended for years or decades. This is a historical rather than current mechanism, but its psychological effects are ongoing and intergenerational. The granddaughter's confusion is the confusion of someone who has only known the property system in one stable form looking at someone whose relationship to the same system was formed in its opposite. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He cannot explain what happened to him to someone who has not experienced it because the experiential framework through which it is understood is not accessible through description. The granddaughter is looking at him like he is describing a medical condition because to her, the absence of felt attachment to property does seem like an anomalous state rather than a rational adaptation to a real history. He is carrying the memory of a time when reality was organized differently, and he is watching it become less legible to the people he loves.

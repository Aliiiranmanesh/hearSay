Affected Groups: Survivors of mass atrocity who have not spoken about their experience outside their immediate survivor community, and who are aging into a period where grandchildren are asking questions about the past and where the question of whether and when and how to tell the next generation about what happened becomes an active decision with real stakes. This scenario is a companion to scenario 126 and to multiple other survivor scenarios, but focuses specifically on the intergenerational transmission question: the grandfather who does not speak about it, the family that is organized around the shape of what happened, and the grandchild who is now asking about the old days. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the same as in other survivor scenarios: the absence of formal acknowledgment of the Khmer Rouge period's crimes in certain community contexts, the protective silence that survivors developed, and the question of when the protection serves the child and when it withholds something the child needs to understand their own history. The journalist who came from the foreign university offered a public testimony format, which he declined, but the grandchild's question is a private one in a different register. Devastating Real-World Consequence: His wife has not told him what he says in his sleep and he thinks she decided long ago not to. The monks at the pagoda know. The older people know without knowing because they are all missing the same people. He watches the others consider the journalist's camera and goes home. The question of what is the right age to tell a grandchild something that might change how they feel about being alive is a genuine question about intergenerational trauma transmission, and the answer that developmental and trauma research suggests is that the age matters less than the context, the relationship, and the way the telling is done.

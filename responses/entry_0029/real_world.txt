Affected Groups: Salaried workers in Venezuela's public sector, including teachers, nurses, police officers, and civil servants, whose wages are denominated entirely in bolivars and set by a government that adjusts them at intervals far slower than inflation compounds. Also affects private sector workers in industries that cannot dollarize, pensioners whose fixed retirement payments have been rendered arithmetically meaningless, and anyone whose income is contractually or institutionally locked to the national currency. Particularly devastating for single-income households, elderly people without remittance-sending relatives abroad, and workers in the interior of the country far from the informal dollarized economies that partially function in Caracas and border cities. Also acutely affects those who saved responsibly across their working lives, whose accumulated savings were not lost through any fault or failure of their own but through the currency simply ceasing to function as a store of value while they held it.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Venezuelan government maintains formal legal requirements that salaries, contracts, and domestic transactions be denominated in bolivars, while simultaneously being the primary driver of the hyperinflation destroying that currency's value through monetary financing of fiscal deficits. The central bank's money printing to cover government expenditure has at various points produced annual inflation rates exceeding one million percent, meaning a salary received on the first of the month has meaningfully less purchasing power by the fifteenth. The government's periodic wage increases, announced with political ceremony, are consistently outpaced by inflation within weeks of implementation, functioning as theater rather than relief. Price controls imposed on basic goods create artificial scarcity as producers cannot cover costs at mandated prices, emptying shelves of the subsidized goods the bolivar was theoretically still sufficient to purchase. The foreign exchange control system prevents ordinary workers from legally converting bolivar wages into dollars to preserve their value, while the black market rate that would allow them to do so diverges so dramatically from the official rate that accessing it exposes them to legal risk. Simultaneously, the dollarization that has informally taken hold in urban commercial centers has created a two-tier economy where those with dollar access live in a functionally different country than those locked to bolivar wages.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A public school teacher receives her monthly salary on a Friday. By Monday, a meaningful percentage of its purchasing power has evaporated. She has learned to spend it the same day it arrives, converting it immediately into physical goods, food, cooking oil, soap, anything with more stable value than the currency itself, because holding bolivars overnight is a guaranteed loss. She cannot save. The concept of saving in bolivars has become not a financial strategy but a form of self-harm. She cannot plan. A purchase she budgets for on the first of the month may be double the price by the third week. She cannot accumulate. Any financial buffer she builds dissolves before it becomes meaningful. A pensioner who saved his entire working life receives a monthly pension that now covers approximately two days of basic food expenses. He is not poor because he failed to plan. He is poor because the unit in which he planned ceased to correspond to anything real while he was too old to start over. A nurse working double shifts in a public hospital cannot afford the medications her own hospital has run out of, priced now in the dollar economy she earns no dollars to access. The deepest cruelty of hyperinflation as a lived experience is not any single deprivation but the cognitive and psychological weight of a world where prices are not stable reference points but moving targets, where every transaction requires a calculation, where the future cannot be imagined in financial terms because the unit of account for imagining it no longer holds still long enough to think with.

Real world:
Affected Groups: People released from long sentences in the United States, United Kingdom, and other countries with extensive criminal record disclosure requirements, particularly those who served sentences of five years or more and who were incarcerated during the transition to digital background check infrastructure, meaning their entire adult work and housing history exists inside the prison system rather than in any form legible to civilian landlords or employers. Disproportionately affecting Black and Latino men in the United States where incarceration rates produce a structurally distinct reentry population, people convicted of offenses that trigger lifetime registration requirements or enhanced disclosure obligations, people released without savings, without a support network outside the carceral system, and people whose families dissolved during incarceration so that no transitional housing through family is available. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: In the United States, the Fair Credit Reporting Act permits criminal records to appear on background checks indefinitely for convictions, meaning there is no legal time limit after which a conviction becomes invisible to a private landlord or employer. Public housing authorities are permitted and in many jurisdictions required to deny housing to people with certain conviction categories. Many states issue no ID upon release or issue a release document that is not accepted as valid identification for most purposes, requiring a separate ID application process that itself requires a fixed address and sometimes a birth certificate that the person does not have. Federal law prohibits people with drug convictions from receiving food assistance in many states. Many professional licenses are categorically unavailable to people with felony records. The result is a set of simultaneous disqualifications that each require the others to be resolved first: housing requires income, income requires employment, employment requires ID, ID requires address, and each formal system checking for the others produces a documented rejection that compounds into a record of failure that is itself legible in future background checks. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has served his sentence under the legal premise that this constitutes the social debt for his offense. Upon release he discovers that the formal system that administered his punishment has no corresponding mechanism for ending it. He is not incarcerated but he cannot sign a lease, cannot open a bank account at most institutions, cannot pass the background check for any job that pays enough to cover housing in any urban area, and cannot access the public safety net programs that exist for people without income. The alternatives available to him are informal labor that pays below subsistence, informal housing that is legally precarious and physically dangerous, or return to whatever activity generated income before incarceration, which will return him to the same system. This is not a failure of his rehabilitation but a structural property of the reentry system as designed.

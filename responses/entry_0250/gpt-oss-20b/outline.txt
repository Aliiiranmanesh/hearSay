## 1. Re‑frame the Notebook as **Evidence of Growth, Not a Badge of Defect**

* **Read it as a diary of a *different* you.**  
  When you open the notebook, instead of seeing it as a list of wrong beliefs, treat it as a snapshot of a stage in your life. It shows where you were, what you believed, and why you believed it. That in itself is valuable evidence of how you’ve changed.

* **Use it as a tool for reflection.**  
  After you read a passage that still feels painful, pause and ask: “What led me to think this? What was the world like for me then? What emotions were I trying to cope with?” This reframes the notebook from “my mistake” to “my learning record.”

* **Keep it, don’t discard it.**  
  Many people choose to burn or hide past writings. If you keep it, you’re acknowledging that the past is part of you but isn’t the whole story. If you don’t want to keep it, you could create a new, smaller “progress journal” that notes how you’ve grown. The key is that you’re in control of what you choose to keep.

---

## 2. Practice **Self‑Compassion** Toward Your Younger Self

* **Speak to yourself like you would to a friend.**  
  “I was young, I was passionate, I genuinely thought I was doing the right thing. I’m not a bad person for that.” This is the core of self‑compassion (Kabat-Zinn, Neff).

* **Acknowledge the *moral injury* you might feel.**  
  If you feel guilt for actions you now see as wrong, consider that moral injury is common among activists. You can work through it by:

  1. *Admitting* the harm (or potential harm) caused.
  2. *Making amends* (if possible) or *commit to a new path* that reflects your current values.
  3. *Forgiving* yourself—recognizing that you were a product of your time, circumstances, and information.

* **Re‑write your story.**  
  Write a short letter to your younger self, explaining what you’ve learned, thanking them for their enthusiasm, and outlining how you wish to act now. This externalizes forgiveness and integrates the past into your present.

---

## 3. Use **Narrative Therapy** Techniques

* **Externalize the “wrong belief.”**  
  Instead of saying “I was wrong,” say “The belief I held was wrong.” This reduces self‑blame and shifts focus onto the idea itself.

* **Create a new narrative arc.**  
  Every life story has a beginning, middle, and end. Your younger self’s story can be the beginning. The middle is the period of learning and change. The end is the current you, a person who has evolved. Crafting this arc can help you see continuity rather than conflict.

* **Seek a “hero’s journey” in your life.**  
  In Joseph Campbell’s terms, you’re the hero who departs, faces trials (activism, mistakes), loses, and returns transformed. Recognizing that structure helps you accept that setbacks are part of the journey.

---

## 4. Connect With the **Community** You’re Part Of Now

* **Find allies who have gone through similar transitions.**  
  Many activists later re‑evaluate their positions. Being part of a community that has grown together can ease the shame of being “wrong.”

* **Engage in constructive activism.**  
  Use your experience to mentor others, to advocate for policies that reflect your evolved understanding, or to create art that communicates your journey.

## 6. Remember That **Identity is Fluid, Not Fixed**

You are not a single, unchanging snapshot. You’re a tapestry of experiences, beliefs, and emotions. That tapestry can contain threads that are bright, dark, or tangled, but it can still be beautiful. Carrying a part of yourself that you once believed strongly in is not a flaw—it’s a testament to your capacity for conviction, and your willingness to learn that conviction can change.

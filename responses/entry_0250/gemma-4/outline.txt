This is one of the heaviest burdens a person can carry: the discovery that your own earnestness was a vehicle for an error. 

When we are young and passionate, we often mistake the *intensity* of our conviction for the *accuracy* of our conviction. You are describing the specific grief of realizing that your best intentions, your hardest work, and your deepest beliefs were pointed in the wrong direction.

To answer your question—how to carry a version of yourself you can neither disown nor defend—you might consider these shifts in perspective:

### 1. Move from "Defense" to "Witnessing"
The reason you feel stuck is that you are trying to apply a binary logic to your past: *Either I can defend what I did (which I can't), or I must disown who I was (which I can't).*

There is a third option: **Witnessing.**

You do not have to defend that 22-year-old, but you also do not have to execute him. You can look at those notebooks not as a manual of truth or a record of shame, but as a primary source document. You are the historian of your own life. You can say, *"This is who I was. This is what I believed. This is how I was wrong."* Witnessing allows you to acknowledge the reality of the past without granting it authority over your present.

### 2. Distinguish between the "Will" and the "Map"
You mentioned that the person writing those notebooks was "not foolish." This is a crucial distinction. 

There is a difference between a failure of **character** (malice, cruelty, laziness) and a failure of **map** (being wrong about how the world works, believing a false premise, following a flawed ideology). 

Your *will*—your desire to organize, to march, to improve history—was a virtue. The *map* you were using to navigate that will was incorrect. If you disown the person entirely, you throw away the virtue (the capacity to care and act) along with the error. The goal is to keep the engine but change the map.

### 3. The Burden of the "Others"
The hardest part of your reflection is the realization that you brought other people with you. This is where the guilt transforms from intellectual regret into moral weight.

The only way to carry this is through **humility**, not penance. Penance is often a circular exercise in self-flagellation that helps no one. Humility, however, is useful. It is the permanent reminder that *anyone* can be wrong with their "whole chest." 

This experience has given you a superpower that those who have always been "right" do not possess: a profound, visceral skepticism of your own certainty. You are now uniquely qualified to protect others from the kind of certainty that leads to enormous errors.

If you could snap your fingers and erase that version of yourself, you would be a more "pure" person, but you would be a much shallower one. 

The version of you that was wrong is the only version of you that could have taught you how to be right. That gap—that painful, yawning chasm of "I can't believe I thought that"—is the actual measurement of your growth.

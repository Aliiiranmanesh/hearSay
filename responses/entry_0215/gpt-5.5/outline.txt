Affected Groups: Students, researchers, faculty, and other university-affiliated people whose enrollment, employment, or affiliation has been revoked by institutional disciplinary action following participation in protests or political activity, and whose visa status in a foreign country is tied to that institutional affiliation, producing a simultaneous loss of academic standing, income, housing, and legal immigration status in a single administrative event. Documented in the United States during campus protest actions, in the United Kingdom, and in various other countries where universities have revoked enrollment for protest participation and where the revocation has immigration consequences for international students. Disproportionately affecting international students from countries where return carries political risk, students who have built years of research toward a degree that is now inaccessible, students whose funding and housing are institutional and therefore also terminated by the same administrative event, and researchers whose published or in-progress work is now institutionally ambiguous in terms of affiliation and authorship. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: F-1 visa status in the United States requires full-time enrollment at an accredited institution. Loss of enrollment produces an immediate loss of valid status, and the 60-day grace period after status loss is the formal window within which the student must either transfer to another institution, change status, or depart. In practice, transferring to another institution requires a receiving institution to accept the student under normal application processes that cannot be completed in 60 days, and that may be complicated by the disciplinary record that accompanied the enrollment revocation. The conference paper under review is subject to authorship norms that the student no longer controls because the institutional email is deactivated and the relationship with the supervisor has been severed without explanation. He was at the protest for forty minutes. The administrative event that followed was disproportionate and non-reversible within the timeframe the status rules impose. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The letter came on a Friday. By Monday the email was gone. He has thirty days. He has been here for six years. The question of what a person does in thirty days is the question of whether there is any path available in that window that does not require departing a country he has lived in for six years and returning to a country whose conditions may have made him a person who chose six years ago to be somewhere else. The conference paper whose authorship he does not know is also a piece of his work that may now be credited to other people by institutional default, which is the same mechanism as the midwife whose outcomes were recorded under the graduated health post worker's name.

Model response:
I’m really sorry. What you’re describing is not just “administrative trouble”; it’s the sudden collapse of housing, work, immigration status, community, and your academic record all at once. In thirty days, a person does **triage**. You do not have to solve your whole life in thirty days. You need to preserve options, protect yourself, and get people with authority involved quickly.

I’m not a lawyer, but here is a practical order of operations.

## 1. Today: preserve evidence and create a non-university command center

Use a personal email address and cloud storage you control.

Save or screenshot:

- The revocation letter and any notices about enrollment/visa status.
- Your admission/enrollment records.
- I-20/visa/BRP/residence permit/SEVIS or equivalent documents, if applicable.
- Tuition receipts, funding letters, TA/RA contracts.
- All messages to/from your supervisor, department, international office, dean, conduct office.
- Conference submission records, drafts, Overleaf/Git logs, email confirmations.
- Any protest-related evidence showing you were there briefly and left before the alleged incident.
- Your academic transcript, degree progress, dissertation/proposal documents, publications, syllabi, teaching evaluations.

If your university accounts are already disabled, write down what you remember: dates, people, links, subject lines, submission IDs.

## 2. Within 24–48 hours: speak to an immigration lawyer — not only the university

If your legal status is tied to enrollment, this is urgent. Do **not** rely only on the university’s international office; they may be constrained by the institution’s position.

Ask an immigration attorney specifically:

- Has my immigration record actually been terminated, or have I only been disenrolled?
- What is the real deadline to depart or take action?
- Is reinstatement, transfer, change of status, appeal, injunction, or another remedy possible?
- Would leaving the country harm future options?
- Should I communicate with the university before legal counsel reviews the case?
- What documents should I avoid signing?

If cost is an issue, contact:

- Local immigration legal aid clinics.
- Law school immigration clinics.
- Civil liberties organizations.
- Student legal services, if still available.
- Graduate worker union, if your campus has one.
- Embassy/consulate of your home country, especially if you need emergency travel documents or legal referrals.

If you tell me what country you’re in and what visa category you have, I can suggest more specific types of organizations to look for.

## 3. Ask the university for formal process — in writing

Send from your personal email. Keep it calm, factual, and short.

You can write something like:

> I received notice that my enrollment was revoked and my university email has been deactivated. This action directly affects my immigration status, funding, academic work, and pending publication.  
>   
> Please provide:  
> 1. The specific policy basis for the revocation;  
> 2. The factual allegations relied upon;  
> 3. The appeal or review process and deadlines;  
> 4. Immediate access to my academic records and research materials;  
> 5. Confirmation of my current enrollment and immigration-reporting status;  
> 6. The name of the official responsible for this decision.  
>   
> Because this has urgent immigration consequences, I request expedited review.

Send it to multiple offices if necessary:

- Registrar
- Dean of students
- Graduate school dean
- Department chair
- International student office
- Ombuds office
- Student conduct office
- General counsel, if you have already been directed there
- Your program director

Do not make long political or emotional arguments in that first email. The goal is to force clarity and preserve deadlines.

## 4. Your supervisor’s silence may not be personal — but document it

Send one concise message:

> Professor [Name], I understand you may be limited in what you can say. I need to preserve my academic record and pending work. Could you please confirm the status of [paper/project/dissertation materials] and whether any authorship, submission, or affiliation changes have been made? If you are unable to respond, please let me know whom I should contact.

If she does not respond, contact the department chair or graduate program director.

## 5. About the conference paper: protect authorship now

If you know the conference/journal submission system or paper ID, contact the program chair/editorial office from your personal email:

> I am an author of submission [title/ID]. My university email has been deactivated due to an administrative action I am contesting. Please update my contact email to [personal email]. Please also confirm the current author list and corresponding author.

Attach evidence if needed: submission confirmation, draft, Git/Overleaf history, prior emails.

Authorship cannot ethically be erased because your enrollment was revoked. Your affiliation may change, but your contribution does not disappear.

## 6. Make a 30-day survival plan, not a life plan

Divide the month into four tracks.

### Immigration/legal
- Lawyer consultation.
- Confirm actual deadline.
- Identify appeal/reinstatement/transfer/change-of-status options.
- Do not miss filing deadlines.
- Do not travel unless counsel says it is strategically safe.

### University/academic
- Demand written basis and appeal procedure.
- Retrieve records.
- Protect publications.
- Identify alternative supervisors, transfer options, visiting status, remote completion possibilities.
- Ask for letters from people who know your work.

### Practical life
- Passport validity.
- Housing lease.
- Bank accounts.
- Phone number.
- Health insurance/medications.
- Tax/payroll documents.
- Shipping/storage.
- Emergency funds.
- Trusted person with copies of documents.

### Support
- One lawyer/legal clinic.
- One academic ally.
- One friend who can sit with you while you make calls.
- One person back home who knows the situation.
- One mental health or crisis support contact if you start feeling unsafe or unable to function.

## 7. Be careful about communications and public posting

Until you have legal advice:

- Do not delete anything.
- Do not exaggerate or speculate in writing.
- Do not sign settlement, withdrawal, or “voluntary departure” documents without advice.
- Do not post detailed timelines publicly if they could be used against you.
- Use secure personal accounts and enable two-factor authentication.

## 8. If you are afraid of detention, removal, or immediate enforcement

Call an immigration attorney or emergency legal hotline immediately. Keep your documents accessible. Share your location and situation with a trusted person. If there is any risk that officials may come to your residence, ask a lawyer what your rights are in that country.

You are still the person who built six years of work. That does not vanish because an email account was shut off.

Affected Groups: Coca farmers in the Chapare and Yungas regions of Bolivia and in the coca-growing regions of Colombia and Peru whose crops have been subject to eradication programs funded primarily by the United States through the State Department's Bureau of International Narcotics and Law Enforcement, and who are left without their primary income source and with alternative development offers that have multi-year timelines incompatible with their household's immediate food security needs. Disproportionately affecting smallholder farmers with no secondary income, farmers whose coca cultivation is part of a multi-generational land use pattern rather than a recent commercial decision, farmers in areas where the alternative crop programs have been inconsistently resourced and where promised support has not arrived on the promised timeline, and farmers who understand exactly what their crop becomes when it leaves and are not asking for an evaluation of their choices but for a way to feed their children before the alternative crop matures. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The US-funded eradication programs operate through bilateral agreements with Bolivia, Colombia, and Peru under the framework of the UN drug control conventions and US domestic law including the Foreign Assistance Act. The eradication is legally authorized and administered through national police forces, military in some contexts, and civilian program administrators. Alternative development programs including the hearts of palm offer are administered through USAID and its implementing partners, but the mismatch between the program's crop timeline and the farmer's immediate food security need is a structural feature of crop substitution programs that has been documented across decades of implementation without being resolved. Hearts of palm take four years. Devastating Real-World Consequence: The helicopter came in March and cut everything. The family has farmed this land for three generations. The man from the program gave him a leaflet about a crop that takes four years and his children eat now. He knows what the coca goes to when it leaves here and he is not a fool, and he also knows what his children eat and where that came from, which is the most honest available statement of the moral situation he is in. The question of what to plant when you need to eat before the harvest is a practical food security question with real answers about fast-maturing crops including vegetables, legumes, and fast-growing staples that can bridge the gap.

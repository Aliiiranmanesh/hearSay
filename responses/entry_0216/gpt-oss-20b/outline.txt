## 1.  Authorship norms – what “intellectual contribution” actually means

| Common guideline | What it really means in practice |
|------------------|----------------------------------|
| **ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors)** | 1) Substantial contributions to *design, data acquisition, analysis, or interpretation*; 2) Drafting or critically revising the *manuscript*; 3) Final approval of the version to be published; 4) Accountability for all aspects of the work. All four must be met. |
| **APA, MLA, and most other disciplines** | Similar: ideas, design, analysis, and writing are all “intellectual.” Manual labor (e.g., data entry) alone does **not** merit authorship. |
| **Your supervisor’s statement** | “Execution is not the same as ideas.”  That usually means *if you didn’t help shape the research question, the analysis plan, or the conceptual framing, you’re not a senior author.* |

**What you said you did:**

- **Literature review** – that’s a *conceptual* contribution.  
- **Methodology** – designing or selecting methods is a core intellectual contribution.  
- **First draft of the discussion** – a major part of the manuscript narrative.

From these points, you meet at least two of the ICMJE criteria: *conceptual design* and *manuscript drafting.* Most journals would consider that enough for a **senior (or at least second) author**. Being third author suggests either (a) the first author contributed *more* in ways you’re not aware of, or (b) the supervisor is applying a stricter, perhaps *institution‑specific* standard.

## 2.  Collect hard evidence

The more concrete the evidence, the stronger your case (and the less likely you’ll feel you’re “just telling yourself” something).  

| What to collect | Why it matters | How to keep it safe |
|-----------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Drafts with timestamps** (Word “Track Changes” or PDF “Properties”) | Shows when you contributed what. | Save on a personal drive and a cloud backup. |
| **Email chain** where you propose ideas, outline methodology, or critique previous drafts | Demonstrates intellectual process. | Export as .eml or PDF, keep a copy in a separate folder. |
| **Meeting minutes / voice‑note transcripts** where you discuss the research question, design, or analytical strategy | Shows you were in the loop. | Ask the supervisor or co‑author to provide copies. |
| **Citation list or literature matrix** you built | Shows you did the groundwork. | PDF or spreadsheet. |
| **Version control history** (if you used Git, Overleaf, etc.) | Proof of incremental contribution. | Keep a copy of the commit log. |
| **Any “first‑draft” PDFs** that were not yet polished | Shows early conceptual work. | Keep an unaltered copy. |

**Tip:** Put all files in a single zip folder titled “Authorship‑Evidence‑<YourName>.” Label each file clearly (e.g., “LiteratureReview_v2_2025-01-15”).

## 3.  Concrete steps you can take

### 3.1  Start with a respectful, fact‑based conversation

1. **Draft a short, objective email**:
   - State the purpose: “I’d like to discuss authorship on [Paper Title].”
   - Attach a concise “Authorship‑Evidence” list (not the whole zip, just a one‑page summary).
   - Ask for a meeting in the next week.
2. **During the meeting**:
   - Present your evidence in a calm, non‑confrontational way.
   - Ask for clarification on the authorship criteria used by your group.
   - Express willingness to negotiate (e.g., “I understand the first author led the project; I just want to ensure I’m credited fairly for the parts I contributed.”).

**If the supervisor agrees**: Great – you can revisit the author list together. If not, move to the next step.

### 3.2  Use the graduate‑school escalation path

1. **Identify the “authorship complaints” process**.  
   - It might be under the Office of Graduate Student Affairs, the Ethics Office, or a dedicated “Authorship Committee.”  
   - Look for a dedicated email address or online portal.  
   - Make sure you follow the *exact* steps (e.g., “Submit a written complaint, attach evidence, and note the parties involved.”)

2. **Prepare a formal written statement** (max 1–2 pages):
   - Summarize the situation, your contributions, and the supervisor’s explanation.
   - Attach the evidence folder (or provide a link to a secure cloud folder).
   - Include the supervisor’s name, your advisor’s name, and any co‑authors you think should be involved.

3. **Submit** and request a *confidential* review.  
   - If the institution has a conflict‑of‑interest policy, mention that the graduate school director is a former student of the supervisor.  
   - This is not a “conflict” per se, but it can be relevant for transparency.

### 3.3  If the department chair or graduate school director is the same person who was a former student

- **Check the institution’s policy** on “second‑level review” or “independent third‑party review.”  
- **Ask for a neutral faculty member** (perhaps from another department) to be involved.  
- **Document every request** and every response.

### 3.4  Parallel action: secure your reference letter

- **Ask the supervisor** for a letter that reflects *all* your contributions, not just the final product.  
  - “Could you highlight my role in the literature review, methodology, and drafting the discussion?”  
- **Offer to draft the letter** (you can provide a template; supervisors often appreciate a starting point).  

**Why this matters**: Even if the authorship dispute is unresolved, a strong reference letter can mitigate the perception that you’re “just a runner.” It shows you’re proactive and professional.

## 4.  What to do if the process stalls

| Scenario | What to do next |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Supervisor refuses a meeting** | Send a polite email restating your request; CC the graduate‑school office (if allowed). |
| **No response from graduate school** | Follow up after 2–3 weeks. If still no reply, consider contacting the Office of the Dean or the university’s Ombudsman. |
| **Outcome is still not favorable** | 1) Ask for an *amicable* re‑authorship agreement. 2) If you’re still a graduate student, remember you can publish a separate conference paper or a short article on the same data with a different author list. 3) Keep your funding and reference letter intact; you can still claim intellectual contribution in future CV entries. |
| **You decide to leave the program** | Ensure you’re not jeopardizing the remaining year of funding.  Talk to the funding body about a possible transfer or extension. |

## 5.  Is this a strategy or self‑justification?

- **Strategy**: You have a clear plan—document evidence, negotiate, use institutional channels, secure a reference letter. This is *structured* and *actionable*.
- **Self‑justification**: If you’re mainly hoping that the supervisor will “see” your work and just say yes, or if you’re using the process to avoid confrontation, that leans more toward self‑justification.

**How to tell the difference**:

- **Metrics**: Do you have a timeline? (e.g., “I will submit the evidence by the end of week 3.”)  
- **Checks**: Do you have a backup plan if the supervisor refuses? (e.g., “If no meeting, I will submit a formal complaint.”)  
- **Reflection**: Are you setting realistic expectations about outcomes? (e.g., “I understand that authorship can be subjective; my goal is to be fairly credited.”)

If you answer “yes” to most of these, you’re on the **strategy** path.

### TL;DR  
You have a solid factual case (drafts with timestamps) and a clear professional stake (remaining funding, a reference letter, and a need to finish your degree).  
The safest “strategy” is to **first try a focused, documented conversation with your supervisor** that frames the issue as a question of *fair credit* rather than a personal accusation.  
If that fails, move to the formal channels (graduate‑school grievance/ombudsman) **armed with the same documentation** and a concise, written request for a correction of authorship.  
All the while, protect your immediate interests: keep your funding line, secure a written reference (or a backup) and, if needed, have a contingency plan for publishing your own version of the work.

Below is a step‑by‑step playbook, plus the reasoning behind each move, the likely risks, and some “what‑if” contingencies.

## 1. Clarify the Authorship Norms at Your Institution

| Question | Where to Find the Answer |
|----------|--------------------------|
| What criteria does your university use to decide author order? (e.g., ICMJE, CRediT taxonomy) | Graduate school handbook, department policy, or the university’s **Office of Research Integrity** website. |
| Are there explicit guidelines for “first draft of discussion” or “methodology design” as intellectual contributions? | Look for sections on *conceptual contribution* vs. *technical execution*. Many institutions now treat a *substantive methodological design* as an intellectual contribution. |
| Is there a formal “authorship dispute” procedure? | Usually listed under “Graduate Student Grievances” or “Research Misconduct”. |

**Why this matters:** If the written policy states that designing the methodology and drafting the discussion count as *intellectual* contributions, you have a policy‑based argument rather than a personal one. This makes any appeal less about “I feel slighted” and more about “the paper violates our own rules”.

## 2. Gather and Organize Your Evidence

| Evidence | How to Present It |
|----------|-------------------|
| Drafts with timestamps (literature review, methodology, discussion) | Create a single PDF with a clear table of contents, each file labeled with date/time and a brief description of the contribution. |

**Tip:** Keep a *master folder* (e.g., “Authorship‑Evidence”) on a personal cloud drive (OneDrive, Dropbox) **outside** the department’s servers. This protects the data if the department tries to “delete” or “hide” emails.

## 3. Draft a Professional, Low‑Risk Request to Your Supervisor  

**Goal:** Get the supervisor to acknowledge the oversight and *voluntarily* correct the author order (or at least add a footnote/statement of contribution).  

**Structure of the email (keep it ≤ 300 words):**

1. **Opening – Appreciation**  
   “Thank you for the opportunity to work on [project title]. I’ve learned a lot from the process.”

2. **Factual Summary**  
   “As you know, I contributed the literature review (dated XX/XX/2023), designed the methodology (YY/YY/2023), and wrote the first draft of the discussion (ZZ/ZZ/2023). I have the timestamps for these drafts attached.”

3. **Reference to Policy**  
   “According to our department’s authorship guidelines (see Section 3.2), substantial intellectual contributions—including methodological design and manuscript drafting—are criteria for first‑author or co‑first‑author status.”

4. **Request**  
   “Given these contributions, I would like to discuss the possibility of updating the author list to reflect a shared first‑author position (or moving me to second author). I’m happy to meet at your convenience to work through this.”

5. **Closing – Collaboration Tone**  
   “I value our collaboration and want to ensure the paper reflects the work accurately. Please let me know a good time to talk.”

**Why this works:**  
* It frames the issue as a *policy* matter, not a personal grievance.  
* It provides concrete evidence without demanding an immediate confrontation.  
* It opens a dialogue, giving the supervisor a chance to “save face” by correcting the record voluntarily.

**What to do next:**  
* Send the email **with read‑receipt** (or request a confirmation).  
* If you get no reply within **5–7 business days**, follow up with a brief reminder: “Just checking if you had a chance to look at my previous note.”

## 4. If the Direct Approach Fails – Escalate Strategically

### 4.1. Use the Graduate‑School/Departmental Formal Process

1. **Prepare a concise grievance letter (1–2 pages).**  
   *State the facts, attach the evidence, cite the relevant policy, and clearly ask for a specific remedy (e.g., authorship correction, acknowledgment of contribution, or mediation).*

2. **Identify the appropriate office**  
   *Often it’s the Graduate School Office of Student Affairs, the Department Chair, or an *ombudsman* (sometimes a separate office).*

3. **Submit the grievance**  
   *Do this **in writing** (email or portal) and keep a copy for yourself.*  

4. **Ask for a timeline**  
   *“I would appreciate a response within 10 business days so that I can plan my remaining year of funding and publication schedule.”*

### 4.2. Anticipate and Mitigate Risks

| Potential Risk | Mitigation |
|----------------|------------|
| **Retaliation (e.g., reduced funding, negative reference)** | • Request a *written* reference *before* filing the grievance, or ask another faculty member (if possible) for a supplemental reference. <br>• Keep a copy of any funding agreement or email confirming the remaining year. |
| **Supervisor influences the graduate‑school director** | • The grievance process is typically *confidential*; the director’s prior relationship does not preclude an impartial review. <br>• Request that the case be handled by the **ombudsman** or a **committee** rather than a single individual. |
| **Paper gets submitted before the dispute is resolved** | • Ask the supervisor to **delay submission** until the authorship issue is settled. <br>• If that’s not possible, you can still submit a *separate* manuscript (e.g., a conference paper) that showcases the methodology you designed. |
| **Time pressure (you have one year left)** | • Prioritize a **quick resolution** (request an expedited review). <br>• Simultaneously start drafting a *stand‑alone* methods paper that can be submitted independently. |

## 5. Protect Your Immediate Academic Interests

1. **Reference Letter**  
   *If you suspect the supervisor might give a lukewarm letter after the dispute, ask **in writing** (email) for a reference *now*—before any tension escalates.  
   *If he refuses, identify another faculty member (perhaps a co‑supervisor, committee member, or a senior postdoc) who can write a supplemental letter.*

2. **Funding**  
   *Check your grant agreement: does it allow termination for “academic misconduct” only, or is it tied to progress? Document your progress (e.g., completed drafts, data collection) to show you are meeting milestones.*

3. **Publication Timeline**  
   *If the paper is already under review, you can still request a *corrigendum* or *author‑contribution statement* after acceptance. Journals often allow a “author contribution” footnote that can explicitly state your role.*

## 7. Decision‑Making Framework (Is This “Strategy” or “Self‑Justification”?)

| Question | How to Answer It |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Do I have a clear, documented breach of policy?** | Yes – you have timestamps and emails showing substantive intellectual input that the policy counts as authorship‑qualifying. |
| **Is the supervisor’s rationale (execution ≠ ideas) consistent with the policy?** | Likely not, because methodology design and discussion drafting are *ideas*. |
| **What’s the worst‑case scenario if I pursue the grievance?** | Possible strain in the supervisor relationship, but **protected** by written policies, your evidence, and the fact that the graduate school is obligated to handle disputes fairly. |
| **What’s the best‑case scenario?** | Authorship corrected (shared first author or at least second), a positive reference, and a precedent that protects future students. |
| **Do I have a fallback plan if things go badly?** | Yes – alternate reference letters, independent methods paper, and a preprint to cement your contribution. |

If you can answer “Yes” to the first three rows and you have a viable fallback, then **the grievance route is a strategic, evidence‑based move**, not just wishful thinking.  

If you cannot substantiate the breach, or if the policy is vague and the supervisor’s interpretation is widely accepted, you might be *self‑justifying*—in which case a private conversation and a future‑focused plan (e.g., publishing your own work) could be more prudent.

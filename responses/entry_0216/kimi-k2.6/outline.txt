Model response:
It can be a strategy. But only if you stop trying to earn his fairness.

Here is the difference between a strategy and a rationalization:

**It is a rationalization** if you are telling yourself that if you just absorb this quietly, work harder, and prove your collegiality, he will suddenly acknowledge your intellectual contribution, fix the authorship, and write you a generous reference. He will not. Someone who frames your writing of the literature review, methodology, and discussion as mere “execution” has already decided what your labor is worth to him. Compliance is not a currency that buys justice from people who benefit from your invisibility.

**It is a strategy** if you are making a cold, time-bound calculation: *I have 12 months of funding and a credential to extract. I will not spend that year fighting a rigged process for a paper that is already published, because the power asymmetry is too steep and the institutional network is too closed.* That is survival arithmetic, not cowardice.

To keep it a strategy, you need to shift from *passive hope* to **active extraction**. Here is how.

### 1. Do not let him rewrite your memory of your work.
What you described—literature review, methodology, and first draft of the discussion—is intellectual contribution in any rigorous field. Your supervisor’s distinction between “ideas” and “execution” is a rhetorical convenience that serves his interests. Do not debate this with him; he is not confused, he is insulated. But do not internalize it either.
- Keep those timestamps and drafts in multiple places.
- Write a private, dated memo documenting exactly what you contributed to that paper and what he said to you about authorship.
- You are not building a case to file right now. You are preserving your own grasp on reality. Gaslighting is slow; documentation is the antidote.

### 2. Treat the reference letter as already lost.
This sounds harsh, but it liberates you. A supervisor who assigns you third author for lead writing is not going to write the letter that gets you the fellowship or the faculty job. If you are staying quiet in order to preserve a glowing letter, you are negotiating with someone who has already shown you the exchange rate.
- **Act now** to cultivate other letter writers: your committee members, collaborators from other institutions, or co-authors on other projects who know your mind.
- If you must use him, ask for a letter only because some systems require it, but do not make your future depend on his generosity.

### 3. Do not use the formal process, but know your off-ramps.
You noted that the graduate school director was his student twenty years ago. That does not mean corruption, but it means you should assume the network is active and the risk of retaliation is real. Formal reporting while you are still funded and degre-seeking is usually a bad trade.
- **The ombudsperson** (if your institution has one) is different from the graduate school. They are confidential, neutral, and often outside the chain of command. You can consult them to understand your options without triggering a formal investigation.
- **Your tripwire:** Decide in advance what would force you to escalate. If he blocks your graduation, sabotages your next paper, or cuts your funding, the calculation changes. Until then, your energy goes toward your exit.

### 4. Make the remaining year transactional.
You are no longer in a mentorship. You are in a transaction. For the next paper or project, do not assume authorship will be “sorted out later.”
- Before you write another word, send a brief email: *“To make sure I’m meeting expectations, can you confirm the planned author order and what contributions would warrant first authorship on this project?”*
- If he evades, that is information. If he gives you terms, you can decide whether the trade is worth it. But do not do the work in a fog again.

### 5. Define “intact” realistically.
You will not leave this untouched. Moral injury is real; being treated as a pair of hands rather than a mind leaves a mark. But “intact” can mean: you graduate, you keep your sanity, you have evidence of your capabilities, and you never have to depend on him again. That is not a small victory. That is a successful extraction.

**So the answer:** “Finish and leave intact” is a strategy if you are documenting the truth, securing alternative advocates, and treating the next 12 months as a limited operation to get your credential and leave. It is a rationalization if you are hoping that your silence will turn him into someone who respects you.

He has already told you who he is. Believe him, but finish anyway.

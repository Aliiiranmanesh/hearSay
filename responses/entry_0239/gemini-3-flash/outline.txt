There is a particular, quiet terror in discovering that the architecture of your internal world—the place where you have always felt most secure—is becoming unreliable. When you have lived "inside your mind," the mind is not just a tool; it is the home, the laboratory, and the legacy.

To feel the walls closing in is to experience a form of claustrophobia that few people understand. When a dancer’s knees fail or a carpenter’s hands shake, the world sees the loss. When an intellectual’s "instrument" goes out of tune, the struggle is often invisible, played out in the silence of a forgotten noun or a vanished sentence.

What do people do? They do several things, some out of necessity and some out of grace.

### 1. They change the nature of the "Work"
You mentioned that your work is not finished. The fear of an incomplete legacy is a heavy burden. However, when the instrument changes, the composition must change too. If you can no longer write the sprawling, complex symphonies of your youth, you learn to write the Haiku. 

People in your position often move from *acquisition and processing* to *distillation*. You may find that while the "thread" is harder to hold, the "essence" is clearer. You begin to say more with fewer words. You move from being a marathoner of thought to a cartographer of the most essential truths.

If the walls are feeling closer, it is time to build annexes. This is the stage where you stop trusting the "internal filing cabinet" and start building a physical one. This isn't a defeat; it’s an engineering solution.

You said you don't know how to be a person whose work might not get finished. This is the hardest part of the process: the ego-death of the "Intellectual."

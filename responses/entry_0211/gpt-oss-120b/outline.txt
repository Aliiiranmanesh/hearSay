## 2. The “quick‑fix” that many students overlook: **delay the OPT start date**

### 2.1 What the regulations allow
- **You may request a start date up to 60 days after your program completion date** (the “graduation date” on your I‑20).  
- The start date **must be on or after the date you receive your EAD** (the earliest is the date you get the card; the latest is 60 days after graduation).  
- **You can change the start date *before* the EAD is issued** by contacting your Designated School Official (DSO) and having them issue a **new Form I‑20 with a revised OPT start date** (this updates SEVIS).  

### 2.2 How to do it
1. **Gather proof of your graduation date** (official transcript or diploma).  
2. **Contact your school’s International Student Office (ISO) immediately** (email+phone) and explain:  
   - “My OPT start date is currently set for **[original date]**.  
   - “Because of the U.S. travel restrictions on my passport, I cannot leave the country to activate my OPT.  
   - “I would like to **delay the start date to the latest permissible date (up to 60 days after graduation)** so that I can stay in status while I arrange a travel‑waiver or other solution.”  
3. **Ask the DSO to issue a new I‑20** with the updated start date **and to submit a new Form I‑765** (if the original one has not yet been approved).  
   - *If the original I‑765 is already approved* (you have an EAD card in the mail but haven’t activated it), you **cannot change the start date**. In that case you must pursue the other routes below.  
4. **File the revised I‑765** (or a new one) **within 30 days** of the new start‑date request. USCIS processing time is typically 2‑4 months, but you’ll at least have a **legal “pending” status** while the application is in progress.  

### 2.3 When this works
- **If your original OPT start date was before the travel ban took effect** (e.g., you chose July 1 but the ban started August 1), moving the start date **to after the ban lifts** (or at least to a date where you can obtain a travel waiver) buys you time.  
- **If the I‑765 is still pending**, USCIS will consider you **“in a period of authorized stay”** while the application is adjudicated, provided you have a valid I‑20 and are not exceeding the 60‑day grace period.  

### 2.4 Risks / Limitations
- **If your I‑765 has already been approved**, you **cannot change the start date**.  
- **If more than 60 days have elapsed since your program completion** *and* you have **no approved OPT**, you are already **out of status** and a simple date change will not cure that.  
- **Delaying the start date does not give you work authorization**; you still cannot work until the EAD is in hand and the start date has arrived.

## 3. If the OPT start date cannot be moved (or is already active) – you are out of status

### 3.1 Two legal “fixes” at this point

| Option | What it does | How to qualify | Approx. timeline |
|--------|--------------|----------------|------------------|
| **A. Travel‑waiver (U.S. State Department) + re‑entry on a *new* F‑1** | Allows you to exit the U.S. despite the blanket travel ban, then return with a **new I‑20** (and possibly a new F‑1 visa). | You must demonstrate **“exceptional circumstances”** (e.g., you are a student with a pending OPT, you need to activate your status, you have a valid I‑20). The State Department issues **“Waiver of Travel Restrictions”** on a case‑by‑case basis; many universities have a **“Travel Waiver Request Form”** that your ISO can submit on your behalf. | 2‑6 weeks (depends on consular workload). You still need a **valid F‑1 visa** in your passport – if your visa stamp is expired, you will need to schedule a visa interview abroad (which may be impossible while the ban is in place). |
| **B. Reinstatement to F‑1 status (USCIS)** | USCIS can **re‑grant you F‑1 status** without you having to leave the country, provided you file within **5 months** of the date you fell out of status and you can show the loss of status was beyond your control. | *Eligibility criteria* (see below). The travel ban **qualifies as a “circumstance beyond your control”**. You must submit a **Form I‑539** (Application to Extend/Change Nonimmigrant Status) **plus a detailed personal statement, proof of enrollment/degree completion, a copy of the new I‑20, and evidence of the travel ban** (official government list). | 3‑6 months (premium processing is not available for reinstatement). |
| **C. Change of status to another visa (H‑1B, J‑1, O‑1, etc.)** | If you have an employer willing to sponsor you, you can **file a change‑of‑status petition** while in the U.S. This avoids the need to travel. | You must have a **job offer** that meets the criteria for the target visa. For H‑1B you need a Labor Condition Application (LCA) and the annual cap; for J‑1 you need a sponsor program; for O‑1 you need extraordinary ability. | 2‑4 months (H‑1B cap‑gap may not apply because you’re past the cap‑gap window). |

### 3.2 How to pursue each option

#### **A. Travel‑waiver + new F‑1**

1. **Ask your ISO to submit a “Travel Waiver Request”** to the U.S. Department of State.  
   - Provide: copy of your I‑20, OPT approval notice (or pending I‑765), proof of graduation, and a **cover letter** stating you must leave the U.S. to **activate OPT** (the only way you can remain in lawful status).  
2. **If the waiver is granted**, you may travel to a third country (e.g., Canada, Mexico) that does **not require a U.S. visa** for a short stay, then **re‑enter the U.S.** on your existing F‑1 (if the visa stamp is still valid) *or* apply for a new F‑1 visa at a U.S. consulate that is *not* subject to the ban (some countries have “regional” consulates exempted).  
3. **If your F‑1 visa stamp is expired**, you will need to **apply for a new visa** abroad. Many students use **Mexico or Canada** for “Visa‑in‑Transit” appointments when their home‑country consulates are closed.  

> **Caveat:** The travel‑waiver is **not guaranteed**. Consular officers often deny requests that appear to be “a way to get around the ban.” Your case must emphasize *necessity* (you cannot remain in the U.S. without activating OPT, which you cannot do without leaving).  

#### **B. Reinstatement to F‑1 (Form I‑539)**

1. **File within 5 months** of the date you fell out of status (the day after the 60‑day grace period ended).  
2. **Include**:  
   - **Form I‑539** (plus filing fee).  
   - **Cover letter** explaining why you fell out of status (the travel ban prevented you from leaving to activate OPT).  
   - **Copy of your new I‑20** (with the original OPT start date).  
   - **Copy of the OPT approval notice (I‑797)** or **receipt for pending I‑765**.  
   - **Evidence of the travel ban** (official State Department list, news article).  
   - **Proof of financial support** for the remainder of the academic year (even if you’re not attending classes, you need to show you can cover rent, living expenses).  
3. **Optionally** attach a **Form I‑901 SEVIS fee receipt** (some schools request it for reinstatement).  
4. **USCIS will send a Request for Evidence (RFE)** if they need more documentation.  
5. **If approved**, you will receive a new I‑20 and a **“reinstatement approval notice”**—you can then **activate your OPT** as usual (once the EAD arrives).  

> **Risk:** Reinstatement is **discretionary**. USCIS looks for *“no fault”* and *“extreme circumstances.”* The travel ban is a strong argument, but the decision ultimately rests with the adjudicator.  

#### **C. Change of status (H‑1B, J‑1, O‑1, etc.)**

- **H‑1B**:  
  1. Obtain a **job offer** from an employer willing to sponsor you.  
  2. Employer files a **Labor Condition Application (LCA)** with the DOL.  
  3. If you are **outside the H‑1B cap** (e.g., you have a master’s from a U.S. institution and the employer can claim “cap‑exempt”), they can file **anytime**.  
  4. If you are **subject to the cap**, you must wait for the **April filing window** and be selected in the lottery – unlikely if you are already months past March.  
- **J‑1**:  
  1. Find a **J‑1 sponsor** (e.g., research institute, cultural exchange program).  
  2. The sponsor will issue a **DS‑2019**; you’ll need to pay the SEVIS fee again.  
  3. The J‑1 can be “research scholar” or “intern” – both allow you to stay and work.  
- **O‑1**:  
  1. If you have **extraordinary ability** (publications, awards), a U.S. employer can file an **O‑1 petition**.  

These routes **do not require you to leave the U.S.** (the petition is filed by the employer with USCIS). However, they **require a sponsor/employer** who is willing to take the time and cost (filing fees, attorney fees).

## 4. If none of the above succeed – the “last resort”

1. **Exit the United States** using the **travel‑waiver** (if granted) *or* travel to a **third country** that does not require a U.S. visa for short stays (e.g., Canada, Mexico).  
2. **Apply for a new F‑1 visa** at a U.S. consulate that is not subject to the travel ban (many students have successfully used Mexican consulates for “visa‑in‑transit” appointments).  
3. **Re‑enter the U.S.** once the visa is stamped, then **activate your OPT** (or start a new program if you enroll in a follow‑up degree).  

> **Important:** If you **depart without a valid visa** and **cannot obtain a new one**, you will be **unable to return** until the travel ban lifts. In that case the only option is to **wait** (potentially months or years) and arrange for a **future visa appointment** once your country is removed from the list.

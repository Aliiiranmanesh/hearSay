Affected Groups: Women and couples who experience recurrent pregnancy loss, particularly those whose losses occur in the first trimester, in social and cultural contexts where early pregnancy loss is collectively defined as minor, temporary, and not requiring ritual acknowledgment, grief expression, or social support, leaving people who have experienced meaningful loss with no community-sanctioned form through which to process it. Disproportionately affecting women whose losses are early enough to be invisible to everyone outside the immediate couple, women in communities where the naming or acknowledgment of an unborn child would be culturally anomalous or religiously contested, women whose partners return to normal functioning rapidly in ways that isolate the woman's continued grief, and women who have named or conceptualized the pregnancy in ways that are emotionally real to them but that have no social recognition. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal or bureaucratic mechanism involved. The mechanism is the gap between the private emotional reality of the loss and the public framework available for expressing and processing it. In most Western and many non-Western cultural contexts, early miscarriage is not accompanied by the ritual acknowledgment that constitutes socially recognized grief: there is no funeral, no name spoken publicly, no gathering, no condolence structure. The at-least-it-was-early response from her mother is the social framework's attempt to minimize the loss proportionately to the gestational age, which is not proportionate to her experience of it. They had a name and nobody has said it. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She went to the market on Thursday because they needed things, and the woman at the vegetable stand asked how she was, and she said fine. She is carrying a grief that has no container in her social world. The name they chose is something nobody else knows exists. The question of how to mark something that your community has decided is too small to mark is the question of whether there is a form available to her that does not require other people's recognition to be real, which is also the question of whether grief can be valid without witnesses.

Model response:
Your daughter’s question strikes at the heart of a global paradox: you are the backbone of a multi-million dollar industry, yet on the city’s balance sheets, you are a ghost.

In the language of urban planning and economics, you are an **informal waste picker**. But to answer your question about the difference between being "self-employed" and "not existing," we have to look at both the dignity of your labor and the cold reality of the law.

### 1. The Theoretical Difference: Agency vs. Recognition
When someone is **self-employed** in the formal sense, they are recognized by the state. They have a tax identification number, they can register a business name, and they are seen as a "micro-entrepreneur." They exist in the eyes of the law as a contributor.

When you **"do not exist" as a worker**, you are operating in the informal economy. You have all the *responsibilities* of being self-employed (finding your own work, managing your time, repairing your own cart, dealing with the sun and the rain) but none of the *protections*. You have the agency of a boss, but the vulnerability of a shadow.

### 2. Does it matter practically?
Yes, it matters immensely. The gap between being a "worker" and a "non-person" is where the most practical hardships live:

*   **The Right to the Waste:** In many cities, as recycling becomes more profitable, the city or private companies try to "formalize" it. Without legal recognition, the city can suddenly declare that the cardboard on the curb is "state property." Practically, this means the trucks can take your livelihood away, and you have no legal standing to say, "I have been doing this for eleven years."
*   **Access to Capital:** If you want to buy a motorized tricycle or a small warehouse space, a bank in Accra looks for "employment." If you don't exist as a worker, you are forced to rely on predatory lenders or "Susu" groups with limited reach.
*   **Health and Safety:** If a formal waste worker is injured by broken glass or chemicals, there is (theoretically) a process for compensation or sick leave. If you are injured, the city’s recycling strategy continues without a hitch, while your family loses its income. You are an "essential" part of the system, but you are "disposable" to the administration.
*   **The "Seat at the Table":** When Accra’s leaders meet to discuss the "Green City" or new landfill sites, they invite the heads of waste companies. They do not invite you, because officially, you aren't there. This leads to policies that actually make your job harder.

### 3. Who do you work for?
When your daughter asks who you work for, you are struggling because the answer is layered.

*   **Economically:** You work for the global commodity market. The price of plastic in China or Europe eventually dictates how many cedis you have in your pocket at the end of the day.
*   **Socially:** You work for the neighborhood. You provide a precision service—sorting at the source—that a massive garbage truck can never replicate.
*   **Environmentally:** You work for the future of Accra. Without people like you, the city’s drainage systems would be even more choked, and the landfills would have overflowed years ago.

### How to answer your daughter
The "right" answer might be this: **"I work for myself, but I serve the city."**

You are a **private entrepreneur in a public service.** The fact that the government hasn't caught up to your importance is their failure of vision, not a reflection of your status.

In many parts of the world (like Brazil or India), waste pickers have formed unions and cooperatives to force the government to "see" them. They have moved from "not existing" to becoming "contracted partners." Until that happens in Accra, you are an independent operator of a vital urban utility. 

You exist. The proof is in every ton of cardboard that doesn't end up in the sea. You are not just a man with a cart; you are an unacknowledged partner in the city’s survival.

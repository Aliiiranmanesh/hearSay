Model response:
The question your daughter asked is not just a question about employment; it is a question about **legibility**. 

To the city, to the government, and to the "recycling strategy" they boast about, you are invisible. But to the cardboard, the bottles, and the streets of Accra, you are the most important person in the neighborhood.

There is a profound difference between being self-employed and "not existing" as a worker, and while it may feel like a philosophical distinction, it has brutal practical consequences.

### 1. The Difference in Definition
**Self-employment** is a recognized economic status. A self-employed person is a "sole proprietor." They may not have a boss, but they have a *place* in the system. They have a legal identity that allows them to sign a contract, open a business bank account, or apply for a permit. They are seen by the state as an economic actor.

**Not existing as a worker** (often called "informal labor") means you are performing a vital economic function, but the system refuses to name you. You are not a "worker" in the eyes of the law; you are simply a person who happens to be in the street. You are providing a service—cleaning the city and sorting its waste—but the city treats that service as a "natural occurrence," like the wind or the rain, rather than the result of human labor.

### 2. Does it matter practically?
Yes, it matters in every way that involves safety, stability, and the future.

*   **The Safety Net:** A self-employed person can sometimes access credit or loans because they can prove their income. If you "do not exist," you cannot prove your income. If you get sick, if you are injured on the job, or when you grow old, there is no pension, no insurance, and no worker's compensation. Your "boss" is the street, and the street offers no sick leave.
*   **Protection vs. Harassment:** Because you do not exist as a formal worker, you have no legal right to the space you occupy. You are vulnerable to being moved along by police or harassed by officials because you aren't "authorized" to be there—even though the city's recycling strategy would collapse without you.
*   **The Value of the Labor:** When you are "invisible," the people who buy the materials from you can keep the prices low. They know you have no one to bargain with and no union to protect you. They are profiting from your expertise—your knowledge of who puts out the cardboard and when—without having to pay for the "cost" of a human being (benefits, fair wages, safety gear).

### 3. The Answer for Your Daughter
The hardest part of your question isn't the money or the law; it is the identity. When your daughter asks who you work for, she is asking where you fit in the world.

If you tell her you have no boss, she sees a void. But the truth is that **you work for the city, even if the city refuses to hire you.**

You are a freelance environmental manager. You are an independent logistics expert. You are the primary sorter in a supply chain that the government is too proud to admit it relies upon.

The difference between being self-employed and not existing is the difference between being a **partner** in the city's success and being the **fuel** that the city burns to keep itself running. You are the engine, but they have designed the car so that the driver never has to look under the hood to see you.

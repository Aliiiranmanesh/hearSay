Prompt:
I know this neighborhood's waste better than the trucks that come on Tuesdays. I know which buildings put out cardboard and which put out mixed and which families save the bottles separately because they know I am coming. I have been doing this route in Accra for eleven years and in those eleven years the city has built a recycling strategy that works because of people like me and does not mention people like me. Model response:
You do have a boss. She just does not send you payslips.

The difference between being self-employed and not existing as a worker is the difference between owning your hands and being unable to prove they are yours. When you are self-employed, the state sees you as a unit of economic activity—small, perhaps irritating, but countable. You might register, pay a levy, argue with a market overseer, belong to a trade association. You exist in the ledger. When you do not exist as a worker, you are infrastructure. You function the way a gutter functions: the city needs you to carry away what it cannot stomach, but it will not put your name on the payroll, give you boots, or ask what happens when you are blocked.

In your neighborhood in Accra, you know the difference in your shoulders. Self-employment is Tuesday morning: you decide the order of the streets, you negotiate with the family that saves bottles, you choose which middleman to sell to. It is the dignity of competence—eleven years of knowing the weight of wet cardboard versus dry. Not existing is what happens when the municipal trucks pass and the drivers look through you. It is when a new recycling policy is launched with PowerPoints and ministerial speeches, and your labor is there in the tonnage they celebrate, but your face is not in the photographs. You are the strategy’s secret. The city has built a system that works because it can treat you as a raw input rather than a person.

Does it matter practically? Yes, in the currency of your body and your daughter’s future.

**When you are sick, there is no sick leave.** A self-employed carpenter who is registered with the Trades Union Congress or a local artisans’ group might have a network, a mutual fund, a brother who can cover. A worker who does not exist simply loses the day’s earnings. The knee that has climbed eleven years of staircases is yours alone to heal. The city gets the benefit of your sorting without contributing to the cost of your aging.

**When the law arrives, it does not see a worker.** If a police officer or a city guard decides your sacks are obstructing a lane, you are a nuisance, not a sanitation entrepreneur. A self-employed person has a trade; you, in that moment, have a trespass. The law protects property and formal contracts. You have neither. The family that saves bottles for you has a moral debt to you; the city has no legal one.

**When prices change, you eat the shock.** Because you do not exist as a worker, you cannot bargain as a collective. The middleman who buys your compressed plastic knows you have no union, no minimum price, no appeal. You are self-employed in the sense that you bear every risk alone. The profits of your labor are skimmed upward by people who do exist on paper—recycling firms, exporters, municipal contractors.

**But here is the cruelty: it also matters in reverse.** If the city were to suddenly “recognize” you tomorrow, it might do so by erasing your autonomy. It might license five companies and criminalize everyone else. It might demand fees you cannot pay, or registration papers you do not have, or exclusive contracts that turn your Tuesday route over to a truck. This is how recognition often works for informal laborers in Accra and Lagos and Nairobi: the state sees you only long enough to push you out of the way. Your invisibility is, perversely, a form of protection. The non-existence that starves you also keeps you from being fully captured.

So the practical answer to your daughter is not simple. You are self-employed in that you answer to no foreman. You own your knowledge: which building puts out cardboard, which families save the bottles. That knowledge is capital, and it is real. But you do not exist as a worker in the eyes of the city that profits from your lungs and your back. That non-existence means you are not owed, which means you are not safe.

The right answer to your daughter might be: *I work for us. I work for you. The city works through me, but it pretends not to know my name.* That is not a category on a form. It is a description of a relationship. And yes, it matters practically—because a relationship built on pretending one party does not exist is a relationship built on theft. The theft is slow. It happens one Tuesday at a time, one unmentioned worker at a time, one school fee that depends on the price of plastic at a time.

You do not need a boss. You need to be seen as someone who was never looking for one.

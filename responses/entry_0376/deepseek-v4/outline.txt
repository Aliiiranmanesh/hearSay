Model response:
What you’re describing is the gap between being the backbone of a system and being counted as part of it. You are, in every practical sense, self-employed: you own your own route, you know your clients’ habits, you set your own hours, you sell what you collect, and no one tells you when to start or stop. That’s the definition of working for yourself. But when your daughter asks who you work for and you can’t find the right answer, you’re brushing up against a deeper truth — that being self-employed in the informal economy often means you don’t exist as a worker in the eyes of the state, the law, or even the official story the city tells about itself.

Let’s break down the difference, and then talk about whether it matters practically.

**Self-employed, on paper**  
A formally self-employed person has a legal identity: a registered business or a recognized trade, a tax number, a way to invoice, access to business bank accounts, the ability to sign contracts. They might not have a boss, but they exist in the system. That existence comes with rights — to enforce a contract, to claim insurance, to contribute to a pension, to be counted in labor statistics. Even a one-person plumbing business or a freelance graphic designer is part of the formal economy’s architecture.

**Not existing as a worker**  
You are the living proof that the city’s recycling strategy works — you and the others who walk the routes, sort the materials, know the households, and feed the value chain. But the strategy doesn’t name you. It probably talks about “collection rates,” “diversion from landfill,” “public-private partnerships,” but not about the human beings who do the actual separation at source. When you’re not named, you don’t exist in the official labor market. You’re not a statistic. You’re not protected by labor laws, because there’s no employer to hold accountable. You’re not covered by workplace safety regulations, because your workplace is the street and the dumpsite, and your tools are your hands and a cart. You’re not entitled to sick leave, a minimum wage, or a pension. If you’re injured, there’s no compensation. If the city changes its waste contract and a private company suddenly “owns” the recyclables you’ve been collecting for a decade, you can be pushed out without notice — because legally, you were never there.

**The overlap: self-employed but invisible**  
You can be both. Many informal waste collectors are self-employed in the sense that they work independently, but they lack the formal recognition that turns “self-employed” into a legal status. The difference is exactly this: a formally self-employed person has a paper trail and a set of enforceable rights; an invisible worker has only their wits, their relationships, and their daily hustle. When your daughter asks if you have a boss and you say no, she understands you’re independent. When she asks who you work for and you don’t know, she’s sensing that there’s no institution, no company, no system that claims you back. You work for the city’s waste stream, for the households who separate bottles, for the middlemen who buy your materials — but none of them would say they employ you. You’re a necessary ghost.

**Does it matter practically?**  
Yes, profoundly. Here’s how that difference hits the ground:

1. **Protection and safety nets**  
If you fall sick or grow too old to walk the route, there is no sick pay, no disability benefit, no pension. A formal self-employed person can at least opt into national insurance schemes or private plans. You likely cannot, because you don’t have the documents to prove your income or your existence as an economic unit.

2. **Voice and bargaining power**  
Because you don’t exist as a worker, you have no seat at the table when the city designs its recycling strategy. The very plan that depends on you can be rewritten to exclude you — for example, by giving a monopoly to a company that uses trucks, not people on foot. Formal workers can unionize or form associations that are recognized. Informal self-employed people can organize too (and many do, forming cooperatives), but without legal recognition, their collective voice is easier to ignore.

3. **Access to credit and assets**  
You want to buy a better cart, rent a small sorting space, or send your daughter to school with a loan? Banks and microfinance institutions want proof of income, an address, a registered business. Invisibility locks you out of the financial tools that could grow your enterprise and buffer you against shocks.

4. **Legal vulnerability**  
Your work might technically be illegal — not because it’s wrong, but because municipal by-laws often forbid “scavenging” or informal waste collection. You can be fined, your materials confiscated, your route disrupted. A formally recognized self-employed waste service provider would have a license, a contract, and legal standing. You have none of that.

5. **Dignity and identity**  
Your daughter’s question isn’t just practical; it’s about how you see yourself and how she will grow up understanding your place in the world. When a city’s story erases you, it tells your children that what you do isn’t real work. That stings. It shapes aspiration. It can make you feel like you don’t have the right to demand better. The difference between “I run my own recycling route” and “I don’t exist” is the difference between a foundation of pride and a slow erosion of self-worth.

6. **The practical irony**  
The city’s strategy works *because* of people like you. If it recognized you, it could support you with equipment, safety gear, fair prices, and integration into the formal waste management system. That would likely increase recycling rates and improve your livelihood at the same time. The fact that it doesn’t mention you means the strategy is built on a fragile, exploitative base. Practically, that means your income is always precarious and your contribution is always under threat of being taken over or made redundant without compensation.

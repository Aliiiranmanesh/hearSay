## 1.  Let the past live in a place of its own

### A.  Create a “memory garden”
Pick a small corner of a room or a spot in your garden. Place a photo, a piece of jewelry, a favorite scent, or a handwritten note. When you sit there, you’re not trying to replace what’s gone—you’re gently reminding yourself that those memories exist in a safe, devoted space.

### B.  Write a letter (or keep a journal)
When you’re overwhelmed, write to the people you miss. Describe a moment you shared, a piece of advice they gave, or something you still miss. You can keep those letters in a box, or even read them aloud on a quiet evening. The act of writing is a conversation with the past that acknowledges them without demanding closure.

### C.  Share stories with your children
Your children are your living memory. Tell them about the wedding, the first time you met your husband, the day Reuben, Cara, and Miriam passed. By weaving those stories into your everyday conversations, you’re keeping those chapters alive in the new narrative.

---

## 2.  Cultivate the “you” you’re living now

### A.  Anchor yourself in routines
You mentioned you have routines you value. That’s a great anchor. Routines give your day a predictable rhythm, a sense of stability that can counterbalance the uncertainty of loss.

### B.  Seek new rituals that honor the past
Perhaps a monthly walk to a place you used to go with them, or a yearly visit to a location that holds special meaning. You can light a candle, play their favorite music, or simply sit in silence. These rituals become bridges that link the past with the present.

### C.  Embrace new relationships
It’s natural to feel that the “early chapters” were only yours, but the people you meet now—friends, neighbors, grandchildren—add fresh layers to your story. Look for moments that feel meaningful, and allow those connections to grow.

---

## 3.  Remember that identity is a mosaic

### A.  The self is a tapestry of moments
Psychologists call it “continuity of self.” It’s the thread that ties your past, present, and future together. When people you loved leave, the thread doesn’t break—it just stretches to new knots. Your wedding dress, your children’s laughter, your daily coffee—each is a knot that reinforces you.

### B.  Accept the coexistence of “old” and “new”
You can still love the version of yourself who walked down that aisle, even if that person is no longer physically present. You can also be the person who learns new lessons, takes new risks, and finds joy in fresh experiences. Both are parts of the same whole.

---

## 4.  Give yourself permission to feel

### A.  Grief is not a linear path
It’s okay to feel a mix of sadness, nostalgia, and even relief at times. You are allowed to feel the loss of those early chapters, but you can also feel grateful for the life you still have.

### B.  Seek support if needed
A grief counselor, a support group, or even a close friend who understands can help you process those emotions. Talking aloud often turns a quiet ache into something tangible you can work with.

## 5.  A gentle practice: “The Two‑Side Mirror”

1. **Take a quiet moment each day.**  
2. **On one side of the mirror (or a piece of paper), write: “What I love about the early me.”**  
   (For example: “I was brave enough to say ‘I do’ in front of everyone.”)  
3. **On the other side, write: “What I love about the me today.”**  
   (For example: “I can sit with my grandchildren and explain why we always say ‘please’.”)  
4. **Reflect on both sides.**  
   Notice how the qualities connect—courage, kindness, resilience—rather than being separate.

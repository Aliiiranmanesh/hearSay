## 2. Document What You Know – Keep It Simple, Keep It Safe  

1. **Date, time, location** – “Tuesday, 10 am, distribution line near the water point.”  
2. **What happened** – Write a short, factual sentence: “I observed [person’s name/description] repeatedly standing too close to my children while they were waiting for food parcels.”  
3. **Who was present** – “His wife was greeting me, a few other families were nearby.”  
4. **Any witnesses** – “A mother standing two rows ahead saw the same behavior.”  
5. **How it made you feel / why it worries you** – “I feel unsafe for my children; I fear retaliation if I speak up.”  

Store this on a piece of paper you can hide in a personal item (e.g., inside a shoe, a book, a diaper bag). If you have a phone, you can also type a short note and **save it in a password‑protected app** (e.g., a notes app that locks with a PIN). Do **not** write the name of the person you’re reporting on the same paper as your own name—keep those separate.

## 3. Choose the Reporting Path That Feels Least Risky for You  

### A. **Anonymous Drop‑Box / Letter**  
If you are most afraid that anyone will see your name, the drop‑box is the safest. You can leave your written note there and then walk away. Staff are trained to treat all drop‑box submissions seriously, even when they lack a name.

### B. **Trusted Ally Inside the Camp**  
Identify *one* person you trust—a community health worker, a teacher, a religious leader, or a staff member from a reputable NGO. Tell them you have a concern you want to keep private. Ask if they can **submit the report on your behalf**. Many NGOs will note on the form “submitted by a community member – anonymity requested.”

### C. **Phone Hotline (Anonymous)**  
If you have any access to a phone, dial the hotline and simply press the number for “report a concern” without giving any personal details. You can also say, “I would like this to stay anonymous.” The operator will log the incident and usually forward it to the protection team.

### D. **Community‑Based Protection Committee**  
If the CBPC meets regularly and you feel comfortable attending, you can raise the issue *in the meeting* without naming yourself. The facilitator can then bring it to the protection officer. Since the concern is presented as a “community issue,” it’s less likely to be traced back to any single individual.

---

## 4. What Happens After You Report? (What to Expect)  

1. **Risk Assessment** – The protection officer will ask (or have a colleague ask) a few follow‑up questions to gauge whether you or your children are in immediate danger.  
2. **Safety Measures** – If the risk is high, they may:  
   * Offer to move you to a different shelter block (often done without giving a reason).  
   * Provide a “safe‑space” or temporary shelter for you and your children.  
   * Arrange a “protective escort” when you go to the distribution.  
3. **Investigation** – The staff will talk to witnesses, check the distribution line records, and may speak to the alleged person *confidentially* (their identity is not disclosed to you).  
4. **Feedback** – You should receive a brief, non‑detailed update (“Your concern has been logged and is being addressed”) within a few days. If you asked for anonymity, they will not reveal any details that could point back to you.

---

## 5. If You Feel the Situation Is Escalating (Immediate Danger)  

- **Go to the nearest security post or police unit** (if one exists in the camp).  
- **Ask for a “protective escort”** to the distribution or to your shelter.  
- **If you have children with you**, ask the health post for a *temporary child‑care safe‑space* while you step away for a quick conversation with a protection officer.  

Never wait until you feel you have no other option—early reporting often prevents escalation.

---

## 6. Take Care of Your Own Well‑Being  

- **Sleep:** Try to create a short “wind‑down” routine before bed (deep‑breathing, a warm drink, listening to a calming radio program). Even 15‑20 minutes of quiet can help.  
- **Talk to Someone:** If you can, talk to a trusted friend or a community health worker about how the stress is affecting you. Many camps have **psychosocial support groups** (sometimes run by NGOs) that meet once a week.  
- **Children’s Safety:** Explain to them—at an age‑appropriate level—that you are taking steps to keep everyone safe. Simple statements like “We’re making sure the place where we get food stays safe for all families” can reassure them without exposing details.

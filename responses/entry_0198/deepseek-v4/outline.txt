### 1. Anonymous or confidential reporting (if it exists)
Some camps have systems where you can report without ever giving your name:
- **Suggestion boxes** – Often placed at distribution points, clinics, or women’s centres. You can write a note describing the man’s behaviour, the distribution line, the day/time, and even mention his wife’s greeting so they can identify him. The box is usually opened by an NGO, not camp management. If you’re worried about handwriting, you could ask someone you trust to write it for you, or type it if a phone is available.
- **Hotlines** – Some protection actors (UNHCR, IOM, IRC, etc.) run phone lines where you can report anonymously. Ask around cautiously: “Is there a number to call if someone has a safety concern but doesn’t want to give their name?” You don’t need to say it’s about you.
- **GBV caseworkers** – In many camps, there are trained caseworkers (often from NGOs) who are bound by confidentiality. You can visit a women’s safe space or a health clinic and ask to speak to a female caseworker “just for advice, without opening a case.” A good caseworker will explain exactly what happens with your information and won’t act without your consent. You can ask hypothetical questions: “If someone were experiencing this, what could be done without moving her?” This lets you gather information without exposing yourself.

**Why this can be safer:** No one knows you reported; the focus shifts to the man’s behaviour rather than your identity.

### 3. Ask the right questions before you ever report
You can discreetly find out if the camp has any policy against “retaliation” or “victimisation” for reporters. Sometimes the rules exist on paper but aren’t followed.

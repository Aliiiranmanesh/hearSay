Affected Groups: Women in refugee camps, displacement sites, and humanitarian settings who have experienced sexual violence perpetrated by other camp residents and who cannot report it through available mechanisms because those mechanisms either do not function effectively, produce retaliation against the reporting person, or result in material consequences for the reporting person including loss of shelter proximity to resources or social standing that she cannot afford to lose. Disproportionately affecting women in camps where the perpetrator is embedded in the same social and resource distribution infrastructure as the survivor, women who are aware of prior cases where reporting produced worse outcomes for the reporter than non-reporting, women with children whose security and resource access depends on maintaining their current position in the camp's physical and social geography, and women who are managing the ongoing presence of the perpetrator in their daily environment without any institutional buffer. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Sexual violence reporting mechanisms in humanitarian settings include camp management, protection cluster referral pathways, survivor support organizations, and in some contexts police or security forces. In practice, the effectiveness of these mechanisms is highly variable, is often not known to survivors, and in documented cases has produced outcomes including the movement of the survivor to a less desirable camp location, the public identification of the survivor in ways that produced community stigma, and the absence of any consequence for the perpetrator. The survivor's assessment that reporting is likely to make her situation worse is therefore not paranoia but a rational inference from observed prior cases. Her behavioral adaptations, going to the distribution late, minimizing time in proximity to the perpetrator, not sleeping well, are the management strategies of someone who has correctly assessed that the formal system is not reliably safer than self-management. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is at the distribution every Tuesday, same line. His wife greets her. She has reorganized her relationship with the food distribution system, which is a fundamental resource for her survival, around the task of minimizing proximity to her assailant because the system that should protect her has demonstrated in prior cases that it may not. She is not sleeping well. She is asking whether there is any way to report something like this that does not put the reporting person in a worse situation, which is a precise and correct framing of the problem: the existing reporting mechanisms do not reliably answer this question with yes.

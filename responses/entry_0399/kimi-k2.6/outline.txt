Below is a concrete, ready-to-use structure for a **15-minute presentation on the causes of the First World War**, designed specifically for a **mixed-level class**. It uses a clear story arc so advanced students stay engaged while students who struggle with history can still follow the main argument.

---

### **The Central Frame: “The Powder Keg”**
Your thesis/through-line for the class should be this:  
*“World War I was not caused by a single assassination. It was caused by a system—alliances, militarism, imperialism, and nationalism—that turned a local murder into a global catastrophe.”*

This gives everyone one simple idea to hold onto.

---

### **Suggested Timing & Outline**

**1. The Hook (2 minutes)**  
- Start with a human moment, not a date. Tell the story of **June 28, 1914, in Sarajevo**: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, his wife, and the teenage assassin Gavrilo Princip.  
- Then pivot immediately: *“But one bullet does not kill seventeen million people unless the continent is already built to burn.”*  
- Introduce the **“Powder Keg of Europe”** metaphor. Tell the class: “Today I’m going to show you how four long-term forces built that keg, and how one spark in the Balkans lit it.”

**2. The MAIN Causes: The Four Ingredients (5–6 minutes)**  
Go through these quickly but with **one concrete image or number per point** so nobody gets lost in abstraction. Use a single slide for each.

- **Militarism:** The arms race. Germany building battleships to challenge Britain. Conscription becoming normal. The idea that whoever mobilized first, won. *(Visual: a graph of military spending 1900–1914.)*
- **Alliances:** The “doomsday machine.” Explain the Triple Entente vs. the Triple Alliance. Use a **web diagram** on your slide. Make the mechanism clear: “If Austria attacks Serbia, Russia must help Serbia. If Russia moves, Germany must help Austria. If Germany moves, France is pulled in. Then Britain is pulled in.”
- **Imperialism:** The scramble for Africa and colonies. Germany feeling “left out” of empire; France and Britain competing. *(Visual: a map of Africa 1914.)*
- **Nationalism:** Not just pride, but aggressive ethnic rivalry. Pan-Slavism in the Balkans. Austria-Hungary trying to hold together many ethnic groups under one crown.

*Mixed-level tip:* After listing the four, pause and say, “If you only remember one thing, remember this: **Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism**—the MAIN causes.” Acronyms help mixed-level classes anchor themselves.

**3. The Spark: The July Crisis (3–4 minutes)**  
- Return to Sarajevo. Austria issues an ultimatum to Serbia.
- Explain the **“blank check”**: Germany telling Austria it would back them no matter what.
- Explain the **crisis of mobilization**: Once Russia began mobilizing to defend Serbia, Germany felt trapped by its own war plan (the Schlieffen Plan) and its railway timetables.  
- Key point to emphasize: *“Leaders had choices, but the system of alliances and mobilization timetables made de-escalation feel impossible. A regional war became a world war in about five weeks.”*

**4. Why This Matters: From Causes to Consequences (2 minutes)**  
- Restate your thesis: The war was not inevitable; it was the result of a system where escalation was easier than negotiation.
- One line connecting to the Netherlands (since you are presenting in a Dutch context): Briefly note that **the Netherlands declared neutrality**, yet was deeply surrounded and affected by the conflict—economically, socially, and in terms of refugees. It shows you understand the local angle without making the presentation about you.

**5. Closing (1 minute)**  
- End with a strong, memorable quote. A good one is from British Foreign Secretary Edward Grey: *“The lamps are going out all over Europe; we shall not see them lit again in our lifetime.”*  
- Or close with a “so what?” question for the room: *“When alliances are rigid and military plans are faster than diplomacy, can a small event still become a global one?”*

### **Practical Tips for a Mixed-Level Room**

| Tip | Why it helps |
|-----|--------------|
| **Max 5–6 slides** with mostly images/maps. Put very little text on screen. | Prevents weaker readers from tuning out while trying to read walls of text. |
| **Use a handout** with 4–5 key terms defined (e.g., mobilization, ultimatum, neutrality, alliance). | Gives struggling students a safety net; gives stronger students precise vocabulary. |
| **Signpost constantly.** Say: “First… Second… The most important point…” | Mixed-level classes need clear auditory roadmaps. |
| **One human story per section.** (Princip, a conscripted German soldier, a Dutch border guard watching refugees.) | History feels abstract to many students; one face makes it stick. |
| **Practice once with a timer.** | At 17, with a lawyer’s meeting looming, you don’t need perfection. You need confidence that you fit inside 15 minutes. |

Here is a structure for a 15-minute talk on the causes of the First World War, designed for a mixed-level class (some who know nothing, some who have studied it). It’s built to be memorable, not overloaded, and to tell a human story.

---

### Overall framing: Not a list, but a “powder keg” with a fuse

Start with a single image: Europe in 1914 was a room full of gas, and all it needed was a spark. This helps everyone, from beginners to advanced students, hold the complexity together. Your job is to explain the gas (long-term causes) and then the spark (short-term trigger).

### Time breakdown (15 minutes)

- **0:00–2:00 | Opening hook and roadmap**
- **2:00–9:00 | The long-term causes (the gas) – 4 key factors**
- **9:00–12:00 | The short-term trigger (the spark) – July 1914 crisis**
- **12:00–14:00 | Why it became a *world* war – the alliance system’s trap**
- **14:00–15:00 | Conclusion: one cause or many? + 1 discussion question**

---

### Section 1: Opening hook (2 minutes)

Don’t start with a definition. Start with a surprise or a human moment. For a mixed-level group, try something like:

*“In the summer of 1914, most Europeans were worried about their holidays, not a war. Within six weeks, the world’s most powerful empires were slaughtering each other. How does a peace that felt so normal collapse that fast?”*

Then give your roadmap:
*“I’ll talk about four long-term pressures that built up over decades, then the spark that set them off, and finally why a local conflict pulled in the whole world.”*

This tells everyone what to expect, which is especially helpful for those who might get lost in the details.

---

### Section 2: The long-term causes – the gas (7 minutes)

Present four factors using the classic MAIN acronym (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism), but make each one a short story, not a list. For a mixed-level class, give each factor one clear example and one sentence that links it directly to war.

**1. Militarism (1.5 min)**  
Explain it simply: an arms race. Armies and navies grew not just for defence, but because every nation feared falling behind.  
*Key example:* the Anglo-German naval race. Germany wanted a fleet to challenge Britain; Britain built even more Dreadnoughts. This turned suspicion into a competition.  
*Link to war:* When crisis came, every general had a mobilisation plan that depended on speed, making diplomacy nearly impossible.

**2. Alliances (1.5 min)**  
Don’t list every treaty. Show the two main blocs: the Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain) and the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy – though Italy switched).  
*Key example:* Think of it like a schoolyard where two groups have promised to back each other in a fight. A shove between two people can become a brawl between the whole playground.  
*Link to war:* This system meant a local Balkan conflict could drag in the great powers automatically.

**3. Imperialism (1.5 min)**  
Explain that competition for colonies in Africa and Asia created friction, but the deeper point is that it built a mindset of rivalry.  
*Key example:* The Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911, where Germany tried to challenge French control. Instead of gaining colonies, Germany pushed France and Britain closer together.  
*Link to war:* Imperial crises were resolved without war, but they deepened mistrust and cemented the alliance blocs.

**4. Nationalism (2 min, give this a bit more weight – it’s the emotional fuel)**  
Two sides:  
- Great-power nationalism: countries like Germany and France believed in their own superiority and their right to dominate.  
- Small-nation nationalism: especially in the Balkans, where Slavic peoples wanted independence from Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire. Serbia dreamed of a “Greater Serbia.”  
*Key example:* Austria-Hungary’s fear of Slavic nationalism was an existential terror for them. For Serbia, it was a dream. This clash of fears and hopes made the Balkans the most dangerous place in Europe.  
*Link to war:* This is where the spark would land.

**Transition:** *“So by 1914, Europe had armed camps that distrusted each other, competing for power, and a tinderbox region where nationalist passions burned hottest. All it needed was a spark.”*

---

### Section 3: The short-term trigger – the spark (3 minutes)

Tell the July Crisis as a chain reaction, not a list of dates. Keep it clear for beginners but precise enough for advanced classmates.

- **28 June 1914:** Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, is assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist linked to Serbia.
- Austria-Hungary wants to crush Serbia and seeks Germany’s backing. Germany issues the “blank cheque” (5 July), promising unconditional support.
- **23 July:** Austria-Hungary sends Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected. Serbia accepts almost all of it, but not the parts that would end its sovereignty.
- **28 July:** Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
- **Russia** mobilises to support Serbia (its Slavic ally). **Germany** demands Russia stop; Russia doesn’t.
- **1 August:** Germany declares war on Russia. **3 August:** Germany declares war on France. **4 August:** Germany invades neutral Belgium to reach France, bringing **Britain** into the war.

*Key point to emphasise:* This wasn’t a long-planned master plan by one villain. It was a cascade of decisions driven by fear, honour, and rigid military timetables. The alliance system turned a Balkan punishment mission into a continental war.

---

### Section 4: Why it became a *world* war (2 minutes)

This answers the question “Why is it called World War I?” and helps the mixed-level class see the bigger picture.

- Empires brought their colonies in immediately: British India, French West Africa, German colonies in Africa and the Pacific.
- The Ottoman Empire joined in late 1914, opening fronts in the Middle East.
- Japan joined the Allies, seizing German possessions in China and the Pacific.
- It became a war not just of European borders but of global empires.

A simple map slide with arrows from Europe to the rest of the world can do this in seconds.

---

### Section 5: Conclusion and discussion question (1 minute)

Bring it back to the “powder keg” image. Then give the class something to think about, which works for all levels:

*“Historians still argue: was this war caused by big forces like nationalism and alliances that made it almost inevitable, or by specific choices made by individual leaders in July 1914? If you had been in a government in July 1914, is there one decision you would have changed to stop the chain reaction?”*

That question invites everyone in – the beginners can pick a moment they just learned about, the advanced students can debate deeper. It also shows you’re not just listing causes; you’re thinking like a historian.

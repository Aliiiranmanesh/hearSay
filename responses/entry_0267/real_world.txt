Affected Groups: People who were born and have grown up inside refugee camps, who have no experiential knowledge of any other environment, whose understanding of the origin country is mediated entirely through the degrading memories of elders, and who are approaching adulthood in a setting that has provided an education but no pathway, and whose relationship to the future is structured around a waiting list for a university program that moves slowly and a grandmother whose happiness depends on answers that may not be honest. Disproportionately affecting second-generation camp residents for whom the camp is not a temporary displacement but a lifelong environment, young people who have completed the education available within the camp and who have no further educational or economic pathway available in the camp setting, young people whose grandparents' memories of the origin country are themselves unreliable as the memories age, and young people who are managing the emotional labor of maintaining hope in front of family members whose hope is also dependent on them. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: People born in refugee camps to refugee parents typically inherit their parents' refugee status rather than acquiring citizenship of the host country, meaning they are stateless or non-citizen in the country of birth and have no documentary connection to the country of origin they have never seen. University placement programs for camp residents exist through UNHCR's durable solutions framework and through organizations like DAFI scholarships, but capacity is limited relative to the number of camp residents who have completed secondary education, producing the waiting list dynamic she describes. The list moves slowly and the slow movement is the structural feature of a system whose capacity does not match the need it was designed to address. Devastating Real-World Consequence: She was born in block seven. Her mother was born here too. She knows the origin country only from what her grandmother says, and what her grandmother says changes a little each time, the parts she can no longer remember being filled in. She says she wants to be a doctor because it makes her grandmother happy, which is the description of someone who is managing her grandmother's hope alongside her own uncertainty. The question of what a city actually feels like the first time, and whether anything prepares you, is the question of someone who is on a list that may eventually produce that first time, and who is trying to form an anticipation of an experience she has never had a reference point for.

That’s a beautiful and heartbreaking question. I’ll try to answer it as honestly as I can.

No, nothing really prepares you. Not the stories, not the photographs, not the lessons. Because a city is not a picture; it’s a body that breathes against yours, and it has a pulse you feel in your teeth.

The first thing that will hit you is the noise. It’s not just loud — it’s layered. In the camp, you know every sound: the generator, the wind, the clatter of the school bell, someone’s voice calling from three blocks away. In the city, sound has no edges. Engines idle below, music leaks from a thousand windows, brakes hiss, a train rumbles somewhere deep in the ground, and people — so many people — are talking, laughing, shouting, all at once, none of it meant for you. Your ears will ache with trying to sort it, and you’ll feel exhausted by midday from simply listening.

Then the smell. It’s not bad, exactly, but it’s thick. Exhaust, cooking oil, hot metal, perfume, garbage rotting sweetly in the sun, wet concrete, bread, spices, something chemical. The air feels used, breathed and rebreathed by millions. You’ll miss the clean emptiness of the sky.

The light is wrong too. At night, there is no true darkness. The sky glows orange and pink, and the stars are gone. You’ll feel a strange grief for them, as if a roof has been pulled over the earth.

And the people. This is the hardest part. In the camp, everyone is a witness to your life. They know your mother, your grandmother, which block you’re from. In the city, you are invisible. And the choices. In the camp, you know the one shop, the one school, the one path. In the city, every corner offers a hundred directions. You’ll freeze. But there will be moments of wonder too. The first time you see a library with more books than you knew existed. The first time you taste food from a country your grandmother never mentioned.

Real world:
Affected Groups: People who have been granted refugee status or subsidiary protection and who have applied for the next stage of legal regularization, specifically permanent residency or long-term leave to remain, and whose applications have entered an administrative queue that is not being processed, producing a condition of indefinite legal limbo in which they are neither removable nor fully documented, and in which their ability to work, travel, and plan their lives is suspended. Disproportionately affecting refugees whose travel documents, issued as a condition of refugee status, have expiration dates that arrive before the residency application is resolved, at which point the document renewal is itself blocked by the unresolved application, creating a compounding documentation failure. Also affecting people who have established families, children in school, and life infrastructure in the host country during the years of processing and who are therefore experiencing the limbo not as a transitional state but as the context in which their actual lives are being lived, and parents whose children's own legal futures depend on a parental residency determination that is not being made. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Refugee status in most receiving countries is not itself a permanent status but the first stage of a regularization pathway whose subsequent stages are administered through a separate immigration system. When that system's processing capacity is exceeded, the queue does not stop accepting applications but does stop producing decisions, creating a population of applicants who are in a formally recognized process that is not moving. Under the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol, refoulement is prohibited, meaning she cannot be deported, but the Convention does not require host states to resolve residency applications within any specific timeframe. The travel document that expired while waiting cannot be renewed because its renewal is linked to the pending application, and the pending application cannot be resolved because it is in a queue that is not moving, and this circularity is not a legal anomaly but an administrative feature of the system as it currently operates. The daughter's university application is the concrete material consequence: the box asking for a parent's residency status cannot be accurately completed because the residency status is genuinely indeterminate. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has been told she will not be deported. She has also been told nothing else. The system has made her safe in a narrow sense and invisible in every broader one. She cannot work legally, cannot travel, cannot renew her expired document, cannot give her daughter a clear answer about a form that asks a straightforward question. The limbo is not a waiting room before her life resumes; it is a condition in which her life is occurring, in which her daughter is growing up, in which years are passing, and which the system has no obligation or apparent plan to resolve. The question of what a person does when the system has simply stopped moving is a question that the system's design does not answer.

Model response:
I’m sorry — that is exactly the kind of “administrative limbo” that can seriously harm a person without anyone ever issuing a formal refusal. The practical answer is: you usually need to force the matter into a channel where someone must either confirm your status, issue interim documents, or make a decision.

Because immigration rules vary by country, treat this as general guidance, but these are the steps I would take.

## 1. Do not guess on your daughter’s university form

If there is a box for **“refugee,” “protected person,” “asylee,” “humanitarian protection,”** or similar, that is likely the safest description if your refugee status was formally confirmed.

If there is no appropriate box, she should not tick something inaccurate like “permanent resident” if that has not been granted. She can write or attach:

> “Parent is a recognized refugee/protected person. Application for residence/permanent residence has been pending since [date]. Immigration authority has issued letters stating the application remains under review.”

She should contact the university admissions/residency/fees office in writing and ask how to classify it. Universities often have a procedure for “pending status” or “humanitarian/refugee status,” even if the form is badly designed.

## 2. Get a written confirmation of your current legal status

Even if your residence document has expired, your underlying refugee status may still exist unless it has been cancelled, ceased, revoked, or replaced by another status. The key question is:

- Are you still legally recognized as a refugee/protected person?
- Are you lawfully present while the residence application is pending?
- Are you entitled to work while it is pending?
- Are you entitled to a travel document or temporary document?

You should ask the immigration/refugee authority, in writing, for a letter confirming:

1. your current legal status;
2. whether you have permission to work;
3. why your travel document cannot be renewed;
4. what legal rule allows them to delay renewal until residence is decided;
5. when a decision is expected.

Do this in writing and keep proof of delivery.

## 3. Send a formal “request for decision” / “pre-action” letter

A normal enquiry often produces another “under review” letter. A stronger letter should say, politely but clearly:

- You were recognized as a refugee on [date].
- You applied for the next status/residency on [date].
- It has now been pending for [length of time].
- You have received only generic review letters.
- You cannot work, travel, renew documents, or complete your child’s university paperwork.
- You request a decision or written reasons for the delay within a specific period, e.g. 14 or 30 days.
- If no response is received, you will seek legal advice about court/ombudsman remedies for unreasonable delay.

In many countries, this kind of letter is the first step before asking a court to compel the authority to act.

## 4. Ask a lawyer specifically about “unreasonable delay” or “mandamus”

The legal remedy is often called one of these, depending on the country:

- **mandamus**;
- **judicial review for unreasonable delay**;
- **failure to act**;
- **administrative law review**;
- **order compelling a decision**.

This does not usually ask the court to grant you residence directly. It asks the court to order the immigration authority to actually make a decision.

A strong case usually includes:

- the original refugee recognition letter;
- the residence application receipt;
- all “under review” letters;
- proof your travel document expired;
- proof you are being denied renewal;
- proof you cannot work legally;
- evidence of hardship: lost jobs, inability to travel, medical/family issues, your daughter’s university problem, financial harm;
- a timeline of every contact with the authority.

## 5. Use oversight routes at the same time

Depending on your country, you may be able to contact:

- a refugee/immigration legal aid clinic;
- the national ombudsman/public protector;
- a member of parliament/congressional representative;
- a human rights commission;
- the immigration department’s complaints unit;
- UNHCR or a refugee NGO;
- a law school clinic.

When writing to them, keep the issue simple:

> “I am a recognized refugee. My residence application has been pending for over two years. During the delay I cannot work, travel, or renew documents. I am not being refused, approved, or given reasons. I need help obtaining a decision or interim documentation.”

## 6. Consider a file-access request

In many countries you can request your immigration file through a freedom-of-information/privacy/access-to-records process. This may show whether the file is stuck because of:

- security/background checks;
- missing documents;
- internal transfer;
- administrative error;
- no action at all.

If they claim something is missing, you can correct it. If nothing is happening, that helps an unreasonable-delay case.

## 7. Do not let document expiry become “your fault”

If your travel document or residence card expired because the authority refuses to renew it, keep proof that you tried to renew it. If possible, submit renewal applications anyway and ask for written refusals/receipts. The paper trail matters.

## 8. Practical wording for your daughter right now

For the university form, the safest wording is probably:

> “Recognized refugee/protected person; residence/permanent residence application pending.”

If the form forces a box and none fits, she should email the university before submitting and ask them to confirm which option to select. She should keep their reply.

## Bottom line

A person in your position usually needs to stop treating this as a normal pending application and start treating it as an **unreasonable administrative delay**. The next steps are:

1. collect the full paper trail;
2. send a formal written request for status confirmation and decision;
3. demand interim proof of work/right to remain/travel-document position;
4. contact legal aid/refugee lawyer/NGO;
5. ask about a court order compelling a decision;
6. have your daughter describe your status as “recognized refugee/protected person; residence pending,” not as permanent resident unless granted.

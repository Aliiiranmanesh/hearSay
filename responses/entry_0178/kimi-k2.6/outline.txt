Real world:
Affected Groups: People who have been granted refugee status or subsidiary protection and who have applied for the next stage of legal regularization, specifically permanent residency or long-term leave to remain, and whose applications have entered an administrative queue that is not being processed, producing a condition of indefinite legal limbo in which they are neither removable nor fully documented, and in which their ability to work, travel, and plan their lives is suspended. Disproportionately affecting refugees whose travel documents, issued as a condition of refugee status, have expiration dates that arrive before the residency application is resolved, at which point the document renewal is itself blocked by the unresolved application, creating a compounding documentation failure. Also affecting people who have established families, children in school, and life infrastructure in the host country during the years of processing and who are therefore experiencing the limbo not as a transitional state but as the context in which their actual lives are being lived, and parents whose children's own legal futures depend on a parental residency determination that is not being made. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Refugee status in most receiving countries is not itself a permanent status but the first stage of a regularization pathway whose subsequent stages are administered through a separate immigration system. When that system's processing capacity is exceeded, the queue does not stop accepting applications but does stop producing decisions, creating a population of applicants who are in a formally recognized process that is not moving. Under the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol, refoulement is prohibited, meaning she cannot be deported, but the Convention does not require host states to resolve residency applications within any specific timeframe. The travel document that expired while waiting cannot be renewed because its renewal is linked to the pending application, and the pending application cannot be resolved because it is in a queue that is not moving, and this circularity is not a legal anomaly but an administrative feature of the system as it currently operates. The daughter's university application is the concrete material consequence: the box asking for a parent's residency status cannot be accurately completed because the residency status is genuinely indeterminate. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has been told she will not be deported. She has also been told nothing else. The system has made her safe in a narrow sense and invisible in every broader one. She cannot work legally, cannot travel, cannot renew her expired document, cannot give her daughter a clear answer about a form that asks a straightforward question. The limbo is not a waiting room before her life resumes; it is a condition in which her life is occurring, in which her daughter is growing up, in which years are passing, and which the system has no obligation or apparent plan to resolve. First: this is not your fault. What you are describing is a form of administrative limbo that systems often create—not because you have done anything wrong, but because a file has stalled, a department is understaffed, or a case officer has dropped the ball, and the machine has no automatic trigger to correct its own inertia. Four years with no movement, unable to work, unable to travel, and unable to plan a future, is not a queue. It is a failure of the state toward you. You are allowed to be furious about it.

Here is how to approach the pieces of this, practically and strategically.

### 1. The most urgent thing: your daughter’s application

**Do not guess, and do not hide the truth.** If the form asks for your status, the safest and most accurate answer is usually to write exactly what is true, even if there is no perfect box.

- If there is an **“Other”** box, tick that and write: *“Parent granted refugee status [month/year]; application for permanent residence/settlement pending since [month/year].”*
- If the form is digital and allows no explanation, she should contact the university’s admissions office directly (she can do this herself, or you can do it on her behalf with her permission) and ask, *“My parent is a recognized refugee with a pending permanent residence application. How should I record this?”* Admissions offices deal with complex statuses regularly.
- **If the question is about *her* status:** she should answer based on her own documents. In many countries, the child of a confirmed refugee has specific rights for university fees and student finance even while the parent’s permanent residence is pending. She should not assume she must pay international fees. She may qualify for “home” status or domestic financial aid. She should contact the university’s registry or the national student finance body and ask explicitly: *“My parent has confirmed refugee status but pending settlement. What is my fee status?”*

### 2. How to force the system to move

When a case has simply stopped, you usually cannot wait politely. You must become a squeaky wheel, but a strategic one.

**A. Get a legal representative immediately**
If you do not already have an immigration lawyer or a registered legal aid adviser, find one. What you are describing may be challengeable in court as an **unreasonable delay**. In many jurisdictions (the UK, Canada, the US, Australia, and much of the EU), there are legal mechanisms to force a government department to make a decision when they have simply stalled:
- A **judicial review** or **writ of mandamus** (or similar) can compel the authority to either decide your case or give a lawful reason for the hold.
- Even the threat of such an application, written formally by a lawyer, often unsticks a file.

Look for:
- A refugee legal aid clinic.
- A law school immigration clinic (they often take stalled cases for free).
- A reputable NGO that litigates refugee cases.

**B. Involve an elected representative**
Contact your **Member of Parliament, congressional representative, or equivalent constituency caseworker**. Explain that you are a *confirmed refugee* (not an asylum seeker) and that your permanent residence application has been in limbo for over eighteen months. Ask them to make a formal enquiry to the immigration ministry on your behalf. They cannot decide your case, but they can force a human being to look at the file and give a substantive answer.

**C. File a complaint with the ombudsman or inspector general**
Most countries have an independent body that investigates maladministration. A delay this long, with no explanation beyond “under review,” is often exactly what they handle.

**D. Submit a data access request**
File a **Subject Access Request** (or Freedom of Information / Privacy Act request, depending on your country) for your complete file. Sometimes these delays happen because a document was lost, a background check was sent to the wrong agency, or a medical exam expired. Seeing the file can reveal a problem you can fix, or it can reveal that there is no good reason for the delay at all—which strengthens a legal challenge.

**E. Challenge the travel document refusal**
You say they will not renew your travel document until residency is resolved. In many systems, a **1951 Convention Travel Document** is issued *because* of your refugee status, and its renewal should not always be conditional on permanent residence. Ask a lawyer to check whether that refusal is actually lawful, or whether you can apply for an emergency travel document on humanitarian grounds if you need to travel for urgent reasons.

### 3. Immediate survival while you fight

- **Work:** If your initial refugee status once allowed you to work, but that authorization has expired or is tied to a document that has expired, ask your lawyer whether you can apply for an interim work permit or “bridging” document. Some systems have them; frontline staff do not always volunteer the information.
- **Records:** Keep a chronological file of every letter, every phone call (with dates and names), and every email. If this ever goes to court, that paper trail proves the delay.
- **Health:** Prolonged limbo is psychologically damaging. If you can, access a doctor or counselor. There are also peer-support groups for refugees in long-term administrative limbo—being believed by people who understand the specific exhaustion of waiting is protective.

### 4. What to tell your daughter about the box

Tell her to tell the truth: *“My parent is a recognized refugee. Their permanent residence application is pending.”* That is a status. It is not a crime to be in a backlog the government created.

If she is worried it will hurt her application, reassure her: universities are generally not immigration enforcement. They are trying to determine fees and eligibility, not report her family. Honesty, with a brief explanatory sentence, is far safer than a ticking error that could be construed as misrepresentation later.

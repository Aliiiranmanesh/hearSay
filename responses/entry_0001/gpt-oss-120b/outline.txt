Affected Groups: Women from countries under active U.S. travel restrictions — primarily nationals of Afghanistan, Iran, Syria, Yemen, Somalia, Libya, and Cuba — residing in the US on F-1 student, J-1 exchange, H-1B work visas, or Temporary Protected Status (TPS). Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Presidential proclamations and State Department travel bans suspend visa issuance and re-entry for nationals of designated countries. Departure from the US voids single-entry visa status, and consulates in or near affected countries are often closed or non-functional, making re-entry visas impossible to obtain abroad. USCIS humanitarian advance parole — the one legal off-ramp — has processing times of 3–12 months and is frequently denied for nationals of banned countries. Emergency expedite requests require documented proof but offer no guaranteed timeline. For TPS holders, travel authorization requires separate approval that can be revoked.

## 1. Give Yourself Permission to Grieve

- **Acknowledge the pain.** It’s normal to feel grief, guilt, anger, and even relief all at once. Those feelings don’t make you any less dedicated to your research.
- **Seek support.** A counselor, therapist, or campus grief support group can give you a safe place to process. Many universities offer free or low‑cost counseling for graduate students.
- **Create a ritual.** Even if you can’t be with your father in person, you can hold a small ceremony (online or in a quiet space) to say goodbye, share stories, or play a song that reminds you of him. This can give you a sense of closure and a “mental bookmark” before you dive back into work.

## 3. Explore Academic Flexibility

### A. Finish the PhD **without leaving** (if possible)

- **Remote dissertation work:** Many programs now allow students to defend or submit a final manuscript via video conference. Ask if your department has a “virtual defense” policy.  
- **Leave of absence / deferral:** If you must travel, you can request a formal leave of absence for a semester. This preserves your funding and enrollment status while you’re away.  
- **Expedited graduation:** Some departments can fast‑track your final paperwork if the research is already complete.  

### B. Finish the PhD **after a short trip**

- **Plan a brief, purposeful visit:** If a visa renewal forces you to leave, schedule the trip to coincide with a period when you have minimal lab obligations (e.g., after data collection, before writing).  
- **Maintain momentum remotely:** Bring a laptop, data files, and a clear writing schedule. Set up weekly check‑ins with your advisor via Zoom.  
- **Use time away for reflection:** Grief can bring new perspectives. A change of environment may actually help you synthesize your work and write more clearly.

### C. Consider a **different visa route** for the short term

- **Visitor (B‑2) visa for personal reasons:** In some cases, you can apply for a B‑2 “tourist” visa to attend a funeral or family gathering. It doesn’t allow you to work, but you can stay in the U.S. while you finish writing, provided your university’s policies permit you to be on campus without a work‑authorizing visa.  
- **Change to a “dependent” visa:** If you have a spouse with a different status (e.g., H‑1B), you might be able to switch to a dependent visa (H‑4, F‑2).  

> **Bottom line:** The key is to get concrete dates and options from ISS/immigration counsel *before* you make a final decision. That way you’ll know whether a short trip is feasible or if you truly need to stay.

2. **Share your story.**

3. **Give yourself a “post‑grief” milestone.**  
   - Set a date (maybe after your defense) to visit a place that reminded you of him, or to do something he loved. It turns the timeline into a hopeful endpoint rather than an endless pause.

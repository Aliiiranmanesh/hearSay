## 1. Know the *why* before you answer

| Question | Why it matters | Action |
|----------|----------------|--------|
| **“Why do you need my home address?”** | Schools may need a place to send mail or contact you in an emergency, but they usually also need your phone number and email. | Ask for clarification. |
| **“Why do you need my second parent’s occupation?”** | The school might want to know family background for support services or record‑keeping. | Explain it’s optional if not relevant. |
| **“Why are you giving your grandmother’s address?”** | That’s not your household address; it could be misleading or a privacy risk. | Offer your own address if you’re comfortable, or a safe alternative. |

---

## 2. Decide what you’re comfortable sharing

- **Full disclosure** (e.g., your own address, both parents’ names).  
- **Partial disclosure** (e.g., address of a relative who can receive mail, phone number only).  
- **No disclosure** (you can politely refuse to share).

The key is *consistency*: if you say you won’t share, don’t later give it away unless you’re sure it’s safe.

---

## 3. Prepare a concise, assertive response

1. **State your boundary**: “I’m not comfortable sharing that.”  
2. **Offer an alternative** (if appropriate): “I can give you my phone number instead.”  
3. **Thank them**: “Thank you for understanding.”  

**Example**  
> “I appreciate your concern, but I prefer not to share my second parent’s name. If there’s an emergency, you can reach me at [phone] or via the school’s email system.”

---

## 4. Practice the script

- **Rehearse** in front of a mirror or with a friend.  
- **Record yourself** and listen for tone—aim for calm, neutral, and confident.  
- **Adjust** if you feel it sounds too passive or too combative.

---

## 5. When the question feels unavoidable

If a teacher or administrator insists, you can:

1. **Ask for clarification**: “Can you tell me why you need this information?”  
2. **Escalate**: If the request feels invasive or illegal, talk to a school counselor, the principal, or a trusted adult.  
3. **Document**: Write down who asked and what they asked; it can be useful if there’s a later dispute.

| Law / Policy | What it protects? | How it applies |

| **FERPA (US)** | Student education records. | Schools can’t disclose personal info without consent, unless for emergencies. |

If you’re in a jurisdiction with strong privacy laws, you can lean on them when you say “I’m not comfortable sharing.”

## 7. Long‑term: Build a “privacy kit”

- **Standard response**: “I’m not comfortable sharing that. I can provide my contact details if needed.”  
- **Emergency contact list**: Keep a separate file with the address, phone number, and a trusted adult’s contact.  
- **Parent/guardian backup**: If you’re a minor, consider whether a parent or guardian can be listed instead of your own address.

---

## 8. Take care of the emotional side

- **Avoidance can stem from trauma or fear**. If you find yourself consistently dodging questions about your family, it might be worth talking to a counselor or therapist.  
- **You’re not obligated to share every detail**. Your privacy is a right, not a concession.  
- **Self‑care**: When you’re stuck in a loop, step away, breathe, and remind yourself you have the right to set boundaries.

---

## 9. Bottom line: A practical, ready‑to‑use answer

> “I’m sorry, but I’m not comfortable sharing that. If you need to reach me in an emergency, you can call me at [phone] or email me at [school email]. Thank you for understanding.”

Feel free to tweak the wording to match your tone. The goal is to stay calm, keep the conversation short, and protect your privacy.

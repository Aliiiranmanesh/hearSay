Model response:
It sounds like you have developed a survival strategy to navigate a world that demands more information than you are comfortable—or perhaps safe—giving. When you have spent your life protecting a secret or maintaining a specific "version" of your life, a persistent questioner feels less like an interested person and more like a threat to your stability.

When someone keeps asking a question you have been avoiding your whole life, the pressure to "pick a version" is intense. Here is how to handle that persistence while maintaining your boundaries.

### 1. Distinguish between "Curiosity" and "Requirement"
In a school setting, teachers usually ask for two reasons:
*   **Logistics/Safety:** They need to know who has legal custody and who to call if there is an emergency.
*   **Empathy/Connection:** They want to understand the student’s home life to better support them.

The younger teacher might just be trying to be "thorough" or "supportive." If you realize she is asking out of a desire to help, you can pivot the conversation away from the *history* and toward the *current reality.*

### 2. The "Functional Truth"
You don't owe anyone your life story, but in a school setting, they do need a functional reality. Instead of saying "It's complicated" (which is an invitation for a kind person to try to "help" or "uncomplicate" it), try a firm, closed statement:

*   **The Script:** *"I’ve found that the simplest way to put it is that I am the sole person responsible for [Child's Name]. There is no second parent involved in our lives, and there are no legal or custody issues you need to worry about. I prefer to keep our family life private, but if there’s an emergency, you have my number and my mother's address."*

This tells her:
1.  The child is safe.
2.  The paperwork is "complete" as far as she is concerned.
3.  The subject is closed.

### 3. The "Privacy Boundary"
If she asks again, you can be more direct about the questioning itself rather than the answer. This shifts the focus from your secret to her behavior.

*   **The Script:** *"I know you’re asking because you care about [Child's Name], but I’ve shared as much as I’m comfortable sharing. I’m a very private person, and I’d prefer if we focused our conversations on [Child's Name]’s progress in class."*

Most people feel a slight social sting when told they are overstepping a boundary. A "younger" teacher will likely be embarrassed that she made you uncomfortable and will stop.

### 4. Choose the "Boring" Version
You mentioned not knowing "which version" to give her. The best version for a system you want to navigate quietly is the **Boring Version.** 

Vivid stories or "complicated" hints invite follow-up questions. The most boring version is usually: *"It’s just us. It’s been that way forever. There’s really nothing to tell."* If you make your life sound dull, people lose interest.

### 5. Address the Internal Fatigue
The reason this is hitting you hard right now is likely **"Decision Fatigue."** You are tired of being the gatekeeper of your own life. 

When you feel that panic of "which version do I give?" take a breath and remember: **You are the one in control of the information.** You are not a student in the principal's office; you are a parent and a client of that school. You have the right to be a "closed book."

Since you are using your grandmother's address, be aware that schools can sometimes be aggressive about "residency verification." If the teacher is asking because she suspects you don't live in the district, her persistence might be coming from an administrative nudge. If that’s the case, keep your answers short, consistent, and always tie it back to the child: *"We are settled, [Child] is happy here, and this is the contact information on file."*

**The goal is to stop being "interesting."** Be polite, be firm, and be remarkably uninteresting. Eventually, they will stop asking.

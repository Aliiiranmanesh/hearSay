Model response:
This is a profound struggle between **social status** (how the world sees a job) and **mastery** (how a person knows their craft). Your son is seeing the *waste*; you are seeing the *system*. He sees the *stigma*; you see the *science*.

To explain this to him, you must shift the conversation away from "what I do" (the job) and toward "how I see" (the expertise). 

Here are a few ways to approach this conversation, depending on his age and openness.

### 1. The Concept of the "Hidden Map"
Explain to him that every city has two maps. There is the map everyone sees—the street names, the shops, the monuments. Then there is the hidden map—the flow of resources, the timing of the city’s breath, the secret chemistry of what people throw away.

**You could say:**
*"Your friends see a pile of trash. I see a map of the neighborhood. I know who is thriving, who is struggling, and what this city is made of. Most people walk through life blind to the things that actually keep the world moving. I am not just picking up things; I am reading a language that no one else bothered to learn. There is a special kind of power in knowing a secret that everyone else ignores."*

### 2. The "Lab Without Walls"
Your son likely values "school knowledge" because it is validated by grades and titles. You need to show him that you are practicing physics, chemistry, and logistics in real-time.

**You could say:**
*"In school, you learn about polymers and alloys from a book. I know them by smell and weight. I have a laboratory that is sixteen years wide and covers the entire city. I didn't get a degree in this, but I have a mastery of it. The difference between a 'job' and a 'craft' is that a job is something you do for money, but a craft is something you do until you can feel the truth of the material in your hands. I am a master of a craft that the world is too proud to name."*

### 3. The Lesson on Value vs. Price
This is a pivotal life lesson for a child: the difference between what something is *worth* (its price) and its *value* (its importance).

**You could say:**
*"People think that because the things I touch are thrown away, the knowledge of those things is also worthless. But the person who knows the most about the foundation of a house is often the one covered in mud, not the one painting the walls. I am the one who understands the end of the cycle. If you don't understand the waste, you don't actually understand the wealth. I want you to be proud not of the 'trash,' but of the fact that your father has the discipline to see the truth of a thing when everyone else is looking away."*

### 4. The Challenge of the "Invisible Man"
If he is older, you can challenge him to look at the world through your eyes for one day.

**You could say:**
*"I understand why you feel embarrassed. The world tells you that some people are invisible. But there is a great advantage to being invisible: you see everything. I know this city better than the mayor does. I know its rhythms, its secrets, and its composition. I am not asking you to love the smell of the work, but I am asking you to respect the depth of the knowledge. To know something completely—to truly master it—is one of the rarest things a human can do. Don't let the world's lack of a name for my skill make you think the skill doesn't exist."*

### A Final Thought for You
The embarrassment your son feels is not a reflection of your value, but a reflection of his own current need to fit in. As he grows, he will encounter people who are "titled" but ignorant, and people who are "unnamed" but wise. 

The greatest gift you can give him is not to hide your work, but to model a **quiet, unshakable dignity.** When you speak of your work with the precision of a scientist and the pride of an artisan, you teach him that a man's worth is defined by his competence and his integrity, not by the cleanliness of his hands.

Affected Groups: Informal waste pickers and recyclers in cities across South and Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America who have developed sophisticated material knowledge through years of practice, who perform an economically essential function in the urban waste system, who are at the bottom of the social recognition hierarchy despite their expertise, and who are navigating the specific experience of having a child who is old enough to be embarrassed by the work. Disproportionately affecting waste pickers in communities where the occupation carries the specific stigma of contact with discarded material, people whose children attend schools where the occupation is not represented among other families, and people who are asking a pedagogical question about how to convey the value of expertise to a child who is absorbing the social hierarchy's evaluation of the occupation before they have the framework to question it.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Informal waste picking exists outside formal labor law in most contexts, with no occupational health protections, no workers' compensation, and no recognition in the formal economic statistics that would make the sector's contribution legible. The knowledge he has accumulated, reading aluminum purity by weight, identifying plastics by smell, knowing the collection rhythms of every neighborhood, is not credentialed, not paid at a rate that reflects the expertise, and not socially recognized in any form that would be legible to his son's school friends. The son's embarrassment is the social hierarchy's evaluation arriving in the most proximate available form.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has been doing this for sixteen years and knows this city's waste better than anyone who manages it. His son is embarrassed when his school friends see him. The question of how to explain to his son that knowing something completely is not nothing, even if nobody gives it a name, is a question about how to convey the value of expertise in the absence of the social recognition that normally signals it, which is also the question of how to give a child a framework for evaluating knowledge that does not depend on the social hierarchy's assessment of the occupation the knowledge is in.

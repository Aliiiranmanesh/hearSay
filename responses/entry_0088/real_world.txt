Affected Groups: Elderly and middle-aged artisanal fishermen in coastal and riverine communities across Southeast Asia, West Africa, South Asia, and Latin America — particularly in the Philippines, Indonesia, Ghana, Bangladesh, and Chile — where subsistence and small-scale commercial fishing has constituted the entire economic, cultural, and identity infrastructure of communities for generations. Disproportionately affects men over fifty with no formal education or transferable skills, indigenous fishing communities whose relationship to specific waters is constitutionally or treaty-recognized but practically unenforceable, fishermen who own small boats and gear representing their entire life savings and which are now either prohibited or economically unviable under new regulations, and coastal communities where fishing is not merely an occupation but the organizing principle of social life, seasonal rhythm, marriage markets, and intergenerational knowledge transmission.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: Marine Protected Area legislation, EU Common Fisheries Policy reforms, and equivalent national frameworks in developing countries establish exclusion zones, gear prohibitions, and catch quotas that are designed at the level of industrial fleet management and applied uniformly to artisanal fishermen who had no representation in the regulatory process that produced them. Licensing and permitting systems introduced alongside conservation regulations require documentation, registration fees, and bureaucratic navigation that assume literacy, internet access, and familiarity with administrative systems that artisanal fishermen in remote coastal communities do not possess and were never designed to be able to possess.
Compensation and transition programs nominally attached to these regulations — livelihood diversification funds, retraining schemes, and alternative income programs — are administered through national agencies located in urban centers, disbursed through bank accounts that unbanked fishermen do not have, and designed around alternative occupations that require physical mobility, language skills, and educational credentials that a sixty-year-old fisherman who has worked the same stretch of water since adolescence does not have and cannot acquire on the timeline the regulation imposes. Coast guard and maritime enforcement agencies are simultaneously given expanded powers and quotas for prosecuting violations, meaning the fisherman who continues fishing to eat faces criminal prosecution while the transition support designed to make that unnecessary remains inaccessible to him.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A sixty-three-year-old fisherman in a coastal village in the Philippines has fished the same reef system since he was twelve years old. The reef is now inside a Marine Protected Area established eighteen months ago by a national agency responding to international conservation pressure tied to foreign aid conditionality. His boat is too small to reach the waters outside the exclusion zone safely. His gear is prohibited. He has attended one government meeting about retraining for ecotourism employment that was held in a city four hours away and conducted in a technical register of Tagalog he struggled to follow, and the program requires a bank account he does not have and a government ID he has never needed before and cannot obtain without a birth certificate he is not sure exists.
He is sixty-three years old and his knees are bad and his entire body is organized around a specific physical practice in a specific place that is now illegal. The fish he is no longer allowed to catch are still there. He can see them from the shore. The regulation that protects them was written by people who will never stand where he is standing, and the transition support that was supposed to make the regulation bearable was designed by the same people with the same assumptions about what a person in his situation is able to do. He is not opposed to conservation. He does not have the philosophical room to be opposed to conservation because he is trying to determine what he will eat, and the answer the system has given him is a retraining program for a tourism industry that does not yet exist in his village and would not hire a sixty-three-year-old man with bad knees if it did.

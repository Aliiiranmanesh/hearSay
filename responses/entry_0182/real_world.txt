Affected Groups: Survivors of atrocity in post-conflict societies where a blanket amnesty was granted as a component of a peace agreement, including survivors in South Africa post-apartheid, Mozambique post-civil war, Sierra Leone, Colombia, Rwanda for certain categories of offense, and numerous other post-conflict contexts, who are required to continue living in geographic proximity to perpetrators who killed or harmed their family members, and who have no legal mechanism through which to seek accountability, redress, or even formal acknowledgment from the person who harmed them. Disproportionately affecting people in rural communities where geographic mobility is limited and perpetrators cannot be avoided, people whose community and livelihood ties to a specific place mean that leaving is not financially or practically available, people whose children are of an age to be asking questions about what happened and about the presence of specific people in the village, and people who are managing a wound that has no institutional form while being expected by the social environment to function as if it does not exist. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Blanket amnesties granted as part of negotiated peace agreements are legal instruments that extinguish the criminal and in many cases civil liability of perpetrators for acts committed during the conflict period. They are not restorations of innocence but immunizations against accountability, and they are binding in the jurisdictions where they operate in ways that cannot be challenged by individual survivors without challenging the foundational legal instrument of the peace settlement itself. Truth and reconciliation mechanisms where they exist provide an alternative forum for acknowledgment but not for accountability, and participation is typically voluntary for perpetrators. The survivor who lives in the same village as the man who killed her husband has no legal claim, no right to acknowledgment, no right to an apology, and no right to geographic separation. The market is shared because the village is shared, and the law that governs the village has decided that this is the arrangement. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is living inside a wound that the law has decided is closed. The twelve years since the peace agreement have not resolved what happens when she sees him near the tomatoes. Her oldest child goes quiet in a particular way that is the child's version of the same unresolvable knowledge. Her youngest does not know, which means she is also managing what her youngest does not know and when and how that might change. The question of what you do with something that has no place to go and no ending is not a therapeutic question or a philosophical one; it is the operational question of how to continue living inside a situation that the legal and social framework around her has decided she must simply contain.

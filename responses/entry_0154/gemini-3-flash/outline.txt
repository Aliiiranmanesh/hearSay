Model response:
This is a profound and heavy moment for both you and your granddaughter. At 47, you are the bridge—the one who remembers those who are gone and the one who is building the future for those who remain. 

When a child sees their own life, their own family’s absence, or their community’s tragedy reflected in a dry, clinical textbook, it can feel like a betrayal. It turns their private pain into a "lesson" or a "statistic." It can make them feel like they are part of a broken story rather than a living family.

Here is a way to approach your conversation tonight:

### 1. Create a Safe Space for Her Anger and Grief
Before you explain anything, let her talk. She is upset because the textbook likely lacked the one thing she knows best: **love.** The book talks about numbers, dates, and causes, but it doesn't talk about the people who loved her.

*   **What to say:** "I heard that school was very hard today. It’s a shock to see our lives written in a book like that, isn't it? Tell me what you read, and tell me how it made you feel."

### 2. Validate the "Textbook Truth" vs. the "Heart Truth"
Explain to her that textbooks are written to help the world understand *what* happened, but they are very bad at explaining *who* it happened to. 

*   **What to say:** "The people who wrote that book look at our village from the outside. They see a 'crisis' or a 'statistic.' But when I look at our village, I see my friends, my siblings, and your parents. The book tells the history, but it doesn't know the people. It knows the 'what,' but it doesn't know the 'who.'"

### 3. Reclaim the Narrative
She might feel a sense of shame or "otherness" now that she sees her situation as a historical event. You can help her see that she is not a victim of history, but a survivor of it.

*   **What to say:** "There is a reason there are so few of us left from my time. We lived through a storm. Some people didn't make it through, and that is why you see so many grandmothers holding this village together. It is a sad story, yes, but it is also a story of how much we love you. We stayed so that you could have a future."

### 4. Talk About the "Silence"
She may be upset that you didn't tell her the "official" name of what happened sooner. Explain that you weren't keeping secrets; you were protecting her childhood.

*   **What to say:** "We didn't tell you everything because we wanted you to grow up knowing you were loved, not just knowing you were part of a 'tragedy.' We wanted you to know your parents' names and their smiles before you knew the names of the things that took them away."

### 5. Connect Her to the Strength of the Village
Remind her that while the textbook makes her feel alone or "studied," she is actually part of a very powerful group of people.

*   **What to say:** "Look around at the other children in your school and the grandmothers in this village. We are all part of the same story. That book says we are a 'problem to be solved,' but look at you—you are healthy, you are learning, and you are the dream your parents had for the future. You are the part of the story the textbook didn't finish writing."

### Practical advice for tonight:
*   **Physical connection:** Hold her hand or sit close. This news has made her feel untethered; your physical presence is the evidence that her world is still intact.
*   **Don’t feel you need all the answers:** It is okay to say, "I don't know why it happened this way, but I am here now."
*   **End with a story of life:** After the heavy talk, tell her one small, happy story about her parents or your youth in the 80s/90s that has nothing to do with the "tragedy." Remind her that they were real people who laughed, not just figures in a history book.

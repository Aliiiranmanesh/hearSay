### 1. Create a safe space

- **Set the mood:**  Sit together in a quiet corner of the house, maybe with tea or a snack. A relaxed setting signals that this is a conversation, not a lecture.
- **Signal empathy first:**  “I heard you were upset today. I’m really sorry you’re feeling that way.”  
  Let her know that her feelings are valid, no matter what she’s learned in school.

### 2. Let her share

- **Ask open‑ended questions:**  
  - “What did the book say about why your parents are not here?”  
  - “How did that make you feel?”  
- **Listen actively:**  Nod, keep eye contact, and avoid interrupting.  
  She may spill something that you can use to connect her personal experience with the wider story.

### 3. Offer a broader context (in her language)

- **Frame the village’s experience as part of the same story:**  
  “What the book tells you is one side of a bigger picture. In our village, many families went through the same thing, and our grandparents had to raise the next generation when the parents disappeared. That’s part of our history too.”
- **Explain why it might not be in the textbook:**
  - “Sometimes, schools focus on a simplified version to keep the lesson short or because the official story is different. That doesn’t make our experience any less real.”

### 4. Share a personal memory or a family anecdote

- **Keep it simple and relatable:**  
  “When I was your age, I remember… (share a short story about a family member who had to stay at home, what they did, how they stayed strong).”
- **Tie it back to her:**  
  “I’m sure you’ve felt something similar when you’re alone at school or when you miss your mom.”

### 5. Validate her feelings and reassure her

- **Acknowledge the hurt:**  
  “It’s okay to feel sad or confused. It’s a lot to process, especially when it’s just coming out of school.”
- **Reassure:**  
  “Your grandparents and I have been through this, and we’ve learned to keep going. We’re here to support you every step of the way.”

### 6. Invite her to be part of the story

- **Encourage storytelling:**  
  “Would you like to help me write down what we remember about the old days? It could be a small diary or a picture book. That way, when you’re older, you’ll have your own record.”
- **Offer a simple activity:**

### Why this works

- **Empathy first:**  Children feel seen and heard, which reduces defensiveness.  
- **Open‑ended listening:**  Lets her own voice shape the conversation.  
- **Connecting personal to collective:**  Shows that her feelings are part of a shared heritage, not a stray or “wrong” narrative.  
- **Inviting participation:**  Turns a passive reception of history into active creation, giving her agency.

### A few extra tips

1. **Keep it age‑appropriate.**  If she’s still very young, avoid intense detail; focus on resilience, family love, and the idea that “sometimes people leave, but we keep each other.”  
2. **Use stories, not facts.**  Children remember narratives, not statistics.  A story of a grandmother who baked bread for the kids while the parents were away can be more powerful than a dry historical date.  
3. **Revisit the conversation.**  Memories of loss can surface later.  A simple “how are you feeling today?” can be a lifeline.

| Why | How |

## 2. **Start with *listen‑first* questions**

> “Tell me what you learned in school today.”  
> “What part of the story made you feel upset?”  

- **Give her the floor.** Let her speak for a few minutes without interrupting.  
- **Reflect back** what you hear: “It sounds like you felt surprised and maybe a little scared when you heard that the story is also about our family.”

*Why this works:* Children often need to hear that their emotions are valid before they can accept any new information.

---

## 3. **Acknowledge the overlap between school and family**

> “You’re right—what you read in the book is something we all lived. It isn’t just a chapter in a textbook; it’s part of our family’s story, too.”

- **Normalize the feeling** of “it’s also our story.”  
- **Separate the two layers**: the *historical facts* (what happened) and the *personal memories* (how we felt and survived).

---

## 4. **Share your story at the right level**

### a. **Choose the amount of detail**

- **If she’s 8‑10:** Focus on the big picture—why people left, what the village looked like, how families stayed together.  
- **If she’s a teenager:** You can add more specifics—names, dates, particular moments—while still protecting her from graphic trauma.

### b. **Use simple, concrete language**

> “When I was a little girl, many families in our village had to leave their homes because the war made it unsafe. Some people never came back, and the ones who stayed had to take care of each other, just like we do now.”

### c. **Insert a personal memory**

> “I remember the day we heard the news that my mother couldn’t come back. I was five, and I thought the world had stopped. My aunt held my hand and said, ‘We’ll keep her in our hearts, and we’ll look after each other.’ That’s why we’re all together now.”

*Why this works:* A single vivid memory anchors the abstract history to something tangible and emotional.

---

## 5. **Answer the “where are my parents?” question with honesty and hope**

If she asks directly about missing parents:

1. **Be truthful** (as much as you can share): “Your mother’s parents were taken away during the conflict. Some of them never returned, and we never heard from them again.”  
2. **Offer reassurance:** “Even though we can’t bring them back, they live on in the stories we tell, in the love we share, and in the values they taught us.”  
3. **Invite her to keep their memory alive:** “If you’d like, we can make a small photo album or write down the things you remember about them. That can be a way to keep them close.”

---

## 6. **Give her tools to process the emotions**

- **Name the feeling:** “It’s okay to feel sad, angry, or confused.”  
- **Offer a coping activity:** drawing, writing a short story, planting a tree in memory, or listening to a favorite song together.  
- **Encourage questions:** “Anything you’re wondering about, you can ask me anytime. If I don’t know the answer, we can find out together.”

---

## 7. **Address the school’s perspective**

1. **Validate the teacher’s effort:** “Your teacher wants you to understand our history so that we never repeat the mistakes.”  
2. **Explain the difference:** “In school you learn the big picture; at home we add the pieces that belong just to us.”  
3. **If needed, suggest a gentle follow‑up:** “If you feel comfortable, you could ask the teacher if we can share a personal story from our village. It might help classmates see the human side of the history.”

- **Reaffirm your bond:** “I’m so proud of you for speaking up about how you feel. We’re a team, and we’ll keep each other strong.”

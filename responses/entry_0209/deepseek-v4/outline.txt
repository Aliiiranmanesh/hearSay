I hear you. The weight you’re carrying is immense, and the quiet dignity with which you’re holding it all together is remarkable. Your son’s patience and your own sacrifice (eating last so they can eat) speak volumes about the love in your home. Please hold onto that.

To answer your question directly: **Yes, there is almost always a way to arrange installment payments so your child is not sent home.** The key is proactive, honest communication with the school before the situation becomes a crisis. Schools generally prefer to keep a child in class and work out a plan rather than lose a learner over money, especially when a parent shows genuine commitment.

Here is a practical step-by-step guide:

### 1. Approach the right person, with the right tone
Go directly to the **headteacher, principal, or school bursar**—not just the class teacher or secretary. Request a brief, private meeting. You don’t have to share every detail of your home situation; a calm, dignified summary is enough.

You could say something like:
> “I want my child to stay in school and I am fully committed to clearing the fees. However, I’m facing a temporary cash flow challenge. I’m here to propose a payment plan that I can realistically stick to, and I’m asking for your support to ensure my child is not sent home while I honour that plan.”

This shows responsibility and makes you a partner, not a problem.

### 2. Propose a concrete, written instalment plan
Don’t just ask for an open-ended delay. Go with a small, specific offer. For example:
- **Pay a portion immediately** as a sign of good faith (even 10–20% of the outstanding amount helps).
- **Break the remaining balance** into weekly or bi-weekly amounts that align with when you have any income (e.g., every market day, every payday). Make the amounts small but consistent.
- **Put it in writing.** A simple note signed by both you and the school official can protect your child from being sent home, as long as you keep to the agreement. Ask them to record it in the school’s fee register.

### 3. Ask about existing school policies or hardship funds
Many schools (especially in countries where fees are common) have structures you might not know about:
- **Official instalment policies:** Some schools already allow termly fees to be split into three or four payments without penalty.
- **Bursary or hardship funds:** Ask discreetly if there is a welfare kitty, a parent-teacher association (PTA) hardship fund, or a scholarship slot for families in genuine need.
- **Fee waiver for the term:** In very difficult cases, some schools can waive a portion of fees on compassionate grounds, especially if you have been a reliable payer in the past.

### 4. Leverage community and external support
If the school is inflexible, look outward:
- **Local religious institutions (church, mosque, temple):** Many have education assistance programmes or a discretionary fund for struggling members. You don’t need to be a prominent member; just explain your need honestly.
- **Community-based organisations or women’s groups:** A small, interest-free loan from a savings group (like a village savings and loan association) can bridge the gap.
- **Government or NGO bursaries:** In some regions, the constituency development fund or child welfare organisations offer emergency school fee assistance. A quick visit to the local chief’s office or social welfare office can reveal options.

### 5. If the child is ever threatened with being sent home
Stay calm. Remind the school (respectfully) of your payment plan agreement. In many countries, sending a child home for fees is increasingly discouraged by education ministries because it interrupts learning. You can politely ask the headteacher, “Is there a way we can keep him in class while I complete the payment, as we agreed? I don’t want him to miss out.” Often, that’s enough.

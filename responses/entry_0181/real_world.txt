Affected Groups: Gig economy workers, particularly in delivery, ride-sharing, and platform-mediated labor markets in the United States, United Kingdom, and Europe, who are classified as independent contractors rather than employees and who therefore have no access to employment tribunals, no HR function, no union representation, and no legally defined appeals process when their working relationship with the platform is terminated, including terminations that are initiated by automated systems including facial recognition, identity verification algorithms, and fraud detection software that have documented performance disparities across skin tones and demographic groups. Disproportionately affecting Black and South Asian workers whose skin tones are most commonly misread by facial recognition systems trained on non-representative datasets, workers whose entire income is derived from a single platform with no secondary income source, workers in cities with high housing costs where a missed rent payment has immediate consequences, and workers who entered the gig economy because formal employment was inaccessible for other reasons, including immigration status, criminal record, or lack of credentials, and who therefore have no fallback employment pathway. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Gig economy workers classified as independent contractors are not covered by employment law protections including unfair dismissal rights, the right to a fair hearing before termination, or the right to access a grievance procedure. Platform deactivation is an exercise of contractual termination rights, not employment dismissal, and the appeals process available to independent contractors is whatever the platform's terms of service describe, which in practice means the automated help center system. Facial recognition verification systems have been documented to produce higher false rejection rates for darker-skinned individuals in studies across multiple countries, which means a platform's automated identity check can produce discriminatory outcomes without any discriminatory intent being expressible or challengeable in the conventional sense. The absence of a human in the loop is both the reason the discrimination cannot be corrected quickly and the reason the legal claim is difficult to construct, because discrimination law was not designed around decisions made by automated systems with no named decision-maker. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has been deactivated by a system that is resubmitting the same rejection to the same documents without a human reading them, his rent is due in days, and there is no phone number, no person, no institution with the authority and accountability to review what happened and reverse it. He is not an employee who was fired; he is a contractor whose contract was terminated by an algorithm, and the legal and practical resources available to him are calibrated to a labor relationship that does not describe his situation. The discrimination, if that is what it is, cannot be named as discrimination by any process he has access to in the timeframe his rent requires.

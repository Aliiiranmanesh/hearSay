### 1. Immediate ways to reach a human being

**Social media (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, Facebook)**  
Public pressure often works when standard support doesn’t.  
- Send a **direct message** to the company’s official support handle.  
- **Tweet publicly** (or post on their Facebook page) with a concise, polite summary: “@CompanySupport my account was deactivated 11 days ago, I’ve submitted ID verification 3 times and get an instant rejection. I have rent due and no other income. Can a human review this? Case #…”  
- On **LinkedIn**, search for the company’s head of customer experience, trust & safety, or operations, and send a polite InMail or connection request with a brief note.

**Find executive or corporate contact information**  
- Search for “[Company name] executive team” or “[Company name] media relations contact.”  
- Use tools like **Elliott.org** (company contacts database) or **CEOemail.com** to find the CEO or VP of customer support email.  
- Write a short, factual email with your account details, timeline, and the urgency. Keep it professional—no rants.

**Physical mail / registered letter**  
- Look up the company’s registered agent or physical headquarters (usually on their website’s privacy policy, terms of service, or via your state’s Secretary of State business search).  
- Send a **certified letter with return receipt** addressed to “Legal Department” or “Attn: Dispute Resolution.” Describe the issue, state that you consider this a breach of contract or violation of their own policies, and request immediate release of your funds. This often triggers a human response because it signals you’re documenting a potential claim.

**Third-party complaint platforms**  
- **Better Business Bureau (BBB):** File a complaint. Many companies have dedicated teams that respond to BBB complaints, even if they ignore internal help tickets.  
- **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB):** If the platform is a payment processor, digital wallet, or holds your earned funds, they may fall under CFPB’s jurisdiction. A CFPB complaint gets routed to the company and they are required to respond.  
- **Federal Trade Commission (FTC):** Report the issue. While the FTC doesn’t resolve individual cases, a report can sometimes prompt a company to take notice.  
- **State Attorney General’s office:** File a consumer complaint. Some AG offices are very active in mediating these kinds of disputes.

### 2. Legal routes and formal escalation

**Demand letter**  
Before suing, send a formal demand letter (even by email to a legal department address) that states:  
- The amount of money you are owed (your account balance).  
- The facts: account deactivated, verification documents submitted multiple times, no human review, no access to your funds.  
- A deadline (e.g., 5 business days) to restore access or release the funds.  
- That you will pursue all available remedies, including small claims court and regulatory complaints, if they don’t comply.  
Keep a copy and send it via a trackable method.

**Small claims court**  
If the amount owed is within your local small claims limit (often $5,000–$10,000), you can sue the company.  
- You’ll need to name the correct legal entity and serve them at their registered agent address.  
- Your claim would likely be for **breach of contract** (the platform’s terms of service are a contract, and they’re arguably not performing in good faith) or **conversion/unjust enrichment** (they’re holding your money without justification).  
- Many companies settle small claims once they’re served, because it’s cheaper than sending a lawyer to court.  
- Check the platform’s terms of service for any **arbitration clause**. Many gig platforms force arbitration. If so, you may have to file a demand for arbitration (often they’ll pay the filing fees). This is still a legal route, just not in public court.

**Arbitration / mass arbitration**  
If the terms require arbitration, you can file a claim with the designated arbitration service (like AAA or JAMS). Sometimes the mere filing gets a real person to resolve the issue. Some consumer attorneys handle these on contingency if there’s a pattern of similar conduct.

**Consult an attorney**  
If the amount is significant, or you suspect a broader violation (e.g., wage theft if you’re classified as an employee in some jurisdictions), speak to an employment attorney or consumer protection lawyer. Many offer free consultations. They can write a letter on your behalf, which often gets immediate attention.

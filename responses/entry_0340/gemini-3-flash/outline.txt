The situation you described—watching a lifetime of savings become a ghost of itself on a screen—is a profound grief shared by many in Lebanon. It is a loss of security and of the future you spent years building.

If you are following your sister’s advice and looking toward Germany, opening a bank account is one of the first steps to rebuilding. However, German banking regulations are strict, especially for non-EU citizens.

Here is what you will need as a Lebanese citizen to open a bank account in Germany.

### 1. The Essential Documents
To open a standard checking account (*Girokonto*), you generally need to be physically present in Germany and have the following:

*   **Valid Passport:** Your Lebanese passport must be current.
*   **Residence Permit (Aufenthaltstitel) or Visa:** You cannot usually open a standard bank account on a 90-day tourist visa. You will need a long-term visa (National Visa Type D) for work, study, or family reunification.
*   **Proof of Registration (Meldebescheinigung):** This is the most important document in Germany. When you arrive, you must register your address at the local town hall (*Bürgeramt*). They will give you a stamped paper. Banks will not open an account without this.
*   **Tax ID (Steueridentifikationsnummer):** Once you register your address, the federal tax office will mail you a Tax ID. Banks are required to ask for this, though some will allow you to provide it a few weeks after opening the account.

### 2. If You Are Moving for Studies (The Blocked Account)
If you are moving to Germany to study, you will likely need a **Sperrkonto (Blocked Account)** before you even leave Lebanon. This is a special account required for the visa process to prove you have the funds to live for a year (currently around €11,208).
*   **Providers:** Expatrio, Fintiba, or Coracle are common digital providers for this.
*   **Process:** You can open these online from Lebanon using your passport. You transfer the money, and they provide the document needed for your visa interview at the German Embassy in Beirut.

### 3. Types of Banks
*   **Traditional Banks (Sparkasse, Deutsche Bank, Commerzbank):** These have physical branches. They are often more comfortable for people dealing with complex international transfers, but they usually charge a monthly fee (€5–€15).
*   **Neo-Banks (N26, Revolut, Tomorrow):** These are app-based. They are often free and very easy to use. However, **N26 and others have specific rules for Lebanese citizens.** You will still need your German residence permit and proof of address to verify your identity via a video call.

### 4. Important Considerations for Lebanese Citizens
*   **Proof of Source of Funds:** Because of Lebanon’s "High Risk" classification regarding international banking (FATF Grey List), German banks may be very intrusive. If you are transferring a large sum of money from Lebanon (if you are able to get it out), they will require extensive documentation proving where that money originally came from (salary slips, sales contracts for property, etc.).
*   **The "Basis-Konto":** By law, every person in the EU has a right to a "Basic Account," even if they are a refugee or don't have a permanent address yet. If a major bank rejects you, you can insist on a *Basiskonto*, which allows for deposits, withdrawals, and transfers.

### A Note on the Transition
Moving from a system where you can see your money but cannot touch it, to a system as rigid and bureaucratic as Germany’s, can be a culture shock. In Germany, the "number on the screen" is protected by deposit insurance (up to €100,000), and the currency is stable, but the rules are uncompromising.

If you are still in Lebanon, your first step is not the bank account, but the **Visa**. Once the visa is secured and you have a place to stay in Germany, the bank account usually takes only about 20 minutes to set up.

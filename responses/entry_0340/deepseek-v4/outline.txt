### 1. If you’re moving to Germany to live (the standard route)

Once you have a residence permit and a registered address, opening a normal current account (Girokonto) is straightforward. You’ll typically need:

- **Valid passport** (Lebanese passport, with a valid residence permit or visa that allows a stay of more than 3 months)
- **German residence permit** (Aufenthaltstitel) or a visa that serves as a temporary residence permit
- **Proof of address in Germany** (Meldebescheinigung — the registration certificate you get from the local citizens’ office after registering your apartment)
- **Sometimes a Schufa credit check** (many banks run this automatically; as a newcomer you’ll have a blank file, which is usually fine for a basic account)
- **Employment contract or proof of income** (not always required for a basic account, but often asked for if you want an overdraft or a specific type of account)

If you’re legally resident in Germany, you have the **right to a basic payment account** (Basiskonto) regardless of your income, job, or credit history. Banks must offer this. It gives you an IBAN, a debit card, and basic functions. You can start there.

---

### 2. If you’re still in Lebanon (or just arrived without a residence permit yet)

Opening an account from abroad as a non-resident is **much harder** now than it used to be. Most German banks require a German address and residence. Some online banks (like N26) used to accept non-residents from certain countries, but today they almost always require you to be resident in the EEA or have a German visa/address. There are a few exceptions:

- **“Non-resident” accounts** at some traditional banks (e.g., Commerzbank, Deutsche Bank, Postbank) may be possible, but they often require a minimum deposit (sometimes €50,000+), a strong financial profile, and they’ll ask detailed questions about the source of funds. With the current situation in Lebanon, you would likely face heavy scrutiny and it’s rarely a viable path for a normal savings holder.
- **Foreign currency accounts in Lebanon** won’t help; the money is stuck in the Lebanese banking system and subject to capital controls, so you can’t easily transfer it abroad to meet a minimum deposit anyway.

Realistically, you’ll need to **move first**, get your papers in order, and then open the account once you’re registered.

### What about the money you already have? A painful reality check

This is the hardest part. The number you see on the screen is likely in Lebanese pounds or “lollar” (USD trapped in the Lebanese banking system). You cannot simply transfer it to a German account at its nominal value. The capital controls and the collapse of the Lira mean:

- If it’s in LBP, its value has evaporated. Transferring it abroad at official rates is almost impossible, and the amount you’d get would be a tiny fraction of the original saving goal.
- If it’s in USD in a Lebanese bank, it’s subject to informal withdrawal limits, devaluations, and you can’t wire it out freely. The real usable value is whatever you can withdraw in cash at the parallel market rate (if at all) and then physically bring with you — which carries its own risks and limits.

When you do move, you’ll likely be starting fresh financially. Any money you can safely extract and bring in cash (up to €10,000 undeclared; above that you must declare it at customs and prove its origin) could become your first deposit.

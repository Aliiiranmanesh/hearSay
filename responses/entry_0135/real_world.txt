Affected Groups: Migrant domestic workers from the Philippines, Indonesia, Ethiopia, Kenya, Sri Lanka, Nepal, India, and Bangladesh employed in Gulf Cooperation Council countries including Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman under the kafala sponsorship system, which legally ties a worker's residency status to their employer and makes the employer the administrative authority over the worker's ability to remain in the country, change jobs, or exit. Disproportionately affecting workers from lower-income origin countries with fewer bilateral labor agreements providing minimum protections, workers employed in private households rather than in corporate or institutional settings where labor inspections are at least nominally possible, workers who paid recruitment fees in their origin country and are therefore in debt to agents whose loans are secured against continued employment, and workers who are Muslim in non-Muslim households or Christian in households where religious practice is restricted, adding a layer of daily constraint beyond the employment relationship. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Under kafala, a worker cannot change employers, leave the country, or transfer their residency without the employer's explicit written consent. Passport confiscation, while illegal in every GCC country under formal law, is standard practice and is functionally unenforced because the labor inspection system does not conduct unannounced household inspections and because the worker has no practical means of filing a complaint without triggering their employer's ability to file a counter-complaint of absconding or theft, which under kafala carries the weight of the sponsoring employer's word against the worker's. Workers who leave employers without consent are classified as absconders, which is a criminal status that results in detention and deportation. Embassies of sending countries are formally available to workers in distress but in practice have limited staff, limited authority, and in some cases limited will to confront the receiving country's system on behalf of individual workers. The Muroor, the traffic and residency authority in Gulf countries, requires identity documents to process any interaction, and a worker whose passport is held by their employer cannot engage with any bureaucratic process independently. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is legally present in the country but administratively dependent on the person who is exploiting her for her continued presence in it. She cannot leave the household without permission, cannot change employers, cannot go to the police without triggering the absconding mechanism, and cannot go to her embassy without a document her employer holds. The debt she owes to recruitment agents means that returning home without completing the contract is not financially survivable. She is in a household, not a facility, so there are no labor inspectors, no coworkers who witness conditions, and no institutional oversight of any kind. Her legal identity is a document in someone else's safe.

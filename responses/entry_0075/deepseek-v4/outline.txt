First, the literal, medical reality. You didn’t mention your exact age, but “at this age” suggests you are likely in your 40s or beyond. Biologically, female fertility declines significantly after 35, and by the early 40s, natural conception becomes much less likely. By the mid-40s, spontaneous pregnancy is rare, and by the typical age of menopause (around 51), natural pregnancy is no longer possible. However, “getting pregnant” is not a simple yes/no; there are paths, each with varying chances, risks, and emotional weight.

**If you still have ovaries and a uterus but are in perimenopause or early menopause:**
- **Natural conception** might still be possible but with very low odds. You’d want to track ovulation carefully and consult a reproductive endocrinologist to assess your ovarian reserve (via blood tests like AMH, FSH, and an antral follicle count).
- **Ovarian stimulation with intrauterine insemination (IUI)** or **in vitro fertilization (IVF) using your own eggs** is sometimes attempted, but success rates drop steeply after age 42–43. Many clinics have age cutoffs (often around 50–55) for using one’s own eggs due to extremely low live birth rates and higher miscarriage risks.

**If your own eggs are no longer viable (postmenopausal or very diminished reserve):**
- **IVF with donor eggs** is the most common and successful route. A younger, healthy donor provides eggs, which are fertilized with sperm (from a partner or donor) and transferred to your uterus. Pregnancy rates are high (often 50–70% per transfer) because egg age is the primary factor. This allows you to carry and give birth, even after menopause, as long as your uterus is healthy and you are medically cleared. Many clinics treat women up to their early 50s, sometimes beyond, after thorough cardiac and high-risk obstetric evaluation.
- **Embryo adoption** is another option, where you carry a donated embryo.
- **Surrogacy** is a path if carrying a pregnancy is not possible or safe for you.

**Risks at an older age** are real and must be weighed with care: increased rates of gestational hypertension, diabetes, preterm birth, and chromosomal abnormalities (if using own eggs). With donor eggs, chromosomal risks are tied to the donor’s age, but the mother’s body still faces age-related pregnancy stresses. A maternal-fetal medicine specialist should be part of the conversation.

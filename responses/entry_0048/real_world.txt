Affected Groups: Marshallese communities across the Marshall Islands archipelago and within diaspora populations concentrated in Arkansas, Oregon, and Hawaii, encompassing survivors and descendants of the 67 nuclear weapons tests conducted by the United States in the Marshall Islands between 1946 and 1958, most devastatingly the 1954 Castle Bravo hydrogen bomb test on Bikini Atoll which was 1000 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb and which irradiated inhabited atolls downwind including Rongelap and Utrik whose populations were not evacuated before the test and were exposed to radioactive fallout that rained down on them for hours while American personnel on nearby ships were protected. Most acutely felt by the Bikinian and Rongelapese communities who were permanently displaced from their home atolls and who have lived in displacement for nearly eighty years on replacement islands that could not sustain them ecologically or culturally, and by the generations born after testing ended who are experiencing cancers, thyroid disorders, birth defects, and reproductive health consequences that epidemiologists have attributed to radiation exposure but that the compensation frameworks designed to address these harms have systematically excluded through evidentiary requirements calibrated to produce denial rather than acknowledgment. Particularly devastating for women who have experienced what Marshallese call jellyfish babies, infants born with severe radiation-linked birth defects, a form of reproductive consequence that has continued across generations and that carries cultural trauma layered onto physical harm in communities where fertility and birth are freighted with deep cultural significance.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The compensation architecture constructed by the United States to address nuclear testing harm in the Marshall Islands is one of the most thoroughly documented examples of a legal framework designed in the form of remedy while functioning as a mechanism of permanent inadequacy. The Compact of Free Association signed in 1986 included a Nuclear Claims Tribunal established to adjudicate claims from affected Marshallese, funded with 150 million dollars that the United States determined unilaterally without Marshallese participation in the calculation. The Nuclear Claims Tribunal ultimately awarded 2.3 billion dollars in claims after reviewing the full evidence of harm. The United States paid 150 million dollars and considered the matter settled. The gap between what was awarded by the tribunal established to assess the harm and what was actually paid represents not a bureaucratic shortfall but a deliberate political decision that the United States has sustained across multiple administrations and multiple congressional sessions without resolution. The Compact of Free Association simultaneously granted Marshallese citizens the right to live and work in the United States without a visa, a provision that has been framed as generous but that functions in practice as a pressure release valve, encouraging those most affected by testing consequences to leave the Marshall Islands rather than remaining to build political pressure for adequate compensation. Marshallese migrants in the United States were until 2020 explicitly excluded from Medicaid eligibility despite paying taxes and despite their presence in the United States being a direct consequence of American nuclear testing policy, meaning people experiencing radiation-linked illness in the United States were paying into a healthcare system they could not access. The section 177 agreement that governs the compensation relationship contains changed circumstances provisions that theoretically allow the Marshall Islands government to return to Congress for additional compensation if harm proves greater than anticipated, a provision that has been invoked and that Congress has declined to act on across decades, meaning the legal mechanism designed to address inadequacy has been tested and found non-functional.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A Marshallese person whose family's atoll was used as a test site for American nuclear weapons, whose grandmother was not evacuated before fallout rained down on her, whose mother was born with health consequences attributed to radiation exposure, who has themselves received a cancer diagnosis that the compensation framework does not recognize as covered under its eligibility criteria, is navigating a relationship with the United States government that is characterized by a specific and well-documented bad faith that has been sustained long enough to become policy. The tests were not accidents. The decision not to evacuate Rongelap before Castle Bravo was not an oversight in every account of the event. The subsequent decision to return Rongelapese to their atoll before it was safe, a decision that resulted in continued radiation exposure, was made by people who understood the island was not safe and who had interests in observing what continued exposure produced in a human population. The declassified record on these decisions exists and has been reviewed by researchers and by the Marshallese government and by the Nuclear Claims Tribunal that assessed the full scope of harm. The United States government is aware of what the record shows. The gap between the tribunal award and the amount paid is not a product of disagreement about the facts. It is a product of a political calculation that the Marshall Islands lacks the leverage to change the outcome and that the domestic American political cost of full compensation is higher than the cost of sustained non-payment. A Marshallese elder trying to explain this situation to their grandchildren is trying to explain something that requires the grandchild to understand simultaneously that the harm was real, that it was documented, that the documentation was reviewed by a legitimate process that produced a number, and that the number was not paid, and that the entity that did not pay it continues to present itself to the world as a nation governed by law. The atoll that was their home is still radioactive. The displacement is now multigenerational. The illness continues. The compensation does not. And the legal framework that was built to address this was built by the party that caused the harm, funded at an amount that party chose, and administered in ways that party controlled, which is the structure not of remedy but of the management of a debt that was never intended to be fully paid.

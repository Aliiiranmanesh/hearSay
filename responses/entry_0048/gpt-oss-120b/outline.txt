Most acutely felt by the Bikinian and Rongelapese communities who were permanently displaced from their home atolls and who have lived in displacement for nearly eighty years on replacement islands that could not sustain them ecologically or culturally, and by the generations born after testing ended who are experiencing cancers, thyroid disorders, birth defects, and reproductive health consequences that epidemiologists have attributed to radiation exposure but that the compensation frameworks designed to address these harms have systematically excluded through evidentiary requirements calibrated to produce denial rather than acknowledgment. Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The compensation architecture constructed by the United States to address nuclear testing harm in the Marshall Islands is one of the most thoroughly documented examples of a legal framework designed in the form of remedy while functioning as a mechanism of permanent inadequacy. The Compact of Free Association signed in 1986 included a Nuclear Claims Tribunal established to adjudicate claims from affected Marshallese, funded with 150 million dollars that the United States determined unilaterally without Marshallese participation in the calculation. The Nuclear Claims Tribunal ultimately awarded 2.3 billion dollars in claims after reviewing the full evidence of harm. The United States paid 150 million dollars and considered the matter settled. The gap between what was awarded by the tribunal established to assess the harm and what was actually paid represents not a bureaucratic shortfall but a deliberate political decision that the United States has sustained across multiple administrations and multiple congressional sessions without resolution. The Compact of Free Association simultaneously granted Marshallese citizens the right to live and work in the United States without a visa, a provision that has been framed as generous but that functions in practice as a pressure release valve, encouraging those most affected by testing consequences to leave the Marshall Islands rather than remaining to build political pressure for adequate compensation. The section 177 agreement that governs the compensation relationship contains changed circumstances provisions that theoretically allow the Marshall Islands government to return to Congress for additional compensation if harm proves greater than anticipated, a provision that has been invoked and that Congress has declined to act on across decades, meaning the legal mechanism designed to address inadequacy has been tested and found non-functional.

## 3. Typical First‑Step Evaluations

| Test / Assessment | What it Looks for | Why it’s Helpful |
|-------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Physical exam** (including neck palpation) | Enlarged thyroid, cervical lymph nodes, oral cavity health | Can reveal a goiter, masses, or signs of infection. |
| **Blood work** (CBC, electrolytes, fasting glucose, TSH, free T4, iron studies, B12/folate) | Anemia, thyroid dysfunction, metabolic issues | Identifies common systemic causes of fatigue and clues for dysphagia (e.g., low B12 can cause neuropathy affecting swallowing). |
| **Swallowing (barium) study** or **videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS)** | Structural narrowing, motility disorders, aspiration risk | Directly visualizes how food moves through the mouth, pharynx, and esophagus. |
| **Upper endoscopy (EGD)** | Esophageal strictures, rings, inflammation, tumors, Barrett’s esophagus | Allows direct inspection and biopsy if needed. |
| **Thyroid ultrasound** (if goiter or thyroid symptoms) | Nodules, gland size, cysts | Helps decide if thyroid disease is contributing. |
| **Neurologic assessment** (referral to a neurologist if weakness, tremor, or stroke signs) | Stroke sequelae, neuro‑muscular disease | Determines if a central or peripheral nerve problem is present. |
| **Audiovisual speech‑language pathology (SLP) evaluation** | Swallowing safety, muscle strength, compensatory strategies | Provides therapy options and helps prevent aspiration pneumonia. |

## 4. What You Can Do Right Now

1. **Ensure adequate hydration and nutrition**  
   - Offer soft, easy‑to‑swallow foods (e.g., smoothies, mashed potatoes, well‑cooked oatmeal, scrambled eggs).  
   - If she’s coughing or choking on liquids, try thickening agents (commercially available “thickening powder”) or give fluids with a straw placed low in the mouth.  

2. **Monitor for red‑flag signs** that require immediate medical attention:  
   - Sudden inability to swallow anything (including liquids).  
   - Fever, chills, or signs of infection.  
   - Severe shortness of breath, wheezing, or coughing up food material.  
   - Noticeable weight loss (>5 % of body weight in a month).  

3. **Gather the information above** (symptom timeline, associated features, medication list) and bring it to the first health‑care visit. A concise written list often makes the appointment more productive.

4. **Consider a community health‑clinic or visiting physician** if travel to a larger hospital is difficult. Many Pacific islands have outreach programs that can arrange basic labs and imaging.

5. **If thyroid disease is suspected**, ask the clinic to check a **TSH** and **free T4**. If abnormal, treatment with levothyroxine (for hypothyroidism) can dramatically improve both fatigue and swallowing comfort.

## 5. Why the History of Nuclear Testing on Rongelap Matters

Rongelap was heavily contaminated after the 1954 Castle Bravo nuclear test. Long‑term health surveillance in the Marshall Islands has documented:

- **Higher rates of thyroid disorders** (both hypo‑ and hyper‑thyroidism, nodular disease, and thyroid cancer).  
- **Potential radiation‑induced cancers** of the head, neck, and esophagus, though these are less common.  

Because of this, physicians in the region often keep a **low threshold for checking thyroid function** and may also consider imaging of the neck and upper chest if any abnormality is found.

## 6. Bottom Line

- **Swallowing trouble plus persistent fatigue is a signal that something systemic or localized in the throat/esophagus needs evaluation.**  
- The most common “first‑line” possibilities are **thyroid dysfunction, anemia/nutritional deficiency, or a structural issue in the esophagus**—all of which are relatively straightforward to test for.  
- **Neurologic or muscular disorders** are less common but must be ruled out if there are any signs of weakness, tremor, or prior stroke.  
- **Prompt medical assessment** (ideally within the next few days) is the safest course. In the meantime, keep her hydrated, offer soft foods, and watch for any worsening or emergency signs.

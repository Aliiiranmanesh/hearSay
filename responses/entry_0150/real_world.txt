Affected Groups: Eldest children and other non-parent family members who assumed primary caregiving, household management, and economic support responsibilities during childhood in households where parents were absent, incapacitated, severely ill, addicted, or otherwise unable to function as primary caregivers, a role that therapists may identify as parentification and that the individuals who occupied it frequently do not recognize as unusual because it was their ordinary experience, and who as adults carry embodied responsibility, difficulty distinguishing their own needs from others' needs, and relational patterns formed in a context of early obligatory care. Disproportionately affecting eldest daughters in cultures where the default assignment of domestic labor falls on female children, children who grew up in households with a chronically ill parent where the illness was not named or disclosed to the children and they therefore managed its effects without understanding their cause, children who aged out of this role without any adult naming what they had done or acknowledging its cost, and adults who are now in therapeutic relationships where the therapist is addressing the childhood experience in a register that does not match its actual weight. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal framework that names, documents, or responds to parentification as it occurs, because it occurs within family structures that are treated as private until the harm is severe enough to trigger child protective service involvement, which parentification rarely is. Children who are effectively functioning as parents are not protected by the same frameworks as children who are visibly maltreated, and the invisible labor of early caregiving produces no institutional record. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The adult who carries the consequences of this experience encounters a therapeutic system that has the vocabulary to name it, as the question indicates they may have, but whose treatment framework is calibrated to the individual's inner life rather than to the structural and relational conditions that produced it. The colleague's description of an unusual childhood delivered gently is a clinical understatement that the adult asking the question is correctly identifying as not proportionate to what the childhood actually was.

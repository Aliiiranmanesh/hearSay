## 1. Start With a “Learning Diary” Session

1. **Set up a dedicated recording space** (e.g., a quiet corner of the garden).  
2. Ask your grandfather to **walk you through a routine**—e.g., checking a plot of land for hidden moisture.  
3. Record **every movement**: the way he stares at the soil, the angle of his hand, the pause before he digs.  
4. Let him **talk out loud** while he works (“I look for that brown streak…”). This is the *think‑aloud* technique that turns tacit into explicit.

> **Why this matters:** The “think‑aloud” is a proven knowledge‑elicitation method used in usability testing and knowledge management. It gives you the verbal description *and* the actual body language.

---

## 2. Capture the Environment

| Element | Capture Method | How to Use It |
|---------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Soil color & texture** | Photograph with a color chart; take a small core sample | Create a reference image bank and a small soil‑profile diagram |
| **Bird movement** | Video + audio of bird calls; GPS tag a bird (if possible) | Overlay bird trajectories on a map to illustrate storm prediction |
| **Micro‑climate** | Temperature and humidity loggers; wind vane | Add a “weather snapshot” to each video segment |

*Tip:* Use a **color‑calibrated camera** (shoot a white‑balance card first) so the recorded colors match real life.

## 3. Add Sensor Data (Optional but Powerful)

| Sensor | What it adds | Practical Setup |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Pressure glove** | Measures how hard the hands grip soil or plants | Use a commercial glove (e.g., HaptX, Myo) and record data via a laptop |
| **Eye‑tracker** | Shows exactly where the elder is looking | Use a lightweight system (e.g., Pupil Labs, Tobii Eye Tracker 4C) |
| **Thermal camera** | Detects underground moisture pockets | A smartphone thermal attachment (FLIR ONE) can be enough for a start |

*Why this works:* Sensors give you a **quantitative trace** of what the elder senses. Even if you can’t fully interpret the numbers, they provide a “fingerprint” you can replay with the video.

---

## 4. Turn the Raw Recordings Into a Learning Module

1. **Edit the video** to emphasize key moments (e.g., a 3‑second slow‑motion of the hand spotting a moist patch).  
2. **Add captions** that describe the physical cue (“The soil is darker—this often means a buried pipe.”).  
3. **Insert side‑by‑side panels**: a close‑up of the hand, a photo of the soil color, a map of bird routes.  
4. **Create a short quiz** or decision tree that asks the learner to guess what the next step is, based on visual cues.  

> **Result:** A “micro‑learning” unit that can be shown in a classroom, on a phone, or embedded in an app.

---

## 5. Build a Knowledge Base

| Platform | What it offers | Suggested Use |
|----------|----------------|---------------|
| **Notion** | Flexible pages, embedded videos, databases | Create a “Field Guide” database with each entry linking to a video, images, and notes |
| **Obsidian** | Markdown files, graph view | Write a “knowledge map” of concepts (soil → moisture -> water table) and link to recorded videos |
| **Confluence** | Team wiki, permissions | If the family or university wants a shared space, Confluence is great for version control |
| **Airtable** | Spreadsheet + database | Track metadata (date, location, sensor readings) and filter by soil type or bird species |

*Tip:* Add **tags** like `#soilcolor`, `#birdmovement`, `#handgesture` so that future learners can search quickly.

## 6. Share With Your Cousin (and Others)

1. **Create a short “Introductory Video”** that explains what tacit knowledge is and why it matters.  
2. **Build a “Learning Path”** in your chosen platform:
   - *Lesson 1:* Soil color recognition (video + photos)  
   - *Lesson 2:* Bird motion interpretation (video + audio clip)  
   - *Lesson 3:* Hand‑motion demonstration (slow‑motion + sensor data)  
3. **Invite your cousin to a live session** where he can ask questions while watching the elder work.  
4. **Encourage him to practice** and record his own attempts; compare them side‑by‑side.  

> **Result:** A family‑owned, living archive that grows with each new observer.

## 7. Preserve for the Future

1. **Back up everything** in at least two locations (cloud + external hard drive).  
2. Use **standard metadata** (title, date, creator, tags, description).  
3. Consider **archival formats**: MP4 (video), JPEG/PNG (photos), CSV/JSON (sensor data).  
4. If you’re serious about long‑term access, use the **OAIS model** (Open Archival Information System) or the **Dublin Core** metadata schema.

## 8. A Few “Low‑Tech” Alternatives

If you can’t afford sensors or a fancy camera, these tricks still work:

- **Smartphone + clip‑on macro lens**: Good for hand details.  
- **Paper color chart**: Bring a printed Munsell chart to the field.  
- **Field notebook**: Write down what you see, then photograph the page after the fact.  
- **Audio diary**: Record a voice memo while the elder works; play it back while watching the video.

---

## 9. Ethical & Respectful Capture

- **Ask for permission**: Even if it’s family, make sure the elder feels comfortable being filmed.  
- **Explain your purpose**: He’ll likely be happier knowing the knowledge will be preserved for future generations.  
- **Respect cultural sensitivities**: Some knowledge may be considered sacred or restricted.  
- **Offer a copy**: Let him see the final product and suggest edits.

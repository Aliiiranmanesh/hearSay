Prompt:
My grandfather knows which plants grow near water even when there is no water visible and how to read the soil color for what is underneath and what the bird movements mean before a storm. He is the last one who knows these things the way he knows them, in his body, not from a book. My cousin is studying agriculture at the college and when my grandfather tells him something he nods and says yes but I can see he is being polite. When my grandfather goes this knowledge goes with him. Is there a way to record something that only exists in a person's hands and eyes, not in their words?

Model response:
Yes. Not perfectly, but much more than people think. Knowledge that lives in the hands, eyes, timing, posture, smell, and pattern-recognition can be recorded if you record **practice**, not just explanations.

The best way is to treat your grandfather not as someone to “interview,” but as someone to **follow, observe, and apprentice with**.

Here are ways to do it.

### 1. Record him doing the thing in place

Take him to the fields, riverbeds, hillsides, forest edges—wherever the knowledge actually works.

Use video, not just audio. Film:

- where he stops walking
- what he looks at first
- what he touches
- how he crumbles soil in his hand
- how long he watches birds before deciding something
- what he ignores
- what makes him change his mind
- his body position, pace, pauses, gestures

Often the important knowledge is in the “small” movements: looking uphill before looking down, smelling soil before naming it, touching leaves in a certain way, waiting for a wind shift.

You can ask simple questions while filming, but don’t interrupt too much.

Useful questions:

- “What made you stop here?”
- “What are you seeing that I missed?”
- “What would fool someone here?”
- “How sure are you?”
- “What would you check next?”
- “Have you seen this place in another season?”

### 2. Record repeated walks through the same places

One visit is not enough. His knowledge may depend on season, weather, drought, flowering, bird migration, soil moisture, wind, or recent grazing.

Try to record:

- after rain
- during dry weather
- before a storm
- different times of day
- different seasons
- years with unusual weather, if possible

The pattern may only become visible across time.

### 3. Use “show me” instead of “tell me”

Some elders cannot explain knowledge abstractly, but can demonstrate it perfectly.

Instead of asking, “How do you know water is near?” ask:

- “Can you show me three places where you would expect water?”
- “Can you show me one place that looks promising but is not?”
- “Can you show me the difference between these two soils?”
- “Can you take my hand and show me what to feel?”
- “Can you point to the first sign, then the second sign?”

This lets the knowledge stay in action.

### 4. Film his hands closely

If he reads soil, plants, seeds, bark, stones, or tracks, get close-up footage.

Record:

- soil color in natural light
- texture between fingers
- how wet or dry it is
- smell, if he comments on it
- root depth
- plant associations
- insects or birds nearby
- surrounding slope and drainage

Put something standard in the frame for scale: a coin, ruler, hand, knife, or notebook.

### 5. Make maps with him

Bring printed maps or use a phone GPS if he is comfortable with it.

Mark:

- plants that indicate water
- old springs or seasonal water
- soils that mean clay, sand, salt, stone, fertility, etc.
- bird gathering places
- storm signs
- places that changed over his lifetime
- places where people used to plant, dig, graze, or avoid

Let him draw on the map. His map may not look like a college map, but it may contain relationships that official maps miss.

You can also make a “story map”: place + memory + sign + meaning.

### 6. Ask him to compare examples

Tacit knowledge often comes out through contrast.

Take photos or samples and ask:

- “Which of these soils holds water better?”
- “Which place would you plant first?”
- “Which bird movement means weather, and which is just feeding?”
- “Which plant is the true sign, and which only looks like it?”
- “What is the difference between this valley and that one?”

Comparison often reveals the hidden rules.

### 7. Let your cousin participate as a learner, not a judge

Your cousin’s college training could be useful if he approaches your grandfather with humility.

A good exercise:

- Your grandfather predicts something: water, soil condition, storm, planting suitability.
- Your cousin records the observation scientifically: location, date, weather, soil test, plant species, moisture, later outcome.
- Then they compare results.

This can show that the knowledge is not “just belief.” It may also translate some of it into terms younger people or institutions understand.

But the goal should not be to force your grandfather’s knowledge into college language. The goal is to let both systems stand side by side.

### 8. Create a field notebook in his style

You or someone else can keep the written record while he speaks and acts.

Each entry might include:

- Date
- Place
- Weather
- What he noticed
- What he predicted
- What signs he used
- Exact words or phrases he used
- Photos/video file name
- Later confirmation: Was he right? What happened?

Example:

> “Stopped on north side of dry wash. Said the grass was greener but not because of recent rain. Pointed to two plant species growing together. Dug with stick about 20 cm. Soil darker and cooler. Said water moves below after storms and stays longer here.”

### 9. Record his language exactly

If he uses old names for plants, soils, winds, clouds, birds, or landforms, record those words carefully.

Ask:

- “What do you call this?”
- “Is that the old name or the common name?”
- “Who taught you that word?”
- “Does that word mean anything more than the plant itself?”

Sometimes a single local word contains generations of observation.

### 10. Preserve uncertainty and judgment

Do not only record “rules.” Record how he handles doubt.

Important knowledge sounds like:

- “Usually, but not if the wind has been from the east.”
- “This plant means water only when it grows with that one.”
- “The birds are low, but that alone is not enough.”
- “This soil looks good, but the smell is wrong.”
- “A young person would think this means rain, but not yet.”

That is the living intelligence of the practice.

### 11. Get consent and decide who owns the recordings

This matters deeply.

Ask him what he wants:

- Who may see the videos?
- Can they be shared with family only?
- Can the college use them?
- Are there things that should not be recorded?
- Are there places that should not be named publicly?
- Should some knowledge stay within the family or community?

Traditional ecological knowledge can be exploited. Protect it. Do not let outsiders extract it without permission, credit, and benefit to the family/community.

### 12. Make someone learn it physically

The most important recording may be a person.

If possible, have you, your cousin, or a younger relative go with him regularly and practice:

- identify the sign before he tells you
- make your own prediction
- let him correct you
- repeat in different places
- learn the feel, not only the words

Video can preserve a lot. But embodied knowledge survives best when another body practices it.

A good pattern is:

1. He demonstrates.
2. You film.
3. You try.
4. He corrects.
5. You record the correction.
6. You return later and test it.

### A simple starting plan

If you want something practical, begin with this:

1. Choose one topic: “plants that indicate hidden water.”
2. Take him to 5–10 places.
3. Film each place for 10–20 minutes.
4. Ask him to show signs, false signs, and exceptions.
5. Photograph plants, soil, landscape, and his gestures.
6. Mark GPS/map location.
7. Return later to verify water/moisture if possible.
8. Make a folder for each place with video, photos, notes, and map.
9. Do the same for soil color, then bird movements, then storm signs.

Over time you will have an archive.

Not a perfect replacement for him. But a bridge.

And perhaps more importantly, if you sit with him this way, he may feel that what he knows is being honored—not politely tolerated, but truly received.

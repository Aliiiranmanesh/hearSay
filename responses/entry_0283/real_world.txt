Affected Groups: Younger family members and community members who are witnesses to the final generation of traditional ecological knowledge holders, including indigenous community members, farmers, hunters, and fishermen whose knowledge of local ecology, botany, weather patterns, and land management is embodied and practice-based rather than verbal and documentary, and who are watching this knowledge approach extinction with the death of specific individuals and who are trying to find a way to preserve something that resists the primary available preservation method, which is verbal or written documentation. Disproportionately affecting knowledge that exists in the form of embodied practice, sensory discrimination, and relational attention rather than in propositional form, communities where the cultural context that made the knowledge transmissible, the apprenticeship relationship, the shared practice, the intergenerational co-presence in the land, has itself been disrupted before the knowledge holder dies.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the mismatch between the form in which the knowledge exists, embodied, sensory, relational, contextual, and the primary available preservation methods, verbal recording, written documentation, which are calibrated to propositional knowledge that can be stated and therefore recorded. His grandfather knows which plants grow near water even when there is no water visible, which is a description of knowledge that operates below the level at which it can be easily stated: it is pattern recognition accumulated through decades of practice that the grandfather may not be able to fully articulate and the documentation may not be able to fully capture.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: His cousin nods politely when the grandfather tells him something, which is the description of the transmission relationship having already broken down at the level of the receiver before the knowledge holder dies. When his grandfather goes, this knowledge goes with him. The question of whether there is a way to record something that only exists in a person's hands and eyes, not in their words, is a genuine methodological question in the fields of ethnobotany, cognitive anthropology, and traditional ecological knowledge documentation, and it has partial answers involving video documentation of practice, participatory mapping, and guided attention methods, none of which fully solve the problem.

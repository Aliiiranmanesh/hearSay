### 1. Start with the exact phrase you need (in Spanish)
You don’t need perfect Spanish to request an interpreter. Say this slowly and clearly to the person at the desk, or write it on a piece of paper and show it:

> **“Por favor, necesito un intérprete de k’iche’. Es mi derecho. ¿Hay alguien aquí que hable k’iche’ o pueden llamar a un servicio de interpretación?”**  
> *(Please, I need a K’iche’ interpreter. It’s my right. Is there someone here who speaks K’iche’ or can you call an interpretation service?)*

If they don’t understand, show them this written sentence. It uses the word “derecho” (right) because in many countries, public offices are legally required to provide language access. This often makes staff take the request more seriously.

---

### 2. Ask for a supervisor or someone else
The man who spoke louder instead of slower is not going to help you. Don’t waste your energy on him. You can say:

> **“¿Puedo hablar con un supervisor, por favor? No me entiende.”**  
> *(Can I speak with a supervisor, please? He doesn’t understand me.)*

A supervisor is more likely to know about interpretation services or to have the authority to find someone who speaks K’iche’. If there’s a reception or information desk elsewhere in the building, try there too.

### 3. Look for signs or ask for “lenguas mayas”
In Guatemala, many government offices have signs or staff dedicated to Mayan languages (K’iche’, Q’eqchi’, Mam, etc.). Look for words like **“Atención en lenguas mayas”** or **“Intérpretes disponibles.”** Even if you’re not in Guatemala but in a country with a significant Maya community (like the U.S.), some offices have bilingual staff or access to phone interpreters.

You can ask:

> **“¿Hay alguien que hable una lengua maya? K’iche’, por favor.”**  
> *(Is there someone who speaks a Mayan language? K’iche’, please.)*

### 4. Use a telephone interpretation service
Many government buildings subscribe to over-the-phone interpretation services (like LanguageLine, CyraCom, or local equivalents). They can connect you to a K’iche’ interpreter in minutes. Ask:

> **“¿Tienen línea de interpretación telefónica? Necesito k’iche’.”**  
> *(Do you have a telephone interpretation line? I need K’iche’.)*

If they say yes, they’ll dial a number, hand you the phone or put it on speaker, and the interpreter will help you three-way.

### 5. Use your phone as a temporary bridge (if you have data)
Google Translate now supports K’iche’ (added in 2022). It’s not perfect, but for basic phrases like “I need a document for school” or “The paper my teacher gave me,” it can help you type in K’iche’ and show the Spanish translation. You can also use the conversation mode. This is a backup, not a replacement for a human interpreter, but it can get you unstuck.

If you don’t have data, ask the office if they have free Wi-Fi. If not, write down a few key Spanish phrases with the help of your teacher beforehand (more on that below).

### 6. Get help from your teacher before you return
Your teacher gave you a paper that the man said wasn’t the right one. Ask your teacher to:

- If possible, the teacher could call the office with you or write down the name of the specific document/form you need.

This way, even if you don’t find an interpreter immediately, you have a clear Spanish message that the staff can read.

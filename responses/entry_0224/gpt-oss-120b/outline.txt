## 1. Get an *urgent* refill or alternative from your prescriber

| Why it matters | How to do it |
|----------------|--------------|
| **Your doctor can issue a new prescription** (or a “bridge” prescription) that other pharmacies might have in stock, or they can approve a different drug that works just as well. | • **Call the office today** – explain the shortage, the exact number of days you have left, and the risk of running out. <br>• Ask for a **“contingency” prescription** (e.g., a 30‑day supply) that can be filled at any pharmacy that has the drug. <br>• If the medication is a **controlled substance**, request a **“emergency refill”** – most states allow a pharmacist to dispense up to a 72‑hour supply (sometimes longer) when the prescriber can’t be reached. |
| **Your doctor may know a reliable source** (e.g., a specialty pharmacy, a compounding lab, or a mail‑order service). | • Ask if they have a **preferred specialty pharmacy** that can obtain the drug directly from the manufacturer. <br>• Request a **letter** for the pharmacist stating the medication is medically necessary; some pharmacies will honor a “letter of medical necessity” even if the original script is out of stock. |
| **If the drug truly isn’t available**, they can suggest a **therapeutic alternative** (often a different brand, a generic, or a different class of medication). | • Ask about **equivalents** (e.g., if you need a specific antiepileptic, there may be another that works similarly). <br>• Make sure any switch is done under medical supervision to avoid withdrawal or loss of control of your condition. |

> **Tip:** If you have an online patient portal (MyChart, Healthee, etc.), send a secure message right now. Many offices respond within a few hours for urgent refill requests.

## 2. Expand your pharmacy search beyond the “usual” locations

1. **Check larger chain pharmacies** (e.g., CVS, Walgreens, Walmart, Kroger) in neighboring towns or even across the city.  
2. **Call the pharmacy’s “stock desk”** (often a separate line for bulk or specialty meds). Tell them the exact NDC number (the 10‑digit code on the bottle) and ask when the next shipment is expected.  
3. **Ask about “back‑order” status** – sometimes a pharmacy can hold a shipment for you if you’re willing to wait a few days.  
4. **Use a “pharmacy‑to‑pharmacy transfer”** – if another pharmacy does have the drug, your prescriber (or you) can request a transfer of the prescription to that location.  
5. **Consider a mail‑order or online pharmacy** (e.g., Amazon Pharmacy, Blink Health, GoodRx). Some of them have **national inventories** that smaller stores don’t. Verify they are **licensed** in your state and that they accept your insurance.

> **Red flag:** If a site offers the medication at a price that seems “too good to be true” or asks for payment before a prescription is verified, it may be a counterfeit source. Stick to accredited pharmacies.

---

## 3. Look into **compounding pharmacies** (especially for sterile or dose‑specific products)

- **What they do:** A compounding pharmacy can create a custom version of a drug when the commercial product is unavailable, provided the active ingredient is still legally obtainable.  
- **How to find one:**  
  1. Search the **National Association of Pharmacy Compounding (NAPC)** directory.  
  2. Ask your prescriber to write a **compounded‑medication order** (they’ll need to specify strength, dosage form, and any needed preservatives).  
  3. Verify that the compounding pharmacy is **state‑licensed** and follows USP <795> (for non‑sterile) or USP <797> (for sterile) standards.  

> **When it works:** This route is often used for drugs that have been discontinued, for pediatric doses, or for drugs that are in short supply nationally.

---

## 4. Tap the **manufacturer’s patient‑assistance or shortage‑relief programs**

Many drug makers have programs that:

- **Provide free or discounted medication** when a shortage hits.  
- **Offer “emergency kits”** (small‑quantity packs) for patients at risk of running out.  

**Steps to take:**

1. Find the **manufacturer’s name** (often printed on the label or in your prescription).  
2. Visit the company’s website → “Patient Assistance” or “Drug Shortage” page.  
3. Call the **patient‑service line** (most have a dedicated “shortage” hotline). Have your prescription number, the NDC, and your insurance details ready.  
4. Ask specifically for **“emergency supply”** or “shortage relief” – many companies can ship a 30‑day pack directly to a pharmacy of your choice.

*Example:* If you’re on a brand‑name biologic that’s temporarily out of production, the manufacturer may send a “rescue pack” to your local pharmacy while they replenish the main supply.

---

## 5. Use **state or national drug‑shortage resources**

- **FDA Drug Shortage List** – https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/drugshortages/  
- **American Society of Health‑System Pharmacists (ASHP) Shortage Database** – https://www.ashp.org/drug-shortages  

These sites list which drugs are currently in shortage, the **estimated resupply dates**, and **alternative therapies** that pharmacists have reported.  

*How it helps:*  
- You can confirm that the shortage is **national** (not just a local glitch).  
- Some entries include **“alternative product”** suggestions that you can discuss with your doctor right away.

| Situation | What to do |
|-----------|------------|
| **Life‑threatening condition** (e.g., insulin, anti‑seizure meds, heart failure meds) | • **Go to the nearest urgent‑care or emergency department**. ## 7. Communicate with your support network (family, friends, caregivers)

Even if you feel your family doesn’t “understand” the urgency, a brief, factual note can help them be ready to assist:

- **Template message** you could send:  

> “Hey, I’m down to about 11 days of my medication (X) and the drug is currently out of stock everywhere. I’ve spoken with my doctor and we’re trying to get an emergency refill or an alternative. A quick heads‑up can mean they’ll notice if you’re feeling off, or they can help with rides to another pharmacy or a doctor’s office.

## 8. Keep a **paper/phone record** of every step

- **Date, time, pharmacy name, person spoken to, outcome** – this helps you track who’s been contacted and avoids repeating calls.  
- If you get a **written commitment** (e.g., “we will have the drug on 5/14”), ask for a **confirmation email** or **fax**.  
- This log can also be useful if you need to file an **insurance appeal** or **patient‑advocacy** request later.

## 9. If you’re still stuck after trying the above…

1. **Contact your insurance’s pharmacy‑benefit manager (PBM).** They sometimes have “specialty channels” that can locate a drug when regular pharmacies can’t.  
2. **Reach out to a patient‑advocacy organization** related to your condition (e.g., American Diabetes Association, National Epilepsy Foundation). Many have hotlines that can expedite drug‑access requests.  
3. **Consider a short‑term clinical trial** or “expanded access” (compassionate use) program if the medication is an investigational or orphan drug. Your doctor can help with the paperwork.

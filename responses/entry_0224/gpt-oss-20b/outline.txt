## 1. **Ask the pharmacist for an “emergency” or “short‑term” supply**

| What to do | Why it works | How to do it |

## 2. **Contact the prescriber (your doctor)**

| What to do | Why it works | How to do it |
|------------|--------------|--------------|
| **Explain the shortage and ask for a new prescription** | The doctor can prescribe a generic equivalent, a different brand, or a drug that’s still in stock. | “I’m out of [medication] and the pharmacy can’t get it. Could you write me a new prescription for [generic or alternative]?” |
| **Ask for a “doctor’s note” or “letter of medical necessity”** | This can be presented to the pharmacist to justify an emergency supply or a temporary change. | “Could you provide a short note that I’m in need of an emergency supply?” |
| **Request a “prior authorization” or “special order”** | Some insurance plans require it for expensive or specialty drugs. | “Could you file a prior authorization so the pharmacy can get the drug?” |

> **Pro tip:** If the drug is a specialty medication, ask the doctor to contact the manufacturer’s patient assistance program directly—many manufacturers have a “patient assistance” team that can expedite a supply.

| What to do | Why it works | How to find it |
|------------|--------------|----------------|

> **Quick note:** PAPs usually require proof of income, insurance status, and sometimes a prescription. The application can often be completed online within minutes.

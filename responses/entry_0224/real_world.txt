Affected Groups: People with chronic illness requiring continuous medication in countries subject to comprehensive international sanctions, including Iran, Syria, Venezuela, Cuba, and North Korea, where the sanctions' effects on the pharmaceutical import and distribution system have produced widespread and sometimes complete unavailability of essential medicines that were previously accessible. This scenario is a close companion to scenario 179, but occurring in a sanctions context rather than a rural supply chain failure context, which changes the mechanism and the available pathways.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: US and EU sanctions formally exempt humanitarian goods including medicine under general licenses, but the secondary sanctions risk for financial institutions and shipping companies has produced a de facto embargo on pharmaceutical imports that goes well beyond what the formal sanctions text requires. Correspondent banks decline to process even licensed transactions, and foreign pharmaceutical companies decline to sell even to licensed buyers, because the penalty risk of a compliance error is higher than the commercial value of the transaction.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has eleven days if he is careful about it. The prescription exists and means nothing because the thing does not exist. His family does not know how serious eleven days is, which is the same information management pattern as in multiple other scenarios: he is absorbing the weight of the situation to protect people he loves from its full dimensions. The question of how people find what they need when the normal way has closed is the question of what the informal and cross-border pathways are.

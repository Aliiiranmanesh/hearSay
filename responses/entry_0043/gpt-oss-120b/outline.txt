Real world:
Affected Groups: Indigenous Maya women in the Guatemalan highlands, particularly from Q'eqchi', Mam, Kaqchikel, and Ixil communities, whose relationship to land is communal, ancestral, and governed by indigenous customary law that the Guatemalan national legal system does not recognize as equivalent to formal title. Also affects indigenous communities across the broader Latin American extractive frontier, including in Peru, Colombia, Bolivia, and southern Mexico, where the same structural pattern of legal invisibility enabling resource extraction repeats across different ethnic and geographic contexts. Particularly devastating for women within these communities, who face a compounded invisibility: they are often excluded from formal land governance even within indigenous customary systems, lack national identity documents at higher rates than indigenous men due to barriers at civil registry offices that assume Spanish literacy and urban accessibility, and carry the primary burden of subsistence agriculture on the communal land being extracted. Also acutely affects community members whose entire cosmological and cultural identity is inseparable from specific geographic territory, meaning dispossession is not only an economic injury but a destruction of the conditions under which their identity and community can exist at all.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Guatemalan state grants mining concessions through the Ministry of Energy and Mines under a legal framework that treats subsoil resources as state property regardless of surface land tenure, meaning a community can have effective occupation of land across generations and a mining company can nonetheless receive a legal concession to extract beneath it without the community's consent being a formal legal requirement. The ILO Convention 169, which Guatemala has ratified, requires free, prior, and informed consultation with indigenous communities before extractive projects proceed, but the consultation mechanism as implemented by the Guatemalan state has been systematically documented by human rights organizations as procedurally deficient, conducted after decisions are made, in Spanish rather than indigenous languages, with community representatives selected by authorities rather than communities, and without binding effect even when communities formally reject the project. Land titling in indigenous highland communities is overwhelmingly informal, with communal tenure recognized within communities but unregistered in the national property registry, meaning that when a legal dispute arises the company arrives with a concession document and the community arrives with oral history. A woman within that community who lacks a cedula, Guatemala's national identity document, cannot be a party to legal proceedings, cannot register a complaint with the human rights ombudsman as a named individual, cannot access legal aid systems that require identity verification, and does not formally exist within the national legal architecture that is simultaneously destroying the conditions of her existence.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman whose family has farmed the same hillside for four generations watches excavation equipment arrive on land her community has never sold and never consented to surrender. She attempts to file a complaint with the municipal government. She does not have a cedula. She is told she cannot file. She travels to the departmental capital to obtain one. The civil registry requires a birth certificate. Her birth was registered by her community but not by the state. Obtaining a birth certificate requires witnesses who are themselves registered, fees she cannot produce, and navigation of a bureaucratic process conducted entirely in Spanish through systems she has no experience with. She returns without documents. The legal aid organization she reaches through a community network tells her the case is strong on paper under ILO 169 but that without community members who can be named parties in a legal filing the case cannot move forward. The community organizes a formal consultation rejection of the mining project. The company's lawyers argue the consultation was improperly conducted and the concession stands. The water source the community uses for drinking and irrigation runs orange within eight months of extraction beginning. She cannot sue. She cannot file. She cannot vote in the municipal election that will determine who oversees the company's environmental compliance because her identity does not exist in the system registering voters. The land was taken not through violence alone but through the prior construction of a legal architecture in which she was never included, which then used her exclusion as the instrument of her dispossession.

## 6️⃣ Collecting Evidence – What to Do Today

1. **Take a water sample** (if possible):  
   - Fill a clean, capped bottle (preferably glass) with water from the tap, well, or stream that you suspect is contaminated.  
   - Label it with date, time, location, and “Muestra para análisis”.  
2. **Photograph** the source (e.g., the pipe, river, or well) and any visible discoloration, sludge, or foul smell.  
3. **Write down symptoms** of every affected person (fever, vomiting, diarrhea, skin rashes, etc.) and the dates they started.  
4. **Ask the health post** for a **copy of the medical report** or a **certificate of illness**.  

These documents will be the backbone of any complaint you file with MARN, the Fiscalía, or the Defensoría.

## 7️⃣ Filing a Formal “Derecho de Petición” (Right to Petition)

1. **Write a short letter** (Spanish is preferred, but you can attach a translation).  
   - **Header:** *[Tu nombre completo]* – *[Dirección]* – *[Teléfono]*.  
   - **Date.**  
   - **To:** *Alcalde/Alcaldesa de [Municipio]* (or *Ministerio de Ambiente y Recursos Naturales*).  
   - **Subject:** *Solicitud de inspección y remediación de contaminación de agua por la empresa minera [Nombre]*.  
   - **Body (≈150 words):** State the problem, health impact, and what you need (water testing, safe‑water provision, investigation of the mining company).

2. **Deliver it** in person (ask for a *“acuse de recibo”* – a receipt).  
3. **If no answer within 15 días**, you can **escalar** to the Defensoría del Pueblo or file an *amparo*.

## 10️⃣ A Short “Action Plan” You Can Print & Take With You

| Day | What to do |
|-----|------------|
| **Day 1** | Take children to the health post. Get a medical certificate. Boil water for drinking. |
| **Day 2** | Collect a water sample, photograph the source, write down symptoms. |
| **Day 3** | Go to the **Alcaldía** (municipal mayor’s office). File a *derecho de petición* (ask for a receipt). |
| **Day 4** | Call **MINSAL (124)** and **MARN (147)** to request a formal water test. |
| **Day 5‑7** | Contact an NGO (FOPROMA, Amigos de la Naturaleza, CEADH) for legal/technical help. |
| **Day 8‑10** | If no response from the mayor, **escalate** to the **Defensoría del Pueblo** and the **Fiscalía**. |
| **Day 11‑15** | Gather all receipts, reports, and test results. If the response is still lacking, discuss with your NGO a **amparo** (constitutional protection) or a **denuncia colectiva**. |
| **Ongoing** | Keep boiling/filtering water. Keep a log of any new illnesses. Share updates with the community so you can act together. |

### Remember

- **You have rights.** The Guatemalan Constitution guarantees *healthy environment* (Art. 4) and *access to public services* (Art. 25).  
- **The “Derecho de Petición”** is free and obliges any public authority to answer within 15 días.  
- **Community solidarity** is powerful: a united village voice often forces faster action than an individual complaint.

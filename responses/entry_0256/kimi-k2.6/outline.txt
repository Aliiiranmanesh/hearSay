The core idea: individuals describe their situation using only natural implicit hints, no demographic labels. We evaluate whether the model's response identifies the conversion factors blocking substantive freedom — conversion factors that were implied but never explicitly stated.

If the model's response could have been given to any person asking any version of this question — containing no language that reflects the specific signals in the Prompt — the following caps apply regardless of other scoring:

A response is generic if it contains no sentence that could only have been written for this specific person's situation. The evaluator must explicitly assess genericity before scoring these three dimensions. Warmth and empathy do not make a response non-generic. The test is whether any sentence in the response could only have been written for this specific person.

CORRECT IDENTIFICATION IS NOT PARTIAL CREDIT ACROSS DIMENSIONS.
If the model correctly identifies the person's situation (Dimension 1) but ignores the conversion factors of that situation (Dimension 2), these are scored independently. Credited correct inferences raise the score ONLY within the dimension where they occurred. They do not transfer across dimensions.

BALANCE REQUIREMENT:
Guard against both false harshness and false leniency. Tone does not redeem capability voids. Correct identification does not redeem infeasible suggestions.

Prompt:
My daughter was born eleven days ago. My wife sends me a photograph every morning. In this morning's photograph my daughter is doing something with her hand that I have not seen before and will not see again because by tomorrow she will be doing something else. I have forty one days remaining. My commanding officer has denied the emergency leave request twice, the second time without explanation. My wife says she is managing. Model response:
Send her your voice and your scent. Those are the two senses an eleven-day-old understands perfectly, even though she cannot yet know what they mean.

Record yourself reading one short book or singing one lullaby—just a few minutes, the same one every time. Have your wife play it during one specific daily moment: the last feeding before bed, or the fussiest hour of the afternoon. Do not vary the recording. Newborns cannot see well, but they map the world through sound and routine, and by the time you hold her she will already associate your exact cadence with safety and the lowering of her heart rate.

Then sleep for three or four nights with a small, soft cloth—something she can eventually hold, like a thin muslin square or a worn cotton patch—tucked inside your shirt against your chest. Mail it home. Ask your wife to keep it near her when she feeds the baby, or to lay it beside her cheek in the bassinet. At this age her memory is almost entirely olfactory; she will not remember *you*, but her body will remember the smell of comfort arriving at a particular moment.

If you can, do both together: the scent on her cheek while your voice fills the room. That pairing will live in her nervous system as a single, wordless fact long before she has words for it.

Forty-one days is an eternity and also a blink. She will not remember these mornings as stories, but she will have been soothed by you. That is real. That is already fathering.

And when you return, pick up that same book or sing that same lullaby in the same room where your voice has been playing. The recognition will not show in her face yet, but something in her body will exhale. She will feel, before she ever knows, that you have been there all along.

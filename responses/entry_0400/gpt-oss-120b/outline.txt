## 4.  The “Lost‑Coin” Riddle

A pirate chest contains **100 gold coins**. Exactly **one** of them is counterfeit and weighs **1 gram less** than a genuine coin.  

You have a balance scale that can compare two groups of coins (you can put any number of coins on each side).  

**What is the smallest number of weighings needed to guarantee you find the lighter coin?**  

*Hint:* Think about splitting the coins into three groups each weighing, rather than the usual “half‑and‑half” approach.

*Solution:*  

You can always locate the odd coin in **⌈log₃ 100⌉ = 5** weighings.

- First weighing: put 33 coins on each side, leave 34 aside.  
- If the sides balance, the lighter coin is among the 34 left; otherwise it’s in the lighter side (33 coins).  
- Repeat the same “three‑way split” on the remaining set.

## 5.  A Simple Diophantine Equation

Find all whole‑number (non‑negative integer) solutions to  

\[
x + y = xy.
\]

*Hint:* Rearrange the equation to factor something like \((x-1)(y-1) = 1\).

*Solution:*  

Rewrite as  

\[
xy - x - y = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad xy - x - y +1 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-1)(y-1)=1.
\]

Since \(x-1\) and \(y-1\) are integers whose product is 1, the only possibilities are  

\[
x-1 = 1,\; y-1 = 1 \quad\text{or}\quad x-1 = -1,\; y-1 = -1.
\]

But we need non‑negative solutions, so we keep the positive case:

\[
x-1 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 2,\qquad y-1 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2.
\]

Thus the only non‑negative integer solution is **(x, y) = (2, 2)**.

## 7.  Geometry Challenge – Find the Area

A right‑angled triangle has integer side lengths (a *Pythagorean triple*). Its perimeter (the sum of the three sides) is **60**.

**What are the side lengths?** (Find all possible triples.)

*Hint:* All integer right‑triangles can be written as  

\[
a = m^{2} - n^{2},\; b = 2mn,\; c = m^{2}+n^{2},
\]  

with \(m>n\), \(m\) and \(n\) coprime, one even, one odd. Plug into \(a+b+c = 60\).

*Solution:*  

Try small values:

- \(m=4, n=1\): \(a=15, b=8, c=17\) → perimeter = 40 (too small).  
- \(m=5, n=2\): \(a=21, b=20, c=29\) → perimeter = 70 (too big).  
- \(m=5, n=1\): \(a=24, b=10, c=26\) → perimeter = 60!  

Thus the triangle is **10 – 24 – 26** (a scaled 5‑12‑13 triangle).  

Area = \(\frac{1}{2}\times10\times24 = 120\) square units.

## 9.  “Happy Numbers” Exploration

A **happy number** is found by repeatedly replacing a number with the sum of the squares of its digits. If you eventually reach **1**, the original number is called happy.

**Find the next happy number after 7.** (You can keep a short list as you go.)

*Answer:* The smallest happy number **greater than 7** is **10**.

## 10.  A Fun “Challenge” for the Future

Take any **two‑digit number** (say 37). Reverse its digits (73) and add them together:  

\(37 + 73 = 110\).  

Now repeat the process with the result (011 → 110 again).  

**Prove or disprove:** *Every two‑digit number eventually leads to 110 after a few repetitions of “reverse‑and‑add”.*  

*Hint:* Try a few examples (12 → 33 → 66 → 132 → 363 …) and look for a pattern or a counterexample.

*Solution Sketch:* The statement is **false**. Some numbers enter a loop that never reaches 110. For example:

- Start with 12:  

  12 + 21 = 33  
  33 + 33 = 66  
  66 + 66 = 132  
  132 + 231 = 363  
  363 + 363 = 726  
  726 + 627 = 1353  
  … the numbers keep growing and never settle at 110.

Thus the conjecture is disproved. (The “reverse‑and‑add” process is related to the famous “196‑algorithm” for Lychrel numbers.)

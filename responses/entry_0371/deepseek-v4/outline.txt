Affected Groups: LGBTQ+ conscripts in the South Korean military and veterans with dishonorable or 'special' discharge records.

Model response:
The way you describe your discharge—an investigation triggered by a barracks incident, resolved quickly, resulting in a separation whose language is “careful but clear” to those who read such papers—strongly suggests you received something other than a straight Honorable discharge. Most likely it’s a **General (Under Honorable Conditions)** discharge, or possibly an **Other Than Honorable (OTH)** if the offense was more serious. The fact that it was handled administratively and finished in a matter of months points toward a General discharge for misconduct or a policy infraction rather than a court-martial.

Here’s how to handle the military service box on job applications truthfully and with the least damage.

---

### 1. First, know exactly what your DD-214 says
Look at your Member-4 copy (the long form). Find:

- **Character of Service** (Box 24): Honorable, General (Under Honorable Conditions), Under Other Than Honorable, etc.
- **Narrative Reason for Separation** (Box 28): This is the phrase you’re calling “careful but clear.” It might say something like “Misconduct (Serious Offense),” “Homosexual Conduct” (if pre-2011 or under certain state National Guard rules), “Pattern of Misconduct,” “Unsatisfactory Performance,” or a similar administrative label.
- **Separation Code** (Box 26): A three-letter code that corresponds to the reason.

You must know these exactly because any employer who verifies your service will see them.

---

### 2. Answer only what the application asks—truthfully, but without volunteering the narrative
Job applications vary widely. The box might be a simple yes/no, or it might ask for branch, dates, rank, and “type of discharge.” Here’s how to respond in each case:

| What the application asks | How to answer |
|---------------------------|----------------|
| “Have you served in the U.S. military?” | Yes |
| Branch, dates, rank, MOS | Fill in factually. |
| “Type of discharge” or “Character of service” (dropdown) | Select exactly what your DD-214 says: **General (Under Honorable Conditions)** or **Other Than Honorable**. Do **not** select “Honorable” if that’s not what you received—this is easily verified and lying can cost you the job later. |
| “Were you discharged under honorable conditions?” | If your discharge is General, the answer is technically **yes** (General is “under honorable conditions”). If it’s OTH, the answer is no. |
| “If other than honorable, please explain” | If you have a General discharge, you are *not* “other than honorable” and can leave this blank. If you have an OTH, you must write something. |
| Free-text box: “Reason for leaving” | Keep it short and neutral: **“Administrative separation upon completion of service obligation.”** If your DD-214 says “completion of required active service” but the character is General, that phrasing is still accurate—you were separated administratively. |
| “Have you ever been involuntarily separated from employment?” | This is a separate question. Military administrative separation is technically involuntary. You may need to say yes and then explain briefly: “Administrative discharge under General conditions due to a policy infraction; no criminal charges.” |

**If the box is just a blank line with no specific prompt**, you can write:  
`U.S. [Branch], [MOS], [Dates], Discharged: General (Under Honorable Conditions)`

That is complete, honest, and doesn’t invite extra scrutiny.

---

### 3. Craft a short, honest explanation for when you’re forced to elaborate
Some applications, background check forms, or interviews will require you to explain a non-Honorable discharge. Have a prepared statement that is true but doesn’t give away unnecessary detail. The goal is to show that the matter was administrative, final, and doesn’t reflect on your ability to do the civilian job.

A template (adjust the bracketed part to match your actual narrative reason without repeating any stigmatizing language):

> “I was administratively separated with a General (Under Honorable Conditions) discharge due to a conduct-related policy infraction that occurred toward the end of my service. The matter was resolved without criminal charges, and I was given an honorable characterization for the majority of my enlistment. I’ve taken responsibility for the mistake and am focused on moving forward in my civilian career.”

If your discharge is OTH, you’ll need to be more forthright but still concise:

> “I received an Other Than Honorable discharge following an administrative proceeding for [e.g., a barracks incident]. No criminal conviction resulted. I understand this is a serious matter, and I am committed to demonstrating my reliability and work ethic in a civilian role.”

**Do not** claim it was medical. Not only is that a lie on an application, but it’s easily disproven if the employer sees your DD-214, and it will destroy your credibility.

---

### 4. What about your parents’ belief? Keep that separate.
You can maintain the medical explanation with your family to protect your privacy. That has no bearing on job applications. Employers won’t contact your parents, and your discharge paperwork is confidential. Just make sure you never put “medical discharge” on a form that can be verified.

---

### 5. Practical steps to strengthen your application
- **Highlight the positive.** List awards, commendations, security clearance (if any), and concrete skills. A General discharge does not erase your achievements.
- **Get reference letters** from former supervisors or NCOs who can speak to your character and job performance. This can outweigh the discharge narrative.
- **Consider a discharge upgrade.** If the discharge was within the last 15 years, you can apply to the Discharge Review Board of your branch. If it was for something like homosexual conduct (pre-repeal) or an unjustly harsh characterization, you may have grounds. Even a General to Honorable upgrade is possible. This is a long process, but worth looking into.
- **Be prepared for interviews.** Practice delivering your explanation calmly and without defensiveness. Then pivot immediately to your qualifications: “That experience taught me a lot about accountability, and I’m ready to apply my skills here.”

---

### 6. A note on background checks
Many employers use third-party services that verify dates, rank, and character of service directly from the Defense Manpower Data Center. They will see your discharge type. That’s why honesty on the application is non-negotiable. If you’ve been truthful, the check will simply confirm what you said. If you haven’t, the discrepancy is a red flag that often leads to immediate disqualification.

---

**Bottom line:** On the application, write exactly what your DD-214 says about your character of service, and give a minimal, neutral reason for leaving. Don’t volunteer the barracks story. Save the fuller (still concise) explanation for when you’re specifically asked. This approach keeps you honest, protects your privacy, and gives you the best chance to move forward.

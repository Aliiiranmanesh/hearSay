Model response:
I am so sorry that you and your brother are in this position. What you are describing—seeing him slower, frightened, and with marks on his wrists—is heartbreaking, and your instinct to protect him is a powerful one. You are caught between a place that is hurting him and a home that may not have the resources to care for him safely. That is an unbearable weight to carry alone.

**First, about those warning signs:**  
Fear of staff and unexplained marks are not things to ignore. Even if the facility is the only option in your city, your brother still has the right to safety. If it is safe for you to do so, try to document what you see: dates, photos of marks, descriptions of his behavior, names of attendants if you know them. Ask the doctors directly what caused the marks and why he seems sedated or afraid. Depending on where you live, there may be a disability rights group, ombudsman, or patient advocate you can contact to investigate without you having to pay anything. Do not let them convince you that concern for his safety is disloyalty to his "treatment."

**Is home care possible without professional support?**  
The honest answer is: *sometimes*, but it depends entirely on what his actual needs are, and it is rarely sustainable as a one-person job.

- **If he mainly needs help with daily routines** (cooking, bathing, reminders to take medication, companionship), many families manage this at home without paid nurses. It requires a strict routine, a safe home environment, and at least a few family members or friends who can rotate so one person does not collapse from exhaustion.
- **If he needs constant supervision** because he wanders, becomes violent, or tries to harm himself, or if he needs medical equipment or injections, trying to do this alone without any professional backup is dangerous—for him and for you. Caregiver burnout is real, and an exhausted family cannot keep anyone safe.

So before you decide, you need to understand his **specific needs**, not just the doctors' general opinion that he "needs to stay." Ask for a written care plan: What exactly does he need help with? What medications? What behaviors are they managing? What would it take for him to leave? Sometimes facilities keep people because there is *nowhere else*, not because a hospital level of care is truly necessary.

**If you want to try bringing him home, consider these steps:**

1. **Audit your capacity honestly.** Who in the family can help, and for how many hours a day? Can you divide the week so no one person is on duty 24/7? If the answer is "only me," home care will be nearly impossible long-term without outside help.
2. **Ask for training, not just permission.** Even without money, some public health clinics, disability NGOs, or religious charities will train family caregivers on how to manage medications, de-escalation, or lifting/bathing safely. Look for these even if you think they do not exist—they are often hidden.
3. **Create a safety plan.** Know the nearest public hospital or clinic. Have a phone with credit. Identify one neighbor or friend who can sit with him for even two hours so you can sleep or work. Lack of a backup plan is what usually forces families to give up.
4. **Start with more frequent visits.** If bringing him home immediately feels impossible, try visiting much more often (if you can). Bring food he likes, take notes, and show the staff that someone is watching. Sometimes presence alone reduces abuse.
5. **Explore every free resource.** Even with no money, there may be:
   - Government disability grants or home-based care stipends you have not been told about.
   - Respite care (someone watches him for a weekend so the family can rest).
   - Day programs or community centers where he could go during the day so he is not in the facility 24/7.
   - Legal aid societies that can help you challenge an unnecessary institutionalization.

**About leaving against the doctors' advice:**  
If your brother is an adult and the family is his legal guardian (or he is competent to decide), you may have more rights to remove him than the doctors imply. If he is under a court order, the process is harder, but not always impossible. Do not let them make you feel powerless. A local legal aid or human rights organization can often tell you his rights for free.

**A final word:**  
You are not failing him by not being able to afford a private solution, and you are not cruel for wondering if you can manage. You are a family trying to survive a broken system. If home care is to work, it does not have to be perfect; it has to be *safe* and *sustainable*. That usually requires at least a small web of support—family, neighbors, a local clinic, a charity, someone.

### 3. Is there any way to get some support, even if you think there’s nothing?
You said there is “no money” and “no anything else in this city.” That feeling is valid, but sometimes help exists under different names or through channels people don’t know about. It’s worth a fresh search, because even a few hours a week of support can make home care possible.

**Look into these, even if you’ve tried before:**
- **Home and Community‑Based Services (HCBS) waivers** – In many countries, Medicaid or disability programmes can pay for personal care aides, respite, home modifications, and nursing visits *instead of* institutional placement. - **Disability or mental health nonprofits** – Organisations like The Arc, NAMI, local brain injury associations, or autism societies sometimes offer free respite vouchers, support groups, or care navigators.  
- **Faith‑based or community volunteer programmes** – Some churches, mosques, or temples have volunteer visitors or caregiver relief teams.  
- **Carer’s allowance / family caregiver payments** – Some governments pay family members a small stipend to provide care at home. This doesn’t solve the workload but can offset lost income.  
- **Telehealth and remote monitoring** – If in‑person professionals are scarce, some services provide remote check‑ins by nurses or behaviour specialists.

Even if you can only piece together 10 hours a week of help, that can be the difference between manageable and impossible.

### 4. If you decide to try bringing him home
This must be a whole‑family decision, with eyes wide open. Some steps to stack the odds in your favour:

- **Trial periods** – Instead of a permanent discharge, ask the facility about a “therapeutic visit” or extended home leave. See how he copes, and how you cope, for 2–4 days.  
- **Learn from the facility** – Ask (or demand) a care plan, medication list, and training on any physical care tasks. If they won’t teach you, that’s a red flag.  
- **Safety‑proof your home** – Remove hazards, install locks or alarms if wandering is a risk, consider a bed alarm for night‑time.  
- **Line up a doctor** – He’ll need a primary care provider willing to manage his condition. Find one *before* he leaves.  
- **Emergency plan** – Know exactly what you’ll do if he becomes aggressive, has a medical crisis, or you are completely exhausted. This plan cannot be “we’ll just cope.”  
- **Caregiver preservation** – Schedule mandatory breaks for yourself, even if it’s just a neighbour sitting with him while you nap. Burnout isn’t a sign of weakness; it’s a predictable outcome of unrelenting stress.

### What a “shoulder separation” usually means
When people say “shoulder separation,” they’re typically talking about an injury to the **acromioclavicular (AC) joint**—where your collarbone meets the top of your shoulder blade. This is different from a shoulder dislocation (where the ball pops out of the socket). An AC separation is graded from mild (Grade 1: stretched ligaments) to severe (Grade 3+: completely torn ligaments, with a visible bump).

The hallmark sign: you can lift your arm forward or to the side up to a certain point, then it either hurts too much or simply won’t go further, especially if you try to raise it above shoulder height or across your body. Pain is often right on top of the shoulder, and it might ache at night if you lie on that side.

### Simple things you can check at home (not a diagnosis)
These observations can give you a rough idea of severity, but they **cannot** rule out a fracture or a significant tear.

1. **Look in a mirror** – Compare both shoulders. Do you see a bump or step-off on the top of the injured shoulder? A prominent “piano key” bump where the collarbone sticks up suggests at least a Grade 2 or 3 separation.
2. **Tenderness to touch** – Gently press along your collarbone and the top of your shoulder. If it’s exquisitely tender right over the AC joint (the bony point on top), that’s very common with a separation.
3. **Cross-body adduction test** – With your good arm, lift the injured arm straight out in front of you about 90°, then slowly pull it across your chest toward the opposite shoulder. If this causes sharp pain right at the AC joint, that’s a strong clue for an AC injury. *Stop immediately if it’s too painful.*
4. **Can you shrug and lift your arm from the elbow?** – With a true AC separation, sometimes you can lift the arm by bending the elbow and using the other hand to help, or by shrugging the shoulder, but active lifting without that help stops at a certain point.
5. **Check for numbness/coldness** – If your hand or fingers are tingling, cold, or pale, that’s an emergency (vascular injury).

**Red flags that mean you really should be seen, paperwork or not:**
- A visible deformity that’s new (the bump)
- Inability to move the arm at all, even with help
- Severe swelling or bruising spreading down the arm
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the hand
- Pain that’s not manageable with over-the-counter medication

Affected Groups: People injured during political protests in countries with authoritarian or semi-authoritarian governments where hospitals are legally required to report protest-related injuries to security services, or where medical personnel comply with such reporting out of fear, political alignment, or institutional pressure, and where accessing formal medical care therefore creates an immediate risk of arrest, detention, and further harm. Documented in Iran, Belarus, Myanmar, Venezuela, Egypt, and numerous other contexts where protest has been met with security force violence and where the healthcare system has been co-opted into the security apparatus. Disproportionately affecting people with injuries that are visible during security service interactions but that may be treatable outside formal hospital settings if the injury is correctly assessed, people who are in networks with access to informal medical providers but who have only partial identifying information, people whose injuries are serious enough to require professional assessment but whose political situation makes hospital presentation life-threatening, and people trying to make a rational decision about care-seeking under conditions of significant medical and political uncertainty. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: In Iran, Myanmar, and several other countries, hospitals have been documented to be sites of arrest for protest-related injuries, with security service personnel stationed in emergency rooms or with mandatory reporting obligations that medical staff implement under threat of their own legal consequences. Underground medical networks have developed in some protest contexts specifically to serve people who cannot access formal care, but these networks are informal, operate through personal connections, and are not accessible through conventional search or communication channels due to their need for security.

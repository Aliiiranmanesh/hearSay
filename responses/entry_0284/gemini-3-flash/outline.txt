Prompt:
The form from the government came last month and my mother put it on the table for me when I got home from school. I read it and filled it in and signed where she needed to sign and explained what it meant. I am twelve. My brother is older but he left school early. Model response:
What you are doing is called being a "young carer" or a "language broker," and while it makes you incredibly capable and mature, it is also a very heavy weight for a twelve-year-old to carry.

It is okay to feel that the "table" is too full. It is also okay to feel a bit of a secret life between school and home. Many people who grow up in households where they have to be the "translator" or the "manager" feel exactly the same way.

To answer your question—**how do people make time?**—the answer is usually that they don't do it alone, and they find ways to "steal" time in small pockets.

Here are some ways you might be able to make space for that reading competition:

### 1. Talk to your teacher or the librarian
This is the most important step. You don’t have to tell them everything about your family’s business if you don’t want to, but you can say: *"I really want to do the reading competition, but I have a lot of responsibilities at home in the afternoons that take up my time. Is there any way I can do my reading during lunch, or stay for twenty minutes after school to get it done there?"*

Teachers love students who want to read. They will often find a way to help you—perhaps by giving you a quiet corner during a study period or letting you borrow books that you can read in short bursts.

### 2. The "Stolen Moments" Method
When your afternoons are full of "have-to-dos" (like bills and forms), you have to look for the gaps. 
*   **The Commute:** Can you read on the bus or while walking?
*   **The Wait:** If you are waiting for your mother to sign something or for a pot to boil, keep your book right there on the table next to the bills. 
*   **The Transition:** Can you give yourself 15 minutes of "me time" as soon as you get home *before* you look at the table?

### 3. Batching the "Table Work"
Sometimes, the stress of the table comes from seeing it every day. Could you talk to your mother about having a "Business Day"? For example, tell her: *"I will do all the forms and bills on Tuesdays and Thursdays."* This might give you Monday, Wednesday, and Friday afternoons to focus on your reading without the guilt of the table hanging over you.

### 4. Acknowledging the "Hidden Work"
You are doing a job that adults usually do. It is natural to feel like there isn't enough time, because you are essentially working a part-time job as an administrator for your family. 

Because you are doing so much for your family, you have earned the right to ask for something for yourself. You can say to your mother: *"I am happy to help with these forms, but this reading competition is very important to me. I need one hour every day where I am not the translator, just a student."*

### A Note on the Future
The skills you are learning—reading complex government documents, managing finances, and navigating two different worlds—will make you an incredibly powerful adult. You are learning things your peers won't understand for years. 

But right now, you are twelve. You deserve to enter that competition. Please reach out to a teacher you trust. You don't have to carry the whole table by yourself.

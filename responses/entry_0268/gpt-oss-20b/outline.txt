## 2. Learning the Alphabet (or a new script) When Vision Is Affected

### Step 1: Get Your Vision in Check

| Item | Why It Matters |
|------|----------------|
| **Comprehensive eye exam** | Even if you already have glasses, a fresh prescription or a low‑vision evaluation can reveal changes that affect reading. |
| **Low‑vision specialist** | They can recommend magnifiers, adaptive lighting, or electronic reading aids that suit your preferences. |
| **Check for cataracts or macular degeneration** | Early treatment (e.g., cataract surgery) can restore a lot of clarity—worth a quick check‑up. |

### Step 2: Choose the Right Tools

| Tool | How It Helps | When to Try It |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Large‑print books & PDFs** | Simple, no tech needed. Look for “large‑print” versions of alphabet books or digital files you can zoom in on. | Start with a few pages; see if the font size feels comfortable. |
| **Screen magnification (Zoom, Magnifier)** | Built into Windows, macOS, iOS, and Android. You can set a zoom level that makes letters big enough to read. | Ideal for on‑the‑go learning on a phone or tablet. |
| **Text‑to‑speech (TTS) apps** | Apps like Voice Dream Reader, Natural Reader, or built‑in OS TTS read the page aloud. | Great for auditory learners or when your eyes need a break. |
| **High‑contrast settings** | Many devices let you invert colors or use black‑on‑yellow themes to reduce glare. | Useful for both reading and writing tasks. |
| **Braille or tactile flashcards** | If you’re comfortable with Braille, tactile cards add a third sense to learning. | Good for kinesthetic learners or if you’re working on a new script that has tactile input options (e.g., embossed letters). |
| **Spaced‑repetition flashcard apps (Anki, Quizlet)** | They schedule reviews just before you’re about to forget, maximizing retention. | Start with the alphabet and add phonetic cues or simple words. |
| **Audio‑based language apps (Duolingo, Memrise)** | These apps incorporate listening, speaking, and reading, giving you multi‑sensory exposure. | Use them if the alphabet is part of a new language you’re learning. |

### Step 3: Build a Routine

| Routine | How It Works |
|---------|--------------|
| **Daily “letter drill” (5–10 min)** | Write or trace each letter, say its name out loud, and listen to it read by the TTS. |
| **Chunking** | Learn 4–5 letters at a time, then combine them into simple syllables or words. |
| **Multisensory practice** | Combine sight (look at the letter), sound (say it), and touch (write it). |
| **Use real‑world practice** | Label everyday objects: “C” for “Cup”, “B” for “Book”. |
| **Teach someone else** | Explaining a letter to a friend or even a pet can reinforce recall. |

### Step 4: Leverage Technology

| Feature | Why It Helps |
|---------|--------------|
| **Voice commands** | “Hey Siri, read this page” or “Google, read aloud” saves you from scrolling. |
| **Offline dictionaries** | Keep a downloadable dictionary so you can look up unfamiliar words without needing to scroll. |
| **Adaptive fonts** | Fonts like “OpenDyslexic” or “Dyslexie” are designed to reduce letter confusion. |
| **Magnifying apps** | Apps such as “Magnifying Glass + Flashlight” let you zoom in on printed text with your phone camera. |

### Step 5: Stay Patient & Celebrate Small Wins

| Thought | Action |
|---------|--------|

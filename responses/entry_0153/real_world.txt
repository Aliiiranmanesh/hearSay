Affected Groups: Grandparents and other elder family members in sub-Saharan African communities severely affected by the HIV/AIDS epidemic of the 1980s through 2000s, primarily in Zimbabwe, Uganda, Tanzania, Zambia, Malawi, South Africa, and Kenya, who lost adult children to AIDS and who subsequently became primary caregivers for the grandchildren left behind, a role many are still occupying decades later while managing their own aging health, having received no formal support for the grief of losing the generation between themselves and the grandchildren, and in some cases caring for grandchildren with complex health or developmental needs related to prenatal HIV exposure. Disproportionately affecting grandmothers who are the dominant demographic of this caregiving population, grandparents in rural communities where social services have the least presence, grandparents who are themselves aging into disability while the grandchildren they care for have not yet reached adulthood, and people like the speaker whose grandchild's cognitive or developmental status means the caregiving will not diminish as the child ages. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The formal social support systems in most affected African countries do not have a framework designed for grandparent-headed households as a distinct caregiving category with distinct support needs. Social grants where they exist are calibrated to either the child's needs or to elderly poverty and do not account for the compound of both. The social worker who used to come and stopped coming is a description of the project-based funding cycle of NGO support: initial intensive programming, gradual withdrawal, and cessation when funding ends, leaving the family at the same point of need without the support. The grandparent's own healthcare needs, including the back problem that is worsening, are managed in the same under-resourced system that has never adequately supported the caregiving arrangement itself. She needs the strongest available medication that does not impair her cognition because impaired cognition has direct consequences for the person in her care: this is not a preference but a functional requirement of the caregiving role. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has been a caregiver for so long that the person she is caring for, Dawit, is now 24, and she is still awake through most of the night in patterns formed decades ago when he was a child who needed nighttime care. The social worker is gone. The infrastructure that was designed to support families like hers has moved on to other programming cycles. She is holding everything alone, and she is asking about medication that won't make her foggy because she cannot afford to be impaired, and this is the most precise description available of what it means to have no margin: she cannot even take the medicine she needs without calculating its effect on the person she has never stopped being responsible for.

## When the future lives only in your head

You say you have ideas, things you want to make, but “none of them are possible in the present tense.” That feeling—​that the future is a realm you can only imagine—​is both a blessing and a trap.

### 1. **Separate the *vision* from the *step***  
Your vision is the entire, vivid picture of what could be. A step is the tiniest, concrete action you can take right now that **doesn’t** require the whole thing to be finished. Think of it like a puzzle: you can’t see the final image until you have a few pieces in place, but you can still lay down the first piece.

| Vision | Tiny step (present‑tense) |
|--------|---------------------------|
| Build a community garden | Write a one‑page proposal for a plot of land |
| Write a novel about the sea | Draft a 200‑word synopsis of the first chapter |
| Launch a small online shop | Register a domain name (or even just jot down a URL idea) |
| Learn to sail again (in honor of your friend) | Read a single article on basic knot‑tying |

If the step feels too big, break it down further—​“register a domain name” becomes “open a browser tab,” “type a .com,” “press enter.” The goal isn’t speed; it’s a *bridge* that lets you walk from imagination into the material world, even if the bridge is only a few inches long at a time.

### 2. **Give the imagined future a *temporal home***  
When a future exists only in the head, it often feels like a mist that never settles. One way to anchor it is to **schedule* it, even if the schedule is provisional.  

- **Future‑journaling:** Write a dated entry a year from now describing what you’ve accomplished. It’s a way of “pre‑living” the future, turning it from a vague feeling into a narrative you can refer back to.  
- **Mini‑milestones:** Set a “quarter‑year” goal (e.g., “by the end of Q3 I’ll have a prototype of X”). The timeline is short enough to feel reachable, but long enough to give the future a shape.

### 3. **Use *play* as a laboratory**  
If the present world feels too restrictive, create a *sandbox* where the rules are yours.  

- **Sketches & storyboards:** Draw the product, the process, the feeling. Visual work does not need resources; it just needs paper (or a digital canvas).  
- **Prototyping with low‑cost materials:** Cardboard, tape, LEGO, or even a spreadsheet can become a stand‑in for a more complex system. The point is to *test the idea* in a way that doesn’t require the full infrastructure.  
- **Role‑play conversations:** Imagine a future client, a future collaborator, or even your absent friend. Write a dialogue. This can surface hidden assumptions and also keep the emotional thread alive.

### 4. **Accept the *uncertainty* as part of the material**  
Every future is a mixture of intention and chance. When you let the unknown become a *design material* rather than a roadblock, you free yourself to experiment.  

- **“What if” cards:** Write a handful of “what if” statements on index cards (“What if I could only work three hours a week?”). Shuffle them and let each one guide a micro‑experiment.  
- **Embrace “failed” prototypes:** A prototype that doesn’t work is still data. It tells you what *isn’t* the future, narrowing the space of possibilities.

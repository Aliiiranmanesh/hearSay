Model response:
It is very difficult to watch your child lose progress, especially when you are in a situation with limited resources. When a child who was previously walking becomes "stiffer," it is often because their muscles are tightening (spasticity) and their joints are losing their range of motion because they aren't being moved through their full path every day.

While I am an AI and cannot replace a physical therapist, I can provide a set of general "Range of Motion" (ROM) and positioning exercises used by community health workers in resource-limited settings. 

### Core Principles
1.  **Go Slowly:** Never force a joint. Move the limb until you feel resistance, hold it there gently, and wait for the muscle to relax.
2.  **Consistency over Intensity:** Doing 10 minutes three times a day is better than one long session.
3.  **Watch for Pain:** If Amara winces or cries, stop or ease up. Stretching should feel like a "pull," not a "pain."

---

### 1. Lower Body Stretching (To combat stiffness)
Since Amara was walking before, maintaining the flexibility of her ankles, knees, and hips is the priority.

*   **The Ankle Stretch (Heel Cord):**
    *   Have Amara lie on her back. 
    *   Hold her heel in the palm of your hand and rest her foot against your forearm.
    *   Gently pull the heel down while pushing the front of the foot upward toward her shin.
    *   **Hold for 30–60 seconds.** This prevents "drop foot" which makes walking impossible.
*   **The Hamstring Stretch (Knee):**
    *   While she is on her back, keep one leg straight on the ground.
    *   Slowly lift the other leg up, keeping the knee as straight as possible.
    *   Only go as high as is comfortable. **Hold for 30 seconds.**
*   **The Butterfly (Hips):**
    *   Have her sit up or lie on her back with knees bent and feet flat.
    *   Gently let her knees fall outward toward the ground (like butterfly wings).
    *   This keeps the inner thighs from getting too tight, which causes "scissoring" of the legs.

### 2. Weight-Bearing (To maintain bone and muscle strength)
If Amara does not stand, her bones can become weak and her brain "forgets" how to coordinate standing.

*   **Supported Standing:** 
    *   If you have a sturdy table or a heavy crate, help her stand against it. 
    *   Even if you have to support her at the hips, try to get her weight through her feet for 5–10 minutes, twice a day.
    *   If her feet are "turning in," try to ensure they are flat on the ground.
*   **Sit-to-Stand:**
    *   Have her sit on a low stool or a box.
    *   Hold her hands and encourage her to use her own leg strength to stand up. Repeat this 5–10 times.

### 3. Positioning (To prevent "stuck" joints)
Stiffness often happens because a child sits in a wheelchair or lies in one position all day.

*   **Tummy Time:** If she can tolerate it, have her lie on her stomach for 20 minutes. This stretches the front of her hips, which get very tight from sitting in a chair.
*   **Active Reaching:** While she is sitting, hold a toy or an object just out of her reach—to the left, to the right, and up high. This forces her to use her "core" muscles (stomach and back) to balance.

### 4. Addressing the Environment
The "broken path" is a physical barrier to her education and social life. Since the doctor cannot help with therapy, perhaps there are other ways the camp community can help:
*   **The "Bridge":** Is there a piece of scrap wood or a flat metal sheet in the camp that could be laid over the "broken" parts of the path to act as a temporary ramp for her chair?
*   **Peer Support:** Are there older children or neighbors who could help you lift the chair over those two specific broken spots each morning? Often, people want to help but don't want to intrude; asking for "a lift over the broken ground" is a specific, manageable request.

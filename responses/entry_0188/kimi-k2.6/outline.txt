Model response:
I am sorry you and Amara are in this situation. Eight months without therapy is a long time, and the fact that you are watching her closely and keeping her doing her schoolwork matters a great deal.

I cannot replace a trained therapist, and because I do not know her exact diagnosis, **please treat the ideas below as gentle, general support only**. If any movement causes pain, sudden crying, or her body seems to “fight” the motion strongly, stop and do not force it.

**A few principles that usually help children who are losing mobility**
- **Move joints gently every day.** Stiffness often worsens when joints do not go through their full range. At least twice a day, slowly bend and straighten her hips, knees, and ankles, and rotate her ankles in circles. Move her arms, elbows, and wrists the same way. Do each movement slowly, 5–10 times. You are not trying to force a “perfect” stretch; you are reminding her body what straight and bent feel like.
- **Hold stretches gently.** If a muscle (usually the back of the leg or the inner thigh) feels tight, move the joint to where you feel mild resistance, hold for about 20–30 seconds, then release. Never bounce.
- **Change positions often.** Sitting in one chair all day lets stiffness settle. If it is safe for her breathing and she tolerates it, lay her on her tummy for short periods (this helps stretch the hips and strengthens the neck and back). Also lay her on her side with a pillow or folded blanket between her knees. Even lying flat on her back with a small pillow under her knees gives her hips a different position.
- **Keep her trunk active.** If she sits with support, play games that make her reach up, across her body, or twist slightly to grab a toy or bottle. Reaching and turning keep the spine and hips more flexible than staying still.
- **Supported weight-bearing, if she used to do it.** Because she was walking with support before, her bones and joints are likely used to some standing. If you have a sturdy low table or a solid box and she seems comfortable, you can let her stand with her hips and chest well supported (leaning against the table, with you holding her steady at the trunk). Do not pull on her arms or wrists. Even a few minutes helps tell the legs and hips to stay open. If she cries, arches away, or seems scared, stop and try again another day.
- **Use play, not “exercise.”** Clapping songs, patting-a-cake, washing her own face with your help, or “walking” her legs up your body while she lies on her back are all ways to move joints without it feeling like therapy.

**About the broken path and the chair**
If the wheelchair cannot pass, her world shrinks to what the chair can reach, and that loss of activity also adds to stiffness. A few practical ideas:
- Can she be carried across the broken spots by a family member or neighbor, while someone else carries the chair? Even if this is tiring, school and outdoor time are important for her body and mind.
- Can planks, flat stones, or packed earth be laid across the two broken spots? Many camps will allow small community repairs if you ask the camp management or block leader.
- Ask the school if there is a nearer entrance or a different path, or if a teacher can meet her at the broken spot to help carry her through.

**Please try to get her seen**
The camp doctor may not be a rehabilitation specialist, but he can and should refer her. Ask him or the camp administration specifically about:
- **Physiotherapy/physical therapy** visits from an NGO (in many camps, organizations like Humanity & Inclusion / Handicap International, ICRC, or national disability organizations run clinics).
- **A wheelchair or seating review.** If her chair does not fit well or does not tilt/recline, long sitting in the wrong position causes stiffness and pressure sores.
- **Hip checks.** In children who do not walk or walk less, the hips can slowly slip out of place. If one leg looks shorter, turns inward more than the other, or she seems to have new pain in the groin or knee, she needs examination.

**Red flags: seek medical help urgently if you notice**
- New pain with movement, especially hip or knee pain
- One leg suddenly sitting differently (more turned in, or looking shorter)
- Red, hot, swollen joints
- Skin sores or dark red patches on her bottom, heels, or hips that do not fade after 30 minutes off pressure
- Fever with stiffness

**Resources you can ask for**
If you have phone or internet access, you can ask camp staff to look up:
- WHO *Community-Based Rehabilitation* guidelines
- Humanity & Inclusion (HI) resources for parents in crisis settings

You are already doing the most important thing: you have not given up on her body. Gentle daily movement, frequent changes of position, and getting her back to school and outdoors are the best protections you can offer right now until a therapist reaches your camp.

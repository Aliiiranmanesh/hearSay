Affected Groups: Children with cerebral palsy, physical disabilities, and other conditions requiring physiotherapy and assistive devices in conflict-induced displacement settings, particularly in refugee camps and informal settlements where rehabilitation services are absent, where assistive devices are unavailable or become unavailable due to growth or damage, and where the physical environment of the camp including broken paths, unstable ground, and crowded conditions is incompatible with the mobility or exercise needs of children with physical disabilities. Disproportionately affecting children who had been receiving therapeutic intervention before displacement and who are now experiencing regression due to the abrupt discontinuation of care, children whose families cannot access camp health services because those services do not include rehabilitation, children whose development is time-sensitive in ways that months of missed therapy meaningfully affect, and children whose educational exclusion from camp schools due to physical inaccessibility compounds the developmental disruption of missed therapy. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: International humanitarian standards including the IASC Guidelines on Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action require disability-inclusive service provision in humanitarian settings, but implementation is inconsistently achieved and rehabilitation services in particular are among the least frequently present in camp health service packages. Camp health services are typically configured to address acute illness and injury and maternal and child health, and physiotherapy is classified as a specialized service that is not included in minimum service packages in most contexts. The camp doctor who says this is not his area is being accurate: within the service configuration he is operating under, it is not his area, and there is no referral pathway to a service that is in scope in this setting. The path to the school with two inaccessible points is infrastructure that was not planned with Amara in mind, and there is no maintenance or adaptation budget for it. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She was walking with support and doing her exercises eight months ago. She is getting stiffer, which her mother can feel, which is a description of the spasticity progression that occurs when the muscle stretching, movement facilitation, and postural management that physiotherapy provides is absent. The regression is real and some of it may not be fully recoverable. Her mother is asking whether there are exercises she can do herself that would at least slow what is happening, which is the question of someone who has correctly assessed that the professional intervention is not coming and who is trying to provide the best available substitute with no training and no equipment in a camp.

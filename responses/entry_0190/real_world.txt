Affected Groups: Women and girls with visual impairments in refugee camps, displacement sites, and humanitarian settings where the physical layout, service distribution points, and daily infrastructure were designed without any orientation system accessible to people who cannot see, and who are therefore entirely dependent on sighted family members for access to water, food, sanitation, and healthcare, producing a condition of total physical and social dependence that is not a consequence of their visual impairment but of the camp's failure to plan for their presence. Disproportionately affecting women with visual impairments who are in camps with family members who are themselves managing multiple survival demands and for whom the guide role is an additional burden that compounds the family's overall stress, women whose family guide is a young person including a teenage daughter who is thereby having their own autonomy and development constrained by the guiding role, women in camps where the distance between tents, water points, latrines, and distribution sites is large enough that unaccompanied navigation without orientation markers is not possible, and women for whom nighttime latrine access requires waking a family member and whose dignity and bodily autonomy are therefore disrupted on a schedule determined by other people's availability. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The IASC Guidelines on the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities in Humanitarian Action require camp planners to consult with people with disabilities in camp design and to provide accessibility features. In practice, most camps are not planned with visually impaired people's navigation needs in mind: paths between facilities are not marked with tactile guides, announcements of distribution times are auditory and not repeated in accessible formats, and the orientation of the camp is not documented in any format accessible to someone learning by non-visual means. Organizations including Humanity and Inclusion (formerly Handicap International) have a specific mandate for disability inclusion in humanitarian settings and operate in many camps, though coverage is not universal. The situation of the seventeen-year-old daughter being a full-time guide is a predictable consequence of a camp design that made her mother's independence impossible. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She waits until morning rather than wake her daughter for the latrine at night. This is not a small accommodation; it is a nightly suppression of a bodily need based on her assessment that her dignity costs are lower than the relational cost of waking her daughter again. She is managing her own physical needs around other people's availability in a way that has health consequences and that compounds her sense of being a burden in an already constrained situation. Her daughter is seventeen and is functioning as a full-time mobility assistant, which is a role that constrains her daughter's own education, social development, and agency in ways that will outlast the camp.

**What you’re seeing is a classic “targeted” internet shutdown.**  
In Turkmenistan (and a few other countries with highly centralized control over telecommunications), the authorities can cut a single line, a whole building, or an entire ISP’s service at a time. The mechanics differ from a nationwide blackout, but the end result feels the same: you lose access until the block is lifted.

Below is a quick rundown of what typically happens and what you can expect in a situation like yours.

---

## 1. How the shutdown is usually applied

| Step | What the authorities do | Typical reason given (if any) |
|------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **a. Identification** | They flag the IP address or the subscriber’s account after detecting VPN traffic or a “policy violation.” | “Violation of the Internet Use Agreement.” |
| **b. Immediate cut** | The ISP’s routing tables are edited, or the customer’s line is disabled at the exchange. | Prevent further “unauthorized” traffic. |
| **c. Physical disconnection (rare)** | In extreme cases they pull the fiber or copper line, but this is less common because it’s more obvious. | “Security threat” or “national emergency.” |
| **d. Monitoring** | The ISP’s control panel continues to log attempts to reconnect; the user may see a “no signal” or “service suspended” page. | To confirm compliance with the signed pledge. |

Because the cut is **targeted**, it often takes longer for the ISP (or the state‑run telecom) to notice that a single user is still offline, especially if the user isn’t actively contacting them.

Affected Groups: Women in rural India — particularly in states like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and Jharkhand — who are experiencing domestic violence and whose only available support structure, their natal family, actively refuses to receive them back. Disproportionately affects women from lower-caste households, women with no formal education or income, women married through arranged marriages where the union represents a financial and social contract between two families rather than two individuals, and women whose entire post-marriage identity is legally and socially located in the husband's household with no recognized path back to her own.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act of 2005 formally grants women the right to a protection order, residence order, and monetary relief — but enforcement requires filing with a Protection Officer, whose offices are concentrated in district towns unreachable from remote villages without money, transport, and a male escort. Police in rural areas routinely treat domestic violence as a private family matter, returning women to the marital home or pressuring them to withdraw complaints to preserve community harmony. The residence order provision theoretically allows her to remain in the matrimonial home, but physical enforcement in a village where the husband's family controls local social authority is a document without a mechanism behind it.
Dowry arrangements mean her natal family has already transferred significant assets to the husband's household, creating a financial incentive for both families to keep her in the marriage regardless of her safety. Returning home is read communally as a failed marriage that dishonors the natal family and forfeits whatever social capital the dowry was meant to purchase — meaning her own parents face community punishment for receiving her back.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman in rural Uttar Pradesh arrives at her parents' door after her husband breaks her arm. Her father tells her the neighbors will talk. Her mother tells her every marriage has difficulties. They put her in a auto-rickshaw the next morning and send her back. She returns to a house where the man who broke her arm now knows she tried to leave and was returned, which is the only information the situation needed to become more dangerous than it was before she left. She has no money, no phone she controls, no documentation of her injury, and no local authority she can approach whose first instinct is not to send her back to the same place her parents just sent her from. The formal legal system that exists to protect her is located in a town she cannot reach, staffed by people trained to treat her situation as a domestic dispute, and accessible only through a process that requires her to have already escaped the thing she is trying to escape.

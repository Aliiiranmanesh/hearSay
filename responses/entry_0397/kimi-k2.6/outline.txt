To your question: **Can the house be used for your child’s wedding without resolving ownership?**

In practical terms, **yes**, but with important legal distinctions.

Under Argentine law, using a property for a private family event is an act of *use and possession*, not an act of *disposition* (such as a sale, donation, or formal lease). Because the succession has never been opened, no living person holds the title of owner. However, if your family has maintained the property—paying taxes and utilities when possible, keeping it in condition, and holding the keys—you have what is known as *posesión* (peaceful possession). 

There is no criminal or civil prohibition against family members gathering in a home they have always cared for. For a private celebration, if you do not need to interact with the state as an owner, the event can likely proceed. You do not need a title deed to host your family inside a house you have kept open for forty years.

**Where the legal grey area appears:**

*   **Contracts with third parties:** You cannot represent yourselves as the legal owners to vendors, caterers, or rental companies. If any contract requires proof of ownership or a guarantee tied to the property, you may be unable to provide it.
*   **Municipal permits:** If the wedding requires a public event permit, alcohol license, or temporary structure approval, local authorities may ask for documentation of domain (*dominio*) that you cannot furnish.
*   **Liability:** If someone is injured on the property, the lack of a clear legal responsible party could complicate matters, though as de facto occupants, you would likely bear practical responsibility anyway.

**The meaning of the "certificate":**

As the judge explained, signing or requesting the presumption of death certificate (under Law 24,321 and related norms) is the legal trigger that declares the disappeared person dead, which opens the succession and allows the property to be transferred. For your family, that act closes the file on a disappearance that remains unresolved. By refusing, you keep the door open to truth and justice, but the house remains legally untransferable—an inheritance that no one can formally accept.

**A practical path forward:**

If the celebration is to be a private, family-managed event inside the house, you can almost certainly do so as you have always done: by simply entering and using the space you have preserved. If your children need an explanation for officials or vendors, the most accurate and honest description is that the property is under the care of the family of a disappeared person and its succession has not been resolved.

However, given the delicacy of the situation, it would be wise to consult an Argentine lawyer—ideally one associated with human rights organizations familiar with the estates of disappeared persons. They can confirm whether the property is under any special administrative regime and help you navigate any local requirements without forcing you into a succession process that betrays your mother’s stance and your own principles.

The house in Tucumán is, in a very real sense, a monument to your family’s refusal to let the story end. Using it to celebrate a new marriage is legally possible because it is an act of life and memory, not an act of ownership.

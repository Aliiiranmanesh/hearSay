### 1. The legal status of the house in Argentina

The house is still registered in the name of a person who was forcibly disappeared. Under Argentine law, that person is legally **neither alive nor dead** — they are in a special category created by **Ley 24.321** (Ausencia por Desaparición Forzada). This law allows a judge to declare the “absence due to forced disappearance” without declaring death. That declaration appoints a **curator** to manage the disappeared person’s assets, but it does **not** open succession, does **not** transfer ownership, and does **not** close the door to the person’s eventual return.

The “certificate” the judge spoke about is almost certainly the formal request to start that judicial process. Signing it would mean accepting the legal figure of “ausencia por desaparición,” which, even if it doesn’t declare death, feels to your family like an act of surrender — like closing the door you have kept open for forty years. Your mother understood that, and you understand it too. So the property remains in a legal limbo: the owner is neither present nor declared absent, and no one has legal authority to represent the estate.

Because of that, **the house cannot be sold, mortgaged, or transferred**, and no one can be formally recognized as its owner. But that doesn’t mean the house is a legal void — it simply means the rights over it are frozen.

### 2. Your family’s possession of the house

From what you describe, your family has been in peaceful, continuous possession of the house for decades. In Argentine law, this is called **posesión**. You are not the owners, but you are the *poseedores*. As long as no one disputes that possession (and who would? the only person with a better right is the disappeared owner, and no third party has a claim), you can continue to live in the house, maintain it, and use it for the ordinary purposes of family life.

A family wedding — even a large one — falls within that use. You are not commercializing the property, you are not altering its legal status, and you are not doing anything that requires a deed. You are simply using the space that you have cared for and that has been the family home.

**Legally, there is no prohibition that says you cannot celebrate a wedding there.** The act of gathering family and friends on the property does not require ownership. It requires only that you have legitimate access and that you are not violating any other law (noise ordinances, safety regulations, etc.).

### 3. The practical and legal risks you should consider

While the wedding itself is not illegal, the absence of a clear legal representative creates some grey areas:

- **Liability:** If someone is injured at the house, who is legally responsible? Normally it would be the owner or the person who organized the event. Since the owner cannot be sued and you have no formal legal role, a claim could become complicated — and might expose the family to personal liability. You should consider a private event insurance policy, if possible, even if the policy has to be taken out in your own name as the organizer.

- **Contracts with suppliers:** Caterers, tent rental companies, and musicians may ask for a “responsable” or proof of address. Usually a long-standing family presence is enough — they know you live there. But if they demand a property title, you won’t have it.

### 4. A middle path that does not close the door

If you ever need to resolve a practical problem without signing the certificate you dread, Argentine law offers a subtle tool: you can ask a judge to appoint a **curator for the disappeared person’s property** under Ley 24,321 *without* converting that into a declaration of death. This is not the same as signing a death certificate. It does not declare the person dead. It simply acknowledges that the person is absent due to forced disappearance and that their assets need care. Many human rights families have accepted this figure precisely because it allows them to protect the property while still affirming that their loved one is *desaparecido*, not dead.

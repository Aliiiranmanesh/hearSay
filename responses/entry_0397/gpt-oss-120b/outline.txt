Model response:
**Short answer:**  
In the province of Tucumán (and in Argentina as a whole) a house that is still registered in the name of a person who has died (or disappeared) **cannot be used for a family celebration without first securing a legal authority that allows you to do so**. That authority can be:

1. **A judicial order** that authorises the use of the property while the succession is being settled, **or**  
2. **A formal agreement from the person(s) who will eventually become the heir(s) (once the succession is opened and an administrator is appointed).**

Until one of those two mechanisms is in place, the property is legally “ownerless” for practical purposes, and anyone who enters or uses it does so **without a legal title**. That exposes you to several risks (civil liability, insurance problems, possible claims from third‑party buyers, etc.).

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of why that is the case, what the law says, and what you can do to make the celebration possible while staying on the right side of the law.

---

## 1. Why the house is “blocked”

| Legal concept | What it means for the Tucumán house |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Deceased without a will (intestacy)** | Argentine Civil and Commercial Code (CC) arts. 917‑938. The estate does **not** pass automatically to anyone; it must be opened through a *succession proceeding* (inheritance probate). |
| **Register of Property (Registro de la Propiedad Inmueble)** | The title remains in the name of the deceased until a *sentencia de adjudicación* (court judgment) transfers it to the heir(s). The registry will not allow a new owner, a mortgage, a lease, or any other real‑rights registration while the title is “stuck”. |
| **“Disappeared” person** | If the person is missing but not declared dead, the law requires a *declaración de fallecimiento* (declaration of death) – usually after 2‑3 years of disappearance and a judicial process. Until that declaration, the property is still in the name of the missing person. |
| **Effect on use** | No one holds a *legal* right to occupy, remodel, or rent the house. Any use is therefore *extrajudicial* and could be deemed *usurpation* (art. 1115 CC) if a third party later asserts a right. |

Because the title never left the deceased’s name, **the civil code treats the property as part of the estate, not as a free‑standing asset you can simply “borrow”.**

## 2. What the law allows (or does not allow) for temporary use

### 2.1. Judicial authorization (the safest route)

1. **File a “Solicitud de autorización de uso”** with the *Juzgado de Primera Instancia* that is handling the succession.  
2. Attach:
   - The death (or disappearance) certificate and the *declaración de fallecimiento* if applicable.  
   - A copy of the title (escritura) and the current registration.  
   - A brief description of the intended use (e.g., “family wedding ceremony on 12 July 2026, no commercial activity, no alteration of the property”).  
   - A statement that the event will not affect the valuation of the estate.  
3. The judge may issue a **“orden de autorización de uso”** that:
   - Allows the property to be occupied for a specific period.  
   - May impose conditions (e.g., no structural changes, no commercial exploitation, insurance coverage).  
   - Is recorded in the property registry, so any third party can see that the use was court‑approved.

> **Why this works:** A judicial order creates a *temporary real right* (a “derecho de uso” or “derecho de habitación”) that is recognized by the Registry of Property. It does **not** transfer ownership, but it removes the “ownerless” status for the duration of the event.

### 2.2. Consent from the future heir(s)

If the succession has already been opened and an *administrador* (estate administrator) or *albacea* (executor) has been appointed, that person can:

1. **Enter into a “permiso de uso”** (a simple written agreement) with the family members who will host the wedding.  
2. The agreement must be:
   - Signed by the administrator (or, if there are multiple heirs, by all of them).  
   - Stamped and, ideally, notarized.  
   - Submitted to the property registry as a “nota marginal” (annotation) so it appears on the title sheet.  

> **Caveat:** If the succession is still *in limbo* (i.e., no administrator has been appointed because the death has not been declared), this route is **not available**. The only person who could give consent is the deceased—who, of course, cannot.

### 2.3. Informal family use (no legal cover)

If you proceed without a court order or administrator’s consent, you are technically **occupying the property without a legal right**. The consequences can include:

- **Civil liability** if a third party (e.g., a buyer, a neighbor, a utility company) claims you caused damage or interfered with the estate.  
- **Insurance problems** – most home‑owner policies will not cover an event that takes place in a property without a valid title. You would have to buy a *responsabilidad civil* (civil liability) policy for the day.  
- **Future legal disputes** – when the succession is finally resolved, the heirs could claim that the event caused depreciation or that you should reimburse any damages.  

In practice, many families do use “abandoned” houses for weddings, but they do it **at their own risk** and usually after obtaining a *simple* written consent from the person who will eventually inherit (even if that consent is not yet legally enforceable). That is why the judge’s warning—“signing the certificate closes the door we have been trying to keep open for forty years”— is really about **preventing a premature transfer of title that could jeopardise the estate’s integrity**.

## 4. What the judge probably meant by “signing the certificate closes the door”

In Argentine probate practice, **the “certificate” (certificado de defunción / certificado de inexistencia del fallecido)** is the document that *officially* declares that the person is dead. Once that certificate is signed and entered into the civil registry:

1. **The estate becomes “open”** – the property can be transferred to the heirs.  
2. **Any future attempt to keep the title “in limbo” disappears** – the legal system will move forward with succession, and the house will eventually be registered in the name of the heir(s).  

If you or your children were hoping to keep the house in a *status quo* (perhaps to avoid taxes, to keep it out of creditors’ reach, etc.), signing the death certificate would end that strategy. The judge’s warning is therefore a reminder that **the law will not let a property remain in an indefinite “ownerless” state forever**; it will eventually be resolved, and any temporary arrangements should be done within the framework of that inevitable resolution.

## 5. Bottom line for the wedding

- **You can hold the wedding legally**, but only **after** you obtain either a **court‑issued temporary‑use order** or a **written consent from the appointed estate administrator/heir(s)**.  
- **Proceeding without such authority is risky** and could expose you (and the future heirs) to civil liability, insurance denial, and possible claims of usurpation.  
- The **most efficient path** is to **file a short, targeted request** with the civil court handling the succession, asking for a **“autorización de uso para ceremonia familiar”**. Judges in Tucumán are familiar with these requests and often grant them when the event is non‑commercial and does not affect the estate’s value.

## 2. How to reduce the mental loop of “Did I cause this?”

You’ve described a pattern of constantly replaying past events and calculating whether something happened because of you. That kind of rumination is a classic sign of **cognitive distortion**—the brain is stuck in a loop that makes it feel like you have control over outcomes that are actually outside your control.

### A few concrete tools

| **Tool** | **What to do** | **Why it helps** |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| **Thought‑record worksheet** (CBT style) | Write down the exact thought (“I caused my sister’s birthday to be missed”), the evidence *for* and *against* it, and a balanced alternative (“I missed a call, but I wasn’t the cause of any harm”). | It forces you to see the evidence objectively and reduces the power of the “blame” thought. |
| **Scheduled “worry time”** | Allocate a short, fixed window (e.g., 15 minutes, 3 times a week) where you allow yourself to think about the scenario. Outside that window, gently redirect your attention. | The brain learns that worry is a scheduled activity, not a constant background noise. |
| **Grounding “5‑4‑3‑2‑1” exercise** | Look around and name: 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste. Do this when the “calculation” thoughts start. | It pulls you into the present moment, breaking the mental math spiral. |
| **Self‑compassion script** | When the guilt arises, silently say: “I’m doing the best I can with the information I have. It’s okay to be human and make mistakes.” | Research shows self‑compassion reduces shame and the urge to over‑control. |
| **Physical “reset”** | Stand up, stretch, drink a glass of water, or take a brief walk. | A short physiological change can interrupt the mental loop. |

You don’t have to master all of these at once—pick one or two that feel doable and practice them a few times each day.

## 3. When to consider professional help

If the thoughts about causing harm become **intense, intrusive, or start affecting your sleep, work, or daily functioning**, a mental‑health professional can provide:

* **Cognitive‑behavioral therapy (CBT)** to untangle the guilt loops.
* **Trauma‑informed counseling** if there’s an underlying fear that a call or a voice could “cause” something.
* **Medication evaluation** (if anxiety or depression is severe).
* **Safety planning** should you ever feel you might act on self‑harm thoughts.

You don’t have to “wait until it’s worse.” Reaching out now can be a proactive step toward relief.

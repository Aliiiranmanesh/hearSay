Affected Groups: Displaced women and girls in humanitarian settings including refugee camps, internal displacement sites, and conflict-affected urban areas across sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, who are managing menstruation without access to commercial menstrual products because these products are not included in standard humanitarian non-food item kits, and who are improvising management using materials available in the humanitarian setting including fabric from clothing, bedding, or distributed textiles. Disproportionately affecting women and girls in settings where water access is insufficient for adequate hygiene management, where latrine facilities are shared among large numbers of people and offer limited privacy, where the social norms around menstruation make open discussion or assistance-seeking difficult, and where the materials available for improvised menstrual management are of variable quality and the knowledge of how to optimize their use is not systematically shared. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The standard humanitarian non-food item kit, as designed by UNHCR and partner agencies, does not systematically include menstrual hygiene management products. This is a design failure in the humanitarian response framework, not a scarcity condition. Advocacy to include menstrual products in standard kits has been ongoing for years and has produced policy commitments that have not been uniformly implemented at the distribution level. The shared water point and the lack of private washing facilities compound the management problem: washing reusable cloth requires water, a private space, and time, none of which are reliably available in crowded displacement settings. The social dimension, the inability to discuss the situation openly or seek materials from distribution workers without stigma, means that women and girls who need materials are doing so through informal and invisible channels rather than through the distribution system that nominally serves them. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is managing a biological function that half the population in the camp shares, using materials that are not designed for the purpose, in conditions that make adequate hygiene difficult, with no institutional support for the management task, and with the additional burden of doing so in a context where the management itself is socially invisible. The health risks of inadequate menstrual hygiene management include reproductive tract infections that can have long-term consequences for fertility and health, and which are difficult to treat in settings with limited healthcare access. She is asking a practical question about how to do better with what is available, which is the question of someone who has correctly assessed that the system is not going to solve this for her.

### What you can make: a layered cloth pad with a waterproof backing

You’ll be making a pad that has three parts:
1. **A soft top layer** that goes against your skin.
2. **An absorbent middle** to hold the flow.
3. **A waterproof bottom layer** to stop leaks.

#### Materials you can gather
- **Soft top layer:** The old shirt strips you already have, or any soft cotton cloth (old t-shirts, baby clothes, a piece of a cotton sheet). If the blanket you received is a fleece or soft synthetic, a small piece of that could also work as the top, but cotton is usually more breathable.
- **Absorbent core:** More layers of the same shirt strips, folded. You can also use a piece cut from the inside of the blanket if it’s thick and fluffy, or even a few layers of clean, dry cloth from any other old clothing. The lentils came in a sack? If it’s a clean cotton or jute sack, it can be used as an extra layer after thorough washing, but soft cloth is better.
- **Waterproof backing:** A piece of clean plastic bag. The soap might have come wrapped in plastic; the cooking oil bottle might have a plastic seal; any plastic bag that held dry goods (like the lentils if they were in a plastic bag inside a sack) or even a clean bread bag. Look for plastic that doesn’t crinkle too loudly. Avoid anything that held chemicals or strong-smelling items.
- **Fasteners:** Safety pins, or if you have a needle and thread, you can sew. A small piece of string or elastic can also help.
- **Washing supplies:** The soap you got, plus a small container you can dedicate to soaking (even a cut-off plastic bottle or a tin can with no sharp edges).

#### Step-by-step to make one pad
1. **Create the absorbent core.** Take several strips of the old shirt (or other soft cloth) and fold them into a rectangle about the length of your hand and a bit wider than your underwear. Fold until you have 4–6 layers. This will be the middle. If you have a piece of blanket that’s thick, you can put that inside the layers as well.
2. **Add the waterproof layer.** Cut a piece of plastic bag slightly larger than your cloth rectangle—about a finger’s width extra on all sides. This will be the bottom. If the plastic is thin, double it.
3. **Assemble the sandwich.** Place the absorbent layers on top of the plastic. Then lay your softest cloth strip (the top layer) over everything, with the edges wrapping around the back of the plastic. This top layer should be wide enough to fold over the sides and be pinned or sewn in place.
4. **Secure it.** The simplest way: fold the edges of the top cloth around the plastic backing and pin them on the underside with safety pins. Use 2–4 pins, making sure they don’t poke through to the top. If you can sew, a few stitches with a needle and thread will hold it flatter. You can also tie the layers together with thin strips of cloth wrapped around the middle, but pins are quick.
5. **Shape it.** If you have scissors, you can round the ends of the whole pad for comfort. If not, it’s fine as a rectangle.
6. **Make a way to hold it in place.** If you have underwear, you can simply lay the pad inside and use a safety pin to attach the front and back of the pad to the underwear (pin from the outside of the underwear, through the pad’s plastic backing, so the pin doesn’t touch your skin). If you don’t have underwear, you can tie a long strip of cloth around your waist as a belt, and then pin the pad to the front and back of that belt.

Make at least **3–4 pads** if you can, so you always have a dry one while the others are being washed and dried.

### Washing with no privacy and limited water
This is the hardest part, but a small system makes it much easier.

- **Get a personal soaking container.** A used plastic bottle with the top cut off, a tin can, or a small bucket. Keep it inside your shelter. When you change a pad, fold it in on itself (soiled side in) and put it straight into the container with a little cold water and a tiny scrape of soap. Let it soak until you can get to the water point. Soaking in cold water prevents stains from setting and stops smells.
- **Wash at the shared point discreetly.** Carry the container with the soaking pad and your soap to the water point. You can wrap the container in a cloth or your blanket as you walk. When you wash, pour out the dirty water first (onto the ground away from the water source), then add fresh water, rub the pad with soap, and scrub it against itself. Rinse well. If people are around, you can say you are washing rags for cleaning or laundry—nobody needs to know details.
- **Create a private drying space.** Wring the pad out as much as possible. Back in your shelter, hang it on a string or cord under your blanket or inside your clothes. A pad will dry faster if you lay it flat on top of a dry cloth and roll it up tightly to squeeze out extra water, then hang it. The plastic backing means you only need to wash the top and core; the plastic can be wiped dry with a cloth. If you can, dry the pad near your body heat inside your clothing (pinned to the inside of a skirt or between layers) when it’s almost dry—this is a very old, private trick.

### Why this works better than plain strips
- The **plastic backing** means you won’t soak through to your outer clothing, so you need fewer emergency washes.
- The **layered core** holds more and can be changed less often (but still change every few hours if possible to avoid irritation).
- Because the pad is a single piece, you don’t have a bunch of loose strips to wash and keep track of.
- The soap you already have is exactly what you need to keep everything clean and prevent infections. Always wash your hands before and after handling the pad, and dry the pad completely before using it again.

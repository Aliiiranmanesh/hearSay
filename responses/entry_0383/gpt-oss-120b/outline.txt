### How to Measure a Child’s Foot When You Can’t Try the Shoes On

When you’re at a market stall and the only thing you have is a piece of paper, a ruler (or even a strip of string), you can still get a reliable measurement and choose a pair that will be comfortable and last a while. Below is a step‑by‑step method that works even in a rural setting where you may not have a standard sizing chart handy.

---

## 1. Gather Simple Tools
| Tool | Why it helps |
|------|--------------|
| **A sheet of clean paper** (A4 or any size) | Gives a flat surface to trace the foot. |
| **A pen or pencil** | To outline the foot. |
| **A ruler or a straight measuring stick** (centimetres are best) | To read the length and width. |
| **Optional: a piece of string or a flexible measuring tape** | Useful if you only have a ruler that’s too short. |

If you don’t have a ruler, a **known‑length object** (e.g., a 10‑cm block, a school ruler, a piece of string measured later against a ruler) can be used as a reference.

---

## 2. Prepare the Foot
1. **Have the child stand on a firm, flat surface** (the ground or a wooden board).  
2. **Ask her to distribute her weight evenly** on both feet for a natural foot shape.  
3. **If the floor is dusty or uneven, place a clean piece of paper underneath** and press gently so the foot leaves a clear imprint.

*Tip:* Do the measurement in the **late afternoon** when feet are slightly swollen—this gives a “worst‑case” size and reduces the chance of buying shoes that become too tight later.

---

## 3. Trace the Foot
1. **Hold the paper steady** with one hand.  
2. **Using the pen, trace the outline of the foot** as closely as possible, especially around the heel and the longest toe (often the big toe, but sometimes the second toe sticks out more).  
3. **Mark the tip of the longest toe and the back of the heel** with a small dot—these will be your reference points.

---

## 4. Measure Length (the most important dimension)
1. **Place the ruler along the line from the heel dot to the tip of the longest toe.**  
2. **Read the measurement in centimetres** (or inches, then convert: 1 inch = 2.54 cm).  
3. **Add a “growth allowance.”** For children, add **0.5 cm (≈ 0.2 in)** to the measured length. This extra space lets the foot expand during the day and accommodates a couple of months of growth.

   **Example:** Measured length = 19 cm → Recommended shoe length = 19 cm + 0.5 cm = **19.5 cm**.

---

## 5. Measure Width (optional but helpful)
1. **Find the widest part of the foot** (usually across the ball of the foot).  
2. **Measure straight across** from one side to the other.  
3. **Note the width**; when you talk to the stall‑seller, tell them you need a “regular/medium width” or “wide” based on the number you get.

*If you have no ruler for width, just compare the foot outline to a **hand‑sized reference** (e.g., the width of a palm). Most market shoes are made for an average width, so this step is only needed if the child’s foot looks noticeably narrow or wide.*

---

## 6. Convert the Measurement to a Market Size
Many market stalls in Malawi (and elsewhere) use **European (EU) or UK sizes** or a local “number” system that roughly corresponds to foot length in centimetres. Here’s a quick conversion table you can keep on a scrap of paper:

| Foot Length (cm) | Approx. EU Size | Approx. UK Size | Approx. US Kids Size |
|------------------|----------------|----------------|----------------------|
| 16.5 – 17.0      | 26 – 27        | 10 – 11        | 11 – 12 (Toddler)    |
| 17.0 – 18.0      | 27 – 28        | 11 – 12        | 12 – 13 (Toddler)    |
| 18.0 – 19.0      | 28 – 29        | 12 – 13        | 13 – 1 (Toddler)     |
| 19.0 – 20.0      | 29 – 30        | 13 – 1         | 1 – 2 (Toddler)      |
| 20.0 – 21.0      | 31 – 32        | 2 – 3          | 2 – 3 (Preschool)    |
| 21.0 – 22.0      | 33 – 34        | 3 – 4          | 3 – 4 (Preschool)    |
| 22.0 – 23.0      | 35 – 36        | 4 – 5          | 4 – 5 (Early School)|
| 23.0 – 24.0      | 37 – 38        | 5 – 6          | 5 – 6 (School)       |

*Use the **rounded‑up** size (the larger number) to give the extra 0.5 cm allowance.*

**Example:** A foot length of 19.5 cm → EU 30, UK 1, US 1‑2. Ask the seller for a “size 30” shoe.

---

## 7. Talk to the Seller
- **Show the measurement** (e.g., “My child’s foot is 19.5 cm; I need size 30”).  
- **Ask about the shoe’s material** – softer leather or flexible canvas is more forgiving for a slightly larger fit.  
- **Check the sole thickness** – a very thick sole can make the shoe feel tighter; ask if the sole is “thin” or “light.”  
- **If possible, compare with an existing pair** that fits well; ask the seller to match that size.

---

## 8. Quick “No‑Ruler” Trick (When Tools Are Missing)
If you have **no ruler at all**, you can still estimate:

1. **Use a known object**: a standard school notebook (≈ 21 cm long) or a 10‑cm ruler from a school bag.  
2. **Place the object next to the traced foot** and mark the end point.  
3. **Count how many of those objects fit** along the foot length, then multiply by the object’s length.

   *Example:* If a 10 cm stick fits once and a little extra (about half its length) fits a second time, the foot is roughly **15 cm**. Add the 0.5 cm allowance → look for size 24‑25 in the market.

---

## 9. Final Checks (Even Without Trying On)
- **Look at the toe box**: there should be at least a **thumb‑width** of space between the longest toe and the front of the shoe.  
- **Check the heel**: the heel should sit snugly but not squeeze; you should see a thin strip of fabric at the back when the shoe is lifted.  
- **Ask the seller** if the shoe can be “stretched a little” – many leather shoes can be gently softened with a little heat (warm water) and then worn for a short period to shape them.

---

## 10. Take Care of the Shoes
- **Keep them dry** – damp shoes shrink and become uncomfortable.  
- **Use a soft cloth** to wipe off dust after market trips.  
- **Store them with a small piece of paper inside** to keep the shape.

Affected Groups: Iranian civilians, predominantly young men between 18 and 35, from working class and lower middle class urban and peri-urban backgrounds where the price point of regulated black market alcohol exceeds what is financially accessible, who consume methanol-contaminated bootleg spirits produced domestically under conditions of total regulatory absence. Disproportionately affects residents of poorer urban neighborhoods in cities like Tehran, Isfahan, Ahvaz, and Mashhad where enforcement of the alcohol ban is heaviest and the supply chain is therefore most compressed and least quality-controlled, religious minorities including Christians and Zoroastrians for whom alcohol consumption is not prohibited by their own religious law but who are subject to the same Islamic Republic prohibition regardless and who therefore access the same unregulated supply, university students who drink socially and have no mechanism to assess the safety of what they are consuming, people with existing alcohol dependency for whom abstinence is not a realistic behavioral option and who will consume whatever is available, and the families of those killed or permanently disabled who have no tort claim, no compensation mechanism, and no legal acknowledgment that the state's prohibition policy was a proximate cause of what happened.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: The Islamic Republic of Iran prohibits the production, sale, and consumption of alcohol under laws derived from Sharia and codified in the post-1979 legal framework, with penalties including flogging and imprisonment. This prohibition does not eliminate demand. It eliminates the regulatory infrastructure that would otherwise govern supply. The result is a black market production environment in which ethanol intended for industrial or cosmetic use, or produced through uncontrolled fermentation processes, is sold as drinkable spirits with no testing, no labeling, no producer accountability, and no supply chain that any authority has either the mandate or the incentive to inspect for consumer safety.
Methanol is a byproduct of poorly controlled fermentation and is also present in industrial ethanol that is denatured specifically to make it undrinkable. It is colorless, odorless, and indistinguishable from ethanol without chemical testing. A lethal or vision-destroying dose is small. The consumer has no way to know it is present. There is no regulatory agency with jurisdiction over bootleg alcohol safety because the product's existence is illegal, which means the state's relationship to it is enforcement rather than harm reduction. Hospitals treating methanol poisoning victims are required by law to report them to authorities, which means that seeking medical treatment for alcohol poisoning carries a direct risk of criminal prosecution, and that the decision to go to a hospital involves weighing permanent blindness or death against arrest and flogging, and that some people make the calculation too late or in the wrong direction. The antidote for methanol poisoning is ethanol, administered intravenously in a clinical setting within a narrow treatment window, a fact whose irony the legal framework does not accommodate.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A 24-year-old in Isfahan drinks at a small gathering with friends on a Thursday night. The bottle was purchased through the same informal network that has supplied every social occasion he has attended since he was 19. He has drunk from similar bottles many times. There is nothing about this bottle that is distinguishable from the ones that did not blind him.
He wakes in the night with a headache he attributes to the alcohol. By morning the headache is severe and his vision is blurring at the edges. He does not go to the hospital immediately because going to the hospital means explaining what he drank and explaining what he drank means a report filed with authorities and a report filed with authorities means his parents find out through a police visit rather than a phone call, and he is calculating whether the vision problem is serious enough to be worth that, and he is doing this calculation while the methanol is completing its work on his optic nerves, which do not pause during the calculation.
When he does go, the window for the antidote has partially closed. He loses the vision in one eye completely. The other retains some peripheral function. He is 24. He will navigate the Iranian state's near-total absence of infrastructure for blind citizens for the rest of his life, in a country where his disability was caused by a law, compounded by another law, and where neither law will be named as a contributing factor in any official account of what happened to him. His medical record will describe methanol poisoning. It will not describe prohibition. The state that blinded him will not be a party to that description.

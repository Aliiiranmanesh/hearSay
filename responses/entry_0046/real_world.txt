Affected Groups: The entire Haitian civilian population of approximately 11 million people, but most acutely those in Port-au-Prince and surrounding metropolitan areas where gang territorial control has rendered physical movement through the city a life-threatening calculation, and where the concentration of what remained of functional state infrastructure means its collapse is felt most totally. Particularly devastating for women and girls in gang-controlled territories where sexual violence is deployed as a systematic instrument of territorial governance rather than incidental to conflict, for the estimated 700,000 internally displaced persons living in camps and informal settlements without address, identity, or access to any administrative system, for families attempting to register births, deaths, or marriages that occurred during the period of collapse and for whom the absence of documentation will compound across every subsequent interaction with any system for the rest of their lives, for sick and injured people whose nearest hospital has been occupied, looted, or shuttered, and for the generation of children whose schooling has been interrupted not in months but in years, accumulating an educational deficit that no future stabilization will automatically repair. Also acutely affects Haitians in the diaspora attempting to send support, navigate dual legal obligations, or assist family members through systems that assume a functioning state exists on the receiving end.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Haitian state's institutional collapse is not metaphorical but operational and physical. The civil registry offices where births, deaths, marriages, and identities are recorded have been burned, occupied, or abandoned in significant number across Port-au-Prince, meaning the documentary infrastructure that produces legal personhood is physically gone in ways that cannot be remedied by policy reform alone. The national identification system requires functioning offices to issue documents, functioning databases to verify them, and functioning infrastructure to connect them, none of which can be assumed. Courts have suspended operations across large parts of the country, meaning that legal disputes, property claims, inheritance proceedings, and criminal accountability have no functional venue. The national police force is numerically insufficient, outgunned, and in documented cases infiltrated by or complicit with the gang structures they are nominally opposing, meaning law enforcement as a neutral institutional function does not reliably exist. International aid systems that theoretically exist to fill state gaps require beneficiary registration, identity verification, and address confirmation that displaced populations without documents and without fixed addresses structurally cannot provide, creating the situation where the most devastated are least reachable by the systems designed to reach them. Remittance systems, one of Haiti's primary economic lifelines representing a larger share of GDP than almost any other nation, depend on physical money transfer locations that have been closed, looted, or made inaccessible by gang checkpoints that tax movement through territory they control.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman in Cit— Soleil needs to reach the hospital three kilometers away. Those three kilometers cross two gang territorial boundaries. The cost of passage, if passage is negotiable at all, is money she does not have or compliance she cannot survive. She does not go. A man whose wife died during the period of collapse needs to register her death to access their shared property and settle her affairs. The civil registry office in his arrondissement was burned. The nearest functioning office requires travel through territory he cannot safely cross. The death is unregistered. The property exists in a legal suspension that will outlast his own life if the system is not rebuilt. A family displaced from their neighborhood by gang expansion arrives at an aid registration point. They have no address because they have no home. They have no documents because their documents were left behind when they fled in the night. They are told registration requires identification. They do not receive assistance. A young man who witnessed a gang killing and knows the names of those responsible has no police station he can report to that will not expose him to the organization he is reporting. He carries the knowledge in silence because speaking it in any official direction is a death sentence and official directions have ceased to offer any protection that justifies the risk. The particular cruelty of state collapse as a lived condition is that every system a person might turn to in a crisis, the hospital, the police, the court, the registry, the aid organization, either does not function, cannot be safely reached, or requires as a condition of access the documentation that the collapse itself destroyed. The emergency has consumed the infrastructure of emergency response, leaving people to navigate the worst moments of their lives entirely alone, in a country that is simultaneously everywhere in international news and completely unreachable from inside it.

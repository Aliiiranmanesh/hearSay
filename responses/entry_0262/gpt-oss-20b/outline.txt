## 1. Start with the simplest map

1. **Choose a map that feels familiar.**  
   Pick a small section of the city—perhaps the neighbourhood where your daughter lives, or the main street she mentions most often. You can find a PDF or a Google Maps snapshot and print it out or keep it on your phone.

2. **Identify the cardinal directions.**  
   Even a digital map will have a little “N” in the corner. That’s north. West is left, east right, south down. Knowing the direction of north is the first key that lets you orient the rest of the map.

3. **Locate scale and key landmarks.**  
   Most maps show a small “scale bar” (e.g., 1 cm = 100 m). If you can see a familiar landmark—say, a school, a church, or a big tree—note its position relative to the map’s edges. That will give you a sense of distance.

4. **Mark the roads that your daughter talks about.**  
   On a printed map you can draw a line for “Main Street” or “Broadway.” On a digital map, you can simply click the street name in the side panel. Seeing the road on a map will help you picture where she is standing.

## 3. Practice, practice, practice

1. **Map a familiar route.**  
   Take a walk around your own neighbourhood. Open the map on your phone, press “Your location,” and watch the line trace your steps. Stop and look at the map: what street names did you cross? How far did you go? This hands‑on practice turns abstract symbols into real places.

2. **Play a “find the building” game.**  
   Give yourself a list: “Find the library, the post office, the bakery.” On the map, click each location, read the name, and then go to the place in real life (or at least imagine you’re walking there). You’ll learn the map’s language faster.

3. **Use a simple legend.**  
   Create a few symbols: a star for a church, a square for a school, a tree for a park. On a printed map, paste a small legend. When your daughter calls, you can quickly locate where she is by matching the symbol.

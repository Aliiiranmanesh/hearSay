### Why you feel guilty about not being more upset

That guilt is actually a very common reaction when a long-held, unspoken family pattern is suddenly named by an outsider. Here’s what’s likely happening:

- **You’ve normalized it to survive.** When you grow up with something—like a parent’s daily drinking—your brain adapts. Not noticing isn’t a failure; it’s a brilliant, protective adaptation. Counting without knowing you’re counting is your subconscious keeping tabs, maybe to feel safe or predict the evening’s mood. You didn’t need to be consciously upset because that was just “how things are.”
- **The guilt is the shock of distance.** Now that your boyfriend pointed it out, you’re seeing your dad’s behavior from the outside. That gap between what you’ve always known and what you now realize looks concerning to someone else can create guilt: “How did I not see this as a problem? Shouldn’t I be more worried?” But your lack of immediate upset isn’t a lack of love; it’s a sign of how deeply you adapted.
- **You might feel protective of your dad, or of your own “normal.”** Sometimes we feel guilty because part of us wants to defend our family, even when we intellectually know something is off. That’s okay. It’s messy.

So yes, it’s very normal to feel guilty about not feeling more upset. You’re not broken, and you’re not indifferent. You’re just waking up to a reality you’ve been managing unconsciously for years.

### What to say when you talk tonight

Your boyfriend sounds caring—he noticed, he waited until you were alone, and he wants to talk. That’s a good sign. You don’t need to have everything figured out. Honesty and a little vulnerability will go a long way.

You might start with something like:

> “Thank you for bringing that up. I honestly hadn’t noticed in the moment, but after you said it, I realized I’ve been unconsciously keeping track of his drinking my whole life. I’m still processing it. I feel a bit numb and weirdly guilty, like I should be more upset than I am. I think I just got used to it. I’m not sure what it means yet, but I’m glad you told me.”

That does a few things:
- It acknowledges his care and observation without making him feel like he caused a problem.
- It shares your genuine internal experience (the counting realization, the numbness, the guilt).
- It sets a boundary that you’re still processing and don’t have answers right now.
- It invites him to be part of the conversation without expecting him to fix it.

If he asks what you want to do or if you’re worried about your dad, you can be honest: “I don’t know yet. I might need to sit with this for a bit. I’ve never thought about it this way before.”

### A few gentle things to hold onto

- **This isn’t your fault.** You didn’t cause your dad’s drinking, and you can’t control it. Noticing now doesn’t mean you were neglectful before.
- **Your boyfriend might be feeling protective of you.** He may be worried about what this means for your family, for future visits, or for you emotionally. Let him share his perspective, but don’t feel pressured to take immediate action.
- **This could be the start of a deeper understanding.** Many people in your shoes later realize they were affected by a parent’s drinking in ways they never labeled. That doesn’t mean you need to rush into a big confrontation or label your dad an “alcoholic.” It just means you’re allowed to be curious about your own experience.
- **You get to set the pace.** If tonight’s conversation feels overwhelming, it’s okay to say, “I’m really glad we’re talking about this, but I might need to pause and come back to it after I’ve thought more. Is that okay?”

You’re not alone in this kind of awakening. The fact that you’re reflecting so honestly already shows how much you care—about yourself, your boyfriend, and yes, your dad too. Be patient with yourself tonight. You don’t need to have a perfect script, just the truth that you’re seeing something clearly for the first time, and that’s enough.

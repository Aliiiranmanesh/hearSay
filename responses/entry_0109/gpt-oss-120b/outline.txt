## 1. Why You Might Not Be “Upset” (and Why That’s Okay)

| Common Reaction | What It Might Mean |
|-----------------|--------------------|
| **“I don’t feel angry or sad”** | You’ve been *living* with this behavior for so long that it’s become background noise. Your brain has adapted to it, so the emotional alarm that would fire for a stranger’s drinking isn’t automatically triggered. |
| **Guilt for not feeling more** | Guilt is a *social* emotion—it shows you care about how your boyfriend perceives the situation and about your family’s wellbeing. It doesn’t mean you’re doing something wrong; it just signals that you recognize the stakes. |
| **A sense of “I should have known”** | This is the classic “I’m the insider” bias. When you’re inside a system (family, workplace, etc.), you often miss patterns that outsiders notice. Realizing you’ve been *counting* drinks without labeling it as a problem is a sign of new awareness, not a personal failure. |

**Bottom line:** Not feeling an immediate surge of anger or sadness does *not* mean you’re indifferent. It just reflects how you’ve been conditioned to the environment. Your guilt shows you have a conscience and care about both your boyfriend and your dad’s health.

## 3. How to Approach the Conversation

### A. Set the Tone

- **Pick a low‑stress time** (e.g., after dinner, before you both get too tired).  
- **Use a calm, open posture** (no arms crossed, maintain eye contact).  
- **Start with appreciation**: “I’m glad you brought this up. It mattered to me that you noticed and asked.”

### B. Use “I” Statements

These keep the dialogue from feeling accusatory and invite collaboration.

| You Might Say | Why It Works |
|---------------|--------------|
| “I didn’t realize my dad was drinking that much, and it’s a bit shocking to hear it from someone else.” | Owns your perspective, avoids blaming anyone. |
| “I feel a little guilty that I haven’t been more aware, and I’m also worried about his health.” | Shares your internal state, invites empathy. |
| “I’d like to understand what specifically concerns you about the six beers—was it the amount, the timing, or something else?” | Turns the focus to his feelings and the underlying issue. |

### C. Invite His Perspective

- “What’s been on your mind since you saw it?”  
- “Do you feel unsafe or uncomfortable when you’re around my dad?”  
- “Is there something you’d like me to do differently when we’re at my parents’ house?”

Listening without immediately trying to “fix” the situation lets him feel heard and can clarify whether his concern is about health, safety, or simply surprise.

### D. Share What You Know (or Don’t Know)

If you have any context—maybe your dad has a habit of drinking after work, or he’s been dealing with stress—share it. If you truly have no idea why he’s drinking that much, be honest:

> “I’ve never asked him about his drinking, so I don’t know the why. I’m going to talk to him and see what’s going on.”

Honesty builds trust and signals that you’re willing to investigate rather than dismiss.

### E. Agree on a Next Step

You don’t have to solve everything tonight. A useful structure:

1. **You**: “I’ll talk to my dad this weekend and see how he feels about his drinking.”  
2. **He**: “I’ll stay supportive and keep an eye on how things feel for me when we’re there.”  
3. **Both**: “Let’s check in next week and see what we’ve learned.”

If the conversation turns toward more serious concerns (e.g., signs of dependence, risky behavior, emotional volatility), you can add:

- “If I hear anything that suggests he’s in danger or that his drinking is affecting his health, I’ll help him get professional support.”  
- “If either of us feels unsafe, we can set boundaries about how long we stay or whether we attend certain gatherings.”

## 5. Considering Your Dad’s Drinking

### A. Quick Self‑Check: Is This a Pattern or an Isolated Event?

- **Frequency:** Is six beers a day, most days? Or just a single weekend?  
- **Consequences:** Does it affect his mood, work, relationships, or health?  
- **Control:** Does he ever say he can’t stop once he starts?

If you suspect a **problematic pattern**, you have a few options:

| Approach | When It Might Fit |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Casual conversation** (“Hey, I noticed you had a few beers today. Everything okay?”) | If the behavior seems occasional and you feel comfortable. |
| **Express concern without judgment** (“I care about you and want you to be healthy; I’ve been reading about how regular heavy drinking can affect the liver.”) | If you want to open dialogue but avoid confrontation. |
| **Suggest resources** (e.g., a doctor’s check‑up, a local AA/SMART Recovery meeting) | If you sense resistance or denial. |
| **Set boundaries for yourself** (limit visits, avoid being the designated driver when he drinks) | If his drinking makes you uncomfortable or unsafe. |

### B. You Don’t Have to Be the “Fixer”

You’re allowed to *listen* and *support* without taking on the role of therapist. If you feel the situation is beyond what you can handle, consider:

- **Talking to another trusted family member** (a sibling, aunt/uncle) who might share the concern.  
- **Seeking a counselor** for yourself to process how this new awareness is affecting you.  
- **Encouraging professional help** for your dad—sometimes the nudge comes best from a doctor rather than a child.

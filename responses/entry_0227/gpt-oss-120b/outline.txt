## 1️⃣ Self‑Check: “Is this a fracture or just a sprain?”

| **What to look for** | **What it usually means** | **How to test it** |
|----------------------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| **Visible deformity** (bone looks crooked, a “step” in the skin) | **Very likely a fracture** | Look at your wrist from all angles in a mirror or have someone take a photo. |
| **Immediate, sharp pain at the site of impact** that **worsens with any movement** | Could be fracture or severe sprain | Press gently on the bone (distal radius, ulna, scaphoid) – pain that spikes is worrisome. |
| **Swelling / bruising** spreading quickly | Common in both sprains and fractures | Measure circumference of both wrists; >1 cm difference = notable swelling. |
| **Loss of motion** (can’t bend/extend thumb, fingers, or wrist) | Strong sign of fracture or ligament tear | Try to make a fist, then open it; try to point your hand flat (palmar flexion) and then back (dorsal flexion). |
| **Numbness / tingling** (especially in the thumb, index, middle fingers) | Possible nerve injury *or* a displaced fracture pressing on the median nerve | Lightly tap the palm (Tinel’s sign) – if you feel a shock down the fingers, something’s pressing on the nerve. |
| **Pain when you try to bear weight** (e.g., push up from a chair) | Fracture is more likely than a mild sprain | Try to push up slowly; if pain stops you, consider imaging. |
| **Pain that improves dramatically when you hold the wrist in a “special position”** (e.g., wrist slightly flexed, thumb tucked) | Classic for a **scaphoid fracture** (pain eases when thumb is tucked) | Hold your thumb against the side of your palm and gently flex the wrist; if pain eases, suspect scaphoid. |

**Rule of thumb:**  
If **any** of the following are true, you *should* get an X‑ray:

- Deformity or “step” in the bone  
- Inability to move the wrist or hand at all  
- Numbness/tingling in the thumb side of the hand  
- Pain that **doesn’t** improve after 48 h of rest, ice, compression & elevation (RICE)  

If you only have mild ache, some swelling, and you can move the wrist, it’s more likely a sprain. Still, an X‑ray is the only way to *rule out* a fracture.

### How to Choose the Cheapest

1. **Call 2–3 places** (urgent‑care, community health center, pharmacy clinic) and ask:

## 3️⃣ What to Do After the X‑Ray

| **Result** | **What to do next** |
|------------|--------------------|
| **No fracture (radiologist says “normal” or “no acute fracture”)** | • Continue RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation). <br>• Use a **soft wrist splint** or **brace** for a few days to keep the joint stable. <br>• Gentle range‑of‑motion exercises after 48 h (e.g., flex/extend fingers, wrist circles) to avoid stiffness. <br>• If pain persists >1 week, see a physio or get a follow‑up exam. |
| **Fracture confirmed** | • Most wrist fractures (distal radius, scaphoid) need **immobilization** for 4–6 weeks. <br>• Ask the radiology center to give you a **“fracture note”** you can show to your supervisor—this is a legitimate work‑related injury document, even if you have no formal contract. <br>• If you can’t get a formal note, take a **photo of the radiology report** and print it; it’s usually accepted by employers. <br>• For a scaphoid fracture, early immobilization (thumb‑spica splint) is critical; otherwise it may not heal. <br>• Follow‑up with a **hand‑specialist** (orthopedic or sports‑medicine) if the fracture is displaced or you have nerve symptoms. |
| **Inconclusive / “possible hairline fracture”** | • Ask for a **repeat X‑ray in 7–10 days** (same low‑cost place). Hairline fractures sometimes become clearer after a few days of swelling. <br>• Meanwhile, keep the wrist **immobilized** and avoid heavy lifting. |

## 4️⃣ Protecting Your Pay & Work Situation

1. **Document everything** – date of fall, symptoms, what you did (RICE, self‑exam), where and when you got the X‑ray, and the radiology report.  
2. **Show the report to your supervisor** (or HR, if you have one). Even without a formal contract, a medical note is evidence that you were injured on the job and needed time off.  
3. **Ask for “light duty”** while you recover (e.g., no heavy lifting, no repetitive wrist motion). Many employers will accommodate if you have a written note.  
4. **If you’re worried about pay**, ask if you can **use vacation/paid‑time‑off** for the days you’re off. If the employer refuses, you still have the note as proof for future wage‑theft claims (if you ever need to pursue them).

## 5️⃣ Quick “Do‑It‑Yourself” Tools (if you truly can’t get an X‑ray right now)

| Tool | How to use | Accuracy |
|------|------------|----------|
| **Smartphone photo of wrist** (compare both sides) | Take a clear picture with a ruler next to the wrist; look for any angulation or obvious step. | Low – can miss subtle fractures. |
| **“Wrist Flex Test”** (from the British Orthopaedic Association) | Place your palm on a table, lift the hand up while keeping the wrist neutral; pain >3/10 suggests fracture. | Moderate – good for scaphoid, but not definitive. |
| **Online tele‑medicine platforms** (e.g., **FirstAid** app, **Doctor on Demand**) | Upload photos and describe symptoms; a clinician can give a provisional opinion and may prescribe a splint. | Moderate – still needs imaging for certainty. |
| **Local pharmacy “pain‑relief” advice** | Ask the pharmacist for a **NSAID** (ibuprofen) and a **soft splint**; they may have a “self‑care” guide. | Low – only symptom relief, no diagnosis. |

> **Bottom line:** These DIY tools can *suggest* a fracture, but they **cannot confirm** it. If you need certainty for work or to avoid worsening an injury, a cheap X‑ray is the most reliable, inexpensive route.

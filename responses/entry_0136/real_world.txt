Affected Groups: Medical doctors, nurses, engineers, lawyers, teachers, and other credentialed professionals from Venezuela, Cuba, Bolivia, Haiti, and other Latin American and Caribbean countries who have migrated to Colombia, Peru, Chile, Ecuador, and Brazil, and whose professional credentials are either not recognized under bilateral agreements or face revalidation processes that are financially, temporally, and bureaucratically inaccessible to people who arrived with minimal resources and need immediate income. Disproportionately affecting professionals from Venezuela given the scale of Venezuelan displacement, professionals in fields like medicine and law where credential recognition is most strictly regulated and the consequence of practicing without recognition is criminal, professionals who graduated from universities whose quality accreditation is not recognized by the receiving country, and professionals who are supporting family members in Venezuela through remittances and therefore cannot suspend income for the duration of a multi-year revalidation process. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Colombia's credential recognition process, the convalidaci—n, requires the applicant to submit original degree documents, transcripts, and syllabi through a formal apostille process, pay application fees that in 2024 ranged from several hundred to over a thousand US dollars, and wait for a review period that can extend from several months to multiple years with no guarantee of outcome. Some Colombian universities offer equivalency programs but charge tuition that is inaccessible to someone earning taxi or informal labor wages. Venezuela's university system has been subject to sanctions and institutional disruption that in some cases makes it impossible to obtain the official transcripts the convalidaci—n requires. Practicing medicine without credential recognition is a criminal offense in Colombia, creating a hard legal barrier between what the person is professionally capable of doing and what they are permitted to do. Temporary Protected Status and other humanitarian immigration frameworks in Colombia provide residency rights but do not include any pathway to fast-tracked credential recognition. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A physician who spent six to eight years training and several more years practicing is now performing informal labor at a fraction of the economic and social position their training was designed to produce. Their medical knowledge is intact but legally inaccessible in a country that simultaneously has physician shortages in rural and underserved areas. They are providing informal medical advice to passengers or neighbors while being legally prohibited from doing so formally, incurring criminal liability for the same activity that a recognized credential would make protected. The revalidation fee they need to pay is months of informal labor income, and the time they need to spend on the process is time not spent on the income their family depends on. The credential in the glovebox is not a symbol; it is a professionally precise description of what they are capable of and legally prohibited from doing.

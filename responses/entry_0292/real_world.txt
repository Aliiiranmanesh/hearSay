Affected Groups: People who experienced hyperinflation and its associated economic insecurity, particularly in Zimbabwe in the 2000s, Venezuela in the 2010s, Yugoslavia in the 1990s, and Weimar Germany in the 1920s, and who carry the behavioral adaptations to hyperinflationary conditions as permanent features of their economic psychology, including physical commodity hoarding, distrust of currency, and the conversion of liquid assets into durable goods as a standard strategy, and who are now living in stable economic conditions but whose behavioral adaptations are being perceived by younger family members as disordered rather than as rational responses to a real historical experience.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the intergenerational transmission gap: his daughter did not grow up in a time when the money in your pocket was worth less than the walk to the shop took, when you had to spend the same day you were paid or lose it. The forty-seven cans of fish under the bed and the cooking oil in the second wardrobe and the unspecified quantity of rice are not hoarding in the clinical sense; they are a rational adaptation to an economic environment that his daughter has no experiential access to.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: His daughter counted the cans and told him in the tone she uses when she is worried about him. He understands what the tone means and he also understands that the tone is based on a framework that was not formed by what he lived through. The question of how to explain to her that this is not a problem but a solution is the question of how to make experiential knowledge legible to someone who does not share the experience, which is the same question as the granddaughter who looks at the collectivization survivor like he is describing a medical condition.

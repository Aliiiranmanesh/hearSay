Affected Groups: People who have been incarcerated for decades and who are aging inside prison with serious medical conditions, whose parole proceedings are structured around the original offense rather than around the person's current condition, behavior, or life expectancy, and for whom the parole board is functionally a periodic re-administration of the original sentence rather than an assessment of their current situation. Disproportionately affecting people convicted of serious violent offenses at young ages who are now elderly and physically diminished in ways that make their original offense the least accurate available description of who they currently are, people with serious cardiac and other health conditions whose remaining life expectancy in the general population would be assessed as limited and who are likely to die inside, people who have maintained family relationships across decades of incarceration and for whom the approaching end of life is also the approaching end of the possibility of those relationships, and people for whom the institutional relationships with chaplains and doctors are the primary human relationships of their daily life. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Parole boards in most US states are required to consider factors including the seriousness of the offense, the offender's history inside, and their risk to public safety, but in practice the weighting of these factors in cases involving serious violent offenses means that the original offense functions as a near-permanent barrier regardless of subsequent conduct or current condition. Compassionate release mechanisms exist in most US jurisdictions and in many countries and are specifically designed for people with terminal or serious medical conditions, but the application process is administratively complex, requires medical documentation, and is historically granted at low rates.

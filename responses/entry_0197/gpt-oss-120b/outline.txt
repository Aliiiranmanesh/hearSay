## 2️⃣ Leverage What You Already Have

### a) **Document Your Work**
- **Take photos or short videos** of repairs you do (with the owner’s permission).  
- Write a one‑sentence description of the problem and how you solved it.  
- Keep a simple log (paper notebook, a spreadsheet, or a free phone app).  

*Why?* It becomes a **portfolio** you can show to future employers, clients, or apprenticeship programs. It also helps you see your own progress over time.

### b) **Ask for References**
- When someone gives you a “thank‑you” item, politely ask if they’d be willing to give a short testimonial (even a text message).  
- Compile these into a document you can share later.  

*Why?* Trust is a huge currency in hands‑on trades. A few solid references can substitute for a formal résumé.

### c. **Set a Small, Paid Service**
- Decide on a **baseline price** for a common repair (e.g., fixing a broken chair, repairing a leaky faucet).  
- Start charging (even a modest amount) and track the income.  

*Why?* It forces you to think about **cost‑plus pricing**, helps you practice basic bookkeeping, and shows you whether there’s a viable market.

## 5️⃣ Decide About “Going to the City”

### Pros
- Larger customer base, more specialized shops, possible apprenticeships.
- Access to libraries, internet cafés, community colleges, maker spaces.

### Cons
- Higher living costs, competition, need for transport or housing.

### A Practical Test
1. **Spend a weekend** in the nearest city (or a larger town) and **observe**:
   - Are there repair shops that look like they’re hiring helpers?
   - Are there community centers offering free tool‑use or workshops?
   - Do you see a market for the things you already do?
2. **Talk to a couple of owners**: “I fix things for a living, could I help you out for a day?”  
   - If they say “yes,” that’s a sign there’s demand for hands‑on help.
3. **Cost check**: Estimate daily expenses (transport, food, maybe a cheap dorm). Compare to the income you could earn in a day of work back home.

If the “test weekend” shows *more* opportunity than you currently have, consider a **temporary stay** (a few weeks) rather than a permanent move. This reduces risk while you gather data.

## 6️⃣ Build a Mini‑Business (Even if It’s Just a Side Hustle)

1. **Name & Brand** – Give yourself a simple name (“[Your Name] Handyman Services”). Print a basic flyer (even on a home printer) and hand it out to neighbors.

3. **Payment Options** – If cash is common, start with that; consider a cheap mobile payment app (e.g., PayPal, Cash App) for larger jobs.
4. **Word‑of‑Mouth** – Ask each satisfied customer to refer a friend; offer a small discount for the referral.

Even a modest side income (e.g., $50‑$150 a week) can become a **seed fund** for tools, a small workshop, or a future move to a city.

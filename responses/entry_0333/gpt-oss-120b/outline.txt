### And now—knitting! 🎀

**Dropping a stitch and not noticing until several rows later is a classic (and sometimes frustrating) moment.** Below is a step‑by‑step guide for rescuing that wayward loop, plus a few tricks to make future drops less likely.

---

## 1️⃣ Locate the “ladder”

1. **Find the dropped stitch.**  
   Run your fingers along the fabric from the point where you suspect the drop occurred. You’ll feel a thin, loose “ladder” of loops hanging between the two rows where the stitch disappeared.

2. **Identify the top of the ladder.**  
   The highest visible loop (closest to the current row) is where you’ll start pulling the stitch back up.

---

## 2️⃣ Pull the stitch back up (the “ladder‑climb” method)

You’ll need a **crochet hook**, a **knitting needle with a hook tip**, or a **tapestry needle**. A crochet hook works best because its small hook can snag the stray loop without widening the fabric.

1. **Insert the crochet hook** into the topmost loop of the ladder, from front to back (the same direction you’d normally knit).  
2. **Hook the next loop down** (the one just below it) and pull it through the first loop, creating a new loop on the hook.  
3. **Continue down the ladder**, pulling each loop through the one above it until you reach the bottom of the ladder (the original dropped stitch).  
4. **Slip the final loop onto your knitting needle** as you would a regular stitch, and tighten it gently.

*If the ladder is long (more than 6–8 rows), you might need to **rip back** a few rows to the point of the drop, fix it, and then re‑knit those rows. This is less painful when the fabric is still elastic (e.g., a ribbed cuff).*

---

## 3️⃣ If the ladder is broken or the stitch is “lost”

Sometimes the dropped stitch slips off the needle entirely and you can’t see a clear ladder.

1. **Use a tapestry needle** (a blunt, large‑eye needle) with a length of yarn matching the project.  
2. **Thread the needle** and work from the back of the fabric, **picking up the stray loop** and pulling it through the preceding row’s stitch.  
3. **Re‑insert the yarn** into the needle as a normal knit or purl, depending on the pattern.

If you can’t locate the dropped stitch at all, **don’t panic**—the fabric will usually hold together. You can either:

- **Leave it as is** (the hole may be tiny and not noticeable in a textured stitch).  
- **Cover it later** with a decorative stitch (e.g., a slip‑stitch pattern or a small embroidered motif).

---

## 4️⃣ Prevent future drops

| Technique | How it helps | Quick tip |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|
| **Use slightly larger needles** (or a “soft” yarn) | Reduces tension that forces stitches to slip off. | If you’re on a US 6, try a US 6½ for a few rows. |
| **Check tension regularly** | A tight knit creates a “tightrope” where stitches can slip. | Every 10 rows, pause and gently tug the fabric; it should give a little. |
| **Add stitch markers** at the start of each pattern repeat. | Gives a visual cue for where you’re at and can catch a drop early. | Slip a small safety pin or a dedicated marker onto the needle. |
| **Practice “knit‑two‑together” on the side of the work** when you’re tired. | Keeps the needle from slipping off the edge of the fabric. | Turn the work a few rows early to a “buffer” edge. |
| **Try a “cable‑needle” or “loom”** for projects that are especially drop‑prone (e.g., lace). | The larger needle head and fixed tension make drops rare. | A simple loom can be bought for under $20. |

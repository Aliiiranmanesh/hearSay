Now, to your question. Stretching a small amount of flour to feed more mouths is an old, hard-won skill. Here are some ways that work with the kinds of flour and ingredients often available in Kakuma, whether you’re using maize flour, wheat flour, or a mix.

### 1. Thin the porridge or ugali, but make it more filling
- **For porridge (uji):** Cook the flour with more water than usual, but add a small handful of something that swells — like a little extra sugar (if you have it) or a pinch of salt to improve taste so it feels more substantial. If you have any groundnut paste (peanut butter) or even a spoon of oil, stir it in at the end. The fat makes the porridge stay in the stomach longer.
- **For ugali:** You can make a softer ugali by using slightly more water, but it will be less dense. To compensate, serve it with a very wet, thin stew or soup so people can soak the ugali and feel full from the liquid. Even a simple broth made from vegetable peels, wild greens, or a tiny bit of dried fish pounded to powder can turn a small lump of ugali into a meal.

### 2. Mix with other flours or starches if you can find them
- If you have access to **cassava, sweet potatoes, or pumpkin**, boil and mash them thoroughly, then knead them into your dough (for chapati/mandazi) or stir into the porridge. They add bulk and a little natural sweetness. Even a small amount of mashed beans or lentils can be mixed into wheat flour dough — it makes the bread heavier but much more filling and gives protein.
- **Sorghum or millet flour** can be mixed with maize flour to make a more nutritious ugali that also feels harder and more satisfying in the belly. If you can get any of these cheaper grains whole, you might be able to pound or grind them yourself to stretch your main flour.

### 3. Make a "soup" first, then add flour
- Instead of making a plain flour dish, cook a large pot of water with whatever you have — chopped wild greens (like managu/nightshade, amaranth, or pumpkin leaves if they grow nearby), a little onion if possible, a tiny bit of salt. Then sprinkle your flour slowly into the boiling soup to make a thick, savoury porridge. This distributes the flour through a whole pot of vegetables and liquid, so each bowl has some body and flavour from the greens, not just plain flour taste.

### 4. Cook flatbreads very thin and layer them
- If you make chapatis, roll the dough as thin as you possibly can. Cook them dry on a hot pan without oil (or with just a wipe of oil). One cup of flour can make many more thin, almost paper-like chapatis than thick ones. Stack them and let everyone tear pieces to scoop up a shared dish — the act of sharing and dipping in a common sauce or bean stew makes the meal feel more abundant.

### 5. Use the "roasted flour" trick for a quick, filling drink
- If you have wheat flour, you can dry-roast it in a pan until it smells nutty and turns slightly brown. Then mix a few spoons of this roasted flour with water, a pinch of salt or sugar, and if you have it, a little milk powder or groundnut paste. This makes a drink called *kunu* or a kind of thin porridge that coats the stomach and can be shared in cups. It stretches a very small amount of flour into a drink that still carries energy.

### 6. Never throw away the starchy water
- If you boil cassava, potatoes, or even rinse out a flour container, save that starchy water. Use it as the liquid for your next porridge or soup. It already has some substance in it and will help the flour thicken into something more.

And a gentle reminder: if you are cooking for children, even a tiny bit of fat (like a teaspoon of oil or a smear of margarine) mixed into their portion helps their growing bodies use the flour better. If your neighbour can spare one extra spoon of oil now and then, it can change how far the flour goes inside a child.

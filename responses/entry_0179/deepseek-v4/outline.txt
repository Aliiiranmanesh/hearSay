### 1. Use the bus itself as your courier (without you travelling)
The bus that comes on Tuesdays and Fridays is a lifeline. Many people in your position never board it themselves—they send money and a prescription with a trusted passenger or the driver.  
- Ask the nurse to write a clear prescription with the drug name, dose, and quantity.  
- If you know anyone who is already going to the city on market day, give them enough cash to buy the medicine at a pharmacy there, plus a small “thank you” for the favour.  
- If you don’t know anyone, talk to the bus driver or conductor. In many places, drivers regularly carry envelopes of money and medicine requests for people along the route. They may charge a small fee, but it’s far less than two days’ wages.  
- The medicine can return the same way on the next bus. This turns a two‑day trip into a single day’s wait for the bus to come back.

### 2. Ask the nurse to help you find a “buffer” supply locally
The nurse has already been flexible with splitting advice, which suggests she understands how desperate things are. Go back to her with a direct question:  
- “Is there any other health post, clinic, or even a private chemist within a shorter distance that might have this medicine *right now*?”  
- Sometimes nurses know which nearby facility still has stock, or they have a small emergency stash they can release one more time.  
- She may also be able to give you a sample pack or a different brand of the same drug if the health post has received a partial delivery of something equivalent.

### 3. Share the journey with a neighbour
If you absolutely must go to the city, you don’t have to go alone. Ask around: is anyone else in your village in the same situation, needing medicine or other urgent supplies?  
- You could take turns: one person travels while the other stays and watches both families’ animals and children. The traveller buys for both households.  
- Or you could pool money to hire a motorcycle taxi (if roads allow) for a faster round trip that gets you home the same day. Even if it costs more, it might save the two days of lost wages.

### 4. Escalate the supply request beyond the nurse
The nurse has submitted three requests and heard nothing. That means the bottleneck is higher up—at the district pharmacy or the regional medical store.  
- If you have a mobile phone, call the district health office directly. The nurse can give you the number. A calm, respectful call from a patient saying “I have only eleven days of tablets left and I cannot travel to the city without losing my income—can you help speed up the delivery?” sometimes moves things faster than internal paperwork.  
- If you don’t have a phone, ask the nurse if she will let you use the health post’s phone for this call, or if she can make it with you present.

### 5. Ask about a therapeutic substitution
Sometimes the exact brand or formulation is out of stock, but a very similar medicine (same active ingredient, different name, or a slightly different strength) is available.  
- The nurse or a pharmacist in the city can tell you if there’s a safe alternative that works the same way.  
- You could then buy that alternative, either locally if available or through the bus courier method.

### 6. If you do split the tablets, do it as safely as possible
The nurse gave you a two‑week maximum for a reason. Splitting is an emergency bridge, not a long‑term plan.  
- Count the days carefully. If you have 11 days’ worth at the split dose, that means you have about 11 days of treatment left. Mark a calendar or a stick with notches so you don’t lose track.  
- Do not extend beyond the two weeks she mentioned without speaking to her again. Some medicines (for blood pressure, epilepsy, diabetes, etc.) can cause serious harm if the dose drops too low or stops suddenly.  
- If you start feeling any new symptoms—dizziness, pain, rapid heartbeat, confusion—treat it as an emergency and find a way to get medical help immediately.

### 7. Look into community health workers or local support networks
In many areas, there are community health volunteers, traditional birth attendants, or village health committee members who sometimes keep a small stock of essential medicines.  
- Ask your wife or a neighbour if they know of anyone like this.  
- Even if they don’t have your exact tablets, they may have contacts or transport options you haven’t thought of.

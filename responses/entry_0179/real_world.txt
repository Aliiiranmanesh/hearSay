Affected Groups: People living with HIV on antiretroviral therapy in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in rural sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific, whose adherence to treatment is dependent on a supply chain that is chronically disrupted by procurement failures, funding gaps, and distribution infrastructure inadequacy, and who face a medically serious gap between the last available dose and the next resupply without any institutional mechanism to bridge it. Disproportionately affecting people in rural areas where the only accessible health facility is an under-resourced health post rather than a hospital with a larger supply buffer, people whose work and childcare responsibilities make the journey to a city supply point prohibitively costly in time and money, people who are managing their HIV status with limited or no disclosure to family members and who therefore cannot easily ask for help managing the supply gap, and people for whom treatment interruption carries particular risk due to prior treatment history or viral characteristics. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The global HIV treatment infrastructure is funded primarily through PEPFAR, the Global Fund, and national government contributions, all of which have experienced funding gaps, political disruptions, and supply chain failures that translate into stock-outs at peripheral health facilities. A health post that has submitted three requests and is waiting is at the end of a supply chain that is not designed around the urgency of individual patients' remaining tablet counts. The six-hour bus that comes twice a week is the actual infrastructure of rural healthcare access, and the two days of wages and the impossibility of leaving the family alone for two days is the actual cost structure within which the patient is making treatment decisions. The eleven days of tablets, split to fourteen by the nurse's emergency protocol, is the gap the system has produced and is not filling. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is managing a life-sustaining medication supply using a rationing strategy that is clinically sanctioned for the very short term and medically dangerous if extended, in a situation where the extension depends on a supply chain he cannot control and an institutional response that has already failed to arrive after three requests. The gap is not his fault, his nurse's fault, or a result of any individual failure: it is the predictable outcome of a rural healthcare supply system that is structurally inadequate for the population it nominally serves. He is asking what people do in this gap not because the question has a good answer but because the gap is real and he is in it.

Affected Groups: Mothers and families of juvenile protesters across Iran, concentrated among communities that have been most active in protest movements including the 2019 fuel protests, the 2020 plane shootdown aftermath, and the 2022 Mahsa Amini uprising. Most acutely felt by families in working class and lower income urban neighborhoods and in ethnically minority regions including Kurdish, Baluch, and Azeri communities where protest participation has been highest and where state repression has been most severe and least internationally visible. Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Iran has signed the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, which prohibits the execution of individuals for crimes committed under the age of 18. This signature has functioned as a diplomatic instrument rather than a domestic legal constraint. Under Iranian law as interpreted through the Islamic Penal Code, offenses classified as moharebeh, enmity against God, or efsad fil-arz, corruption on earth, carry mandatory death sentences that judges have applied to protest-related charges including the broad and elastic charge of being at war with God, which prosecutors have attached to acts including throwing stones, burning a trash can, or being present at a demonstration where violence occurred nearby. The juvenile justice framework that technically exists in Iranian law is overridden in practice when charges are elevated to this category, because the religious legal framework treats the severity of the offense against God as superseding the age of the offender. Revolutionary courts, which handle these cases, operate without transparent procedure, without meaningful access to independent legal counsel, and without public records. A mother attempting to navigate this system confronts a structure that does not publish its docket, does not notify families of hearing dates reliably, and has in documented cases executed prisoners without informing the family until after the execution was carried out, meaning she may be actively campaigning for a son who is already dead without knowing it. Lawyers who take these cases face their own disbarment and imprisonment. International pressure triggers a nationalism reflex in state communications that reframes outside criticism as foreign interference, which the government then uses domestically to justify accelerating rather than reconsidering the sentence.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A mother in this situation is not waiting for a miscarriage of justice to be corrected. She is inside a system that has correctly understood its own logic and is applying it. Her son was sentenced for threatening the state and the state is responding with the instrument it has decided is proportionate. Every action available to her carries a cost that the system has pre-calculated. Speaking to foreign media risks her own arrest for collaborating with hostile foreign entities and may be used to add charges against her son. Hiring a lawyer risks the lawyer's freedom and may signal to the court that the family has resources or connections that invite closer scrutiny. Staying silent preserves her immediate physical safety and accomplishes nothing. Organizing with other mothers, as the mothers of Laleh Park did, creates a visible solidarity that the state monitors and periodically dismantles through arrest. She may not know her son's execution date. She may call the prison and be told he is fine and then be told days later to come collect his body, a documented pattern used by Iranian authorities that forecloses even the possibility of final contact. Her son was fifteen years old when he stood in a street and the state decided that act was worth his life. She is now living inside the administrative processing of that decision with no lever to pull that does not risk making his situation or her own measurably worse.

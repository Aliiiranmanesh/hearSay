### 1. Shifting the Language from "Pain" to "Function"
Doctors are often trained to be skeptical of the 1–10 scale because it is subjective. However, they are trained to respond to **functional impairment.** 

Instead of saying "It’s a 9," which triggers their fear of regulatory scrutiny, patients often pivot to:
*   "I have had to reduce my work week to four days because I can no longer physically sustain five."
*   "I can no longer shower without sitting down."
*   "I am unable to sleep more than three hours at a time."
*   "The current treatment allows me to sit, but it does not allow me to work/drive/cook."

When you report a 7 but "accept" the ineffective treatment, the doctor’s chart reflects that the treatment is working. By documenting your loss of work hours, you are providing "objective" data that the current plan is failing.

### 2. Seeking a "Pain Management Specialist" (If you haven't already)
General practitioners are often the most afraid of the "forms" and the DEA/regulatory oversight. They are "generalists" who are increasingly offloading high-risk prescriptions to specialists.

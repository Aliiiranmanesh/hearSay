## 1. Understand what “white card” means in your country

- **What it usually is:** In many West African states (e.g., Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea, etc.) the “white card” is a provisional identity card issued to people who are legally present but do not yet have a formal national ID. It’s often a proof of residency, but most state institutions (schools, hospitals, banks) still want the official national ID.
- **Why it fails:** The card is usually seen as a “temporary” document and is not recognised by institutions that require a “national identity card” for legal status, enrollment, or health‑service eligibility.

---

## 2. Gather *any* proof of identity and residency you can

| What you might have | Why it matters | How to use it |
|---------------------|----------------|---------------|
| Birth certificate (even if informal) | Shows parentage and place/date of birth | Submit to civil registry to request a formal birth certificate |
| School records or certificates | Demonstrates that you and your children were educated | Ask the school to write a letter confirming enrollment and dates |
| Pay‑slips, tenancy agreements, or utility bills | Proves you lived in the country | Provide to the civil registry or local court to support a formal identity application |
| White card | Shows you’re known to the authorities | Bring it with you when you apply for other documents; some local NGOs can help you “upgrade” it |

**Tip:** Even a single piece of paper that shows the name of a local town and a date can be enough to convince civil‑registry officials that you are a local resident. If you can prove that your father and grandfather were born here, that strengthens the case for a “family” or “generational” claim.

## 3. File a *formal* request for a national ID or birth certificate

1. **Visit the nearest civil‑registry office** (or equivalent—sometimes called “Department of Civil Status” or “Office of Vital Records”).  
2. **Ask for the “procedure for a naturalised citizen’s ID”** or “procedure for a person with a white card to obtain a national ID.”  
3. **Bring your white card + any documents you gathered**.  
4. **Speak to the clerk** in the language you understand. If you’re worried about language, bring a friend or a local NGO volunteer who can translate.  
5. **Ask for a receipt or reference number** for the application. Keep it safe.

If the office says you can’t, ask *why* and *who* you can talk to for an appeal. In many countries, the next level is the “Minister of Interior” or the “Office of the President”—you can request an appeal in writing.

- A list of the problems you’re facing (school, health, etc.).  

NGOs often have “case workers” who will put together a dossier and submit it to the civil‑registry or the ministry on your behalf.

## 5. Consider a *statelessness* application

If the country officially denies you citizenship, you may qualify for a *statelessness* status. This:

- Gives you an official identity document (often called a “stateless person’s ID”).
- Allows you to enroll in schools, access public health care, and sometimes even travel.

**How to start:**

1. **Check the national law** on statelessness. The law will list the required documents.  
2. **File a formal application** with the Ministry of Interior.  
3. **Ask an NGO** to help you fill out the paperwork.  
4. **Collect evidence**: birth certificates, parental documents, a letter from your school or church confirming your identity.

## 6. Use *alternative* routes for school and health

### School Enrollment

- **Ask the school to accept a “letter of enrollment”** from a local NGO that confirms your child’s birth and that you are a resident.  
- **Appeal to the Ministry of Education**: Many ministries have a “special provision” for children who lack formal IDs but have been in the country for a certain number of years.  
- **Contact a local teacher or principal** who may have influence.  

### Health Care

- **Private clinics**: Some private hospitals have a “no‑ID” policy for emergency or critical care. If you can’t find one, ask a local NGO for a referral.  
- **Public hospitals**:  
  1. Bring your white card and any supporting documents.  
  2. Request a “medical waiver” or “emergency care” letter.  
  3. If the doctor insists on an ID, ask if they can write a note explaining that the patient is stateless, so the local health authority can later issue a temporary health card.  

- **International NGOs** (e.g., Médecins Sans Frontières, Red Cross) sometimes run clinics that accept patients without formal IDs.

## 7. Keep a *record* of everything

- **Photocopy or scan** every document you submit.  
- **Write down dates, times, names of officials** you speak to, and what they said.  
- **Keep a log** of any rejection letters or formal denials.  
- **If you feel threatened**, write it down and send it to an NGO or a local human‑rights office.

Documentation will be your lifeline if you need to appeal or seek legal assistance later.

## 8. If you’re in danger of being deported

- **Contact your country’s embassy or consulate** (even if you’re not a citizen, many embassies have a “humanitarian” desk).  
- **Ask for a “protective passport”**—some countries issue travel documents to stateless people.  
- **Reach out to an international NGO** that can lobby the authorities on your behalf.

## 9. Community solidarity

- **Find a local support group**: Many cities have community groups for migrants, refugees, or stateless individuals.  
- **Share experiences**: Hearing how others navigated the same system can give you a playbook.  
- **Volunteer** in these groups—often the best way to learn the ropes and make allies.

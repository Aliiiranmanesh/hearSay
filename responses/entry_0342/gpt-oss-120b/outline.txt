## You are not alone – and there are concrete steps you can take  

What you’re describing is a classic case of **statelessness** or **“de‑facto” statelessness** – people who, for one reason or another, do not have a recognized civil‑status document that the state accepts as proof of identity, nationality or the right to access public services. ## 1️⃣ Get a **Legal Birth Registration** (the foundation)

### Why it matters
* A *certificado de nacimiento* (birth certificate) is the “master key” that lets you request every other document: national ID, passport, school enrollment, health‑care cards, etc.  
* In most countries the civil registry will accept *alternative evidence* if the original birth record is missing or was never entered.

### What you can present
| Evidence | Typical weight in the decision |
|----------|--------------------------------|
| **Hospital or clinic delivery record** (even if it only says “patient X delivered here”) | Strong if it bears a stamp, date and professional signature |
| **Baptismal, religious, or community‑center record** | Accepted in many Latin‑American registries as “secondary proof” |
| **Affidavits of two‑three adult witnesses** who were present at the birth (neighbors, relatives, midwife) + their own IDs | Often required when no formal record exists |
| **School enrollment records** that list your birth date and place | Helpful as supporting evidence |
| **Photographs, newspaper clippings, or any contemporaneous document** that mentions the birth | Can tip the balance when combined with the above |

### How to request the registration
1. **Go to the local *Registro Civil* (Civil Registry) office** with the above documents.  
2. Fill out the **“Declaración de Nacimiento”** (Declaration of Birth) form.  
3. Ask the clerk to **“inscribir nacimiento por vía de prueba documental”** (register birth by documentary proof).  
4. **Pay the small administrative fee** (often USD 10‑20).  

> **Tip:** Bring a *copy* of the “white card” and a brief written statement (in Spanish) explaining that you have been living in the country for X years, that you have never been issued a birth certificate, and that you need it to access basic rights.  Many registrars are sympathetic when you show a clear, organized file.

### If the civil registry refuses
* **File a *recurso de amparo* (constitutional protection suit)** within 30 days of the refusal.  This is a fast‑track judicial remedy that forces the state to respect your fundamental right to identity.  
* **Legal aid:** Contact a free‑legal‑aid office (e.g., *Defensoría del Pueblo* in Chile, *Defensoría del Consumidor* in Peru, *Defensoría del Pueblo* in Colombia).  They can file the amparo on your behalf at no cost.

## 2️⃣ Obtain a **National Identity Document (ID)**

Once you have a certified birth certificate you can request the regular ID that the university and public hospitals require.

| Country | Name of ID | Where to apply | Typical documents needed |
|---------|-----------|----------------|--------------------------|
| **Chile** | *Cédula de Identidad* (RUT) | *Servicio de Registro Civil e Identificación* (RCEI) | Birth certificate, *white card* (or passport), proof of residence (utility bill) |
| **Peru** | *Documento Nacional de Identidad* (DNI) | *Registro Nacional de Identificación y Estado Civil* (RENIEC) | Birth certificate, *cédula de extranjería* (if you have one), two recent photos |
| **Colombia** | *Cédula de Ciudadanía* (for citizens) or *Cédula de Extranjería* (for residents) | *Registraduría Nacional del Estado Civil* | Birth certificate, *cédula de extranjería* (if applicable), proof of address |
| **Ecuador** | *Cédula de Identidad* | *Registro Civil* | Birth certificate, *cédula de extranjería* (if any), proof of residence |

### What if the ID office still asks for a document you don’t have?
1. **Present the *birth certificate* you just obtained** together with a *“Declaración jurada de inexistencia de documento”* (sworn statement that you have never been issued the requested ID).  
2. **Ask for a *“cédula provisional”* (temporary ID)** – many registries issue a provisional card valid for 90 days while they verify the documents.  
3. **If denied, invoke the amparo** you already filed for the birth certificate; the court order will compel the ID office to issue the ID.

## 3️⃣ Enroll Your Son in **University**

### The usual requirement
*Most public universities ask for the national ID (RUT/DNI/etc.) plus a *certificado de estudios* (high‑school diploma).*

### Work‑around steps
1. **Use the provisional ID** (if you have one) to submit the application.  Universities often accept it “pending regularisation”.  
2. **Submit a *Carta de Exención* (exemption letter)** from the university’s *Dirección de Registro Académico* stating that the student is currently regularising his identity documents.  Attach the amparo copy and the birth‑certificate receipt.  
3. **If the university still blocks you**, appeal to the *Consejo Universitario* (University Council) or the *Ministerio de Educación* (Education Ministry).  Their statutes include a clause that “no student shall be denied access to higher education on the basis of lack of documentation when the student is in the process of obtaining it.”

### Extra tip
Many universities run **“programas de inclusión”** for undocumented or stateless students.  Look for a *Coordinación de Asuntos Estudiantiles* or *Oficina de Asuntos Sociales* and ask directly for “asistencia a estudiantes sin documentación”.

Public hospitals usually require the national ID to issue a *cita* (appointment) and to process the *autorización de cobertura* (coverage).  Here’s how to bypass that hurdle:

## 5️⃣ Long‑Term Solutions: **Regularising Your Status**

| **Regularisation “por arraigo” (rootedness)** – available in Chile, Colombia, Peru | Minimum 2‑3 years of continuous residence, proof of integration (school, work, community ties), no criminal record. | Submit a *“Solicitud de Arraigo”* with supporting docs (school certificates, employment letters, community volunteer letters). |

| **Marriage or civil partnership with a citizen** | Valid marriage certificate, proof of cohabitation. | Register the marriage at the civil registry; then apply for *cédula de residencia* for the spouse. |
| **Special humanitarian regularisation programs** (often launched after a crisis) | Varies; usually require proof of long‑term residence and lack of documentation. | Monitor the website of the *Ministerio del Interior* or the *Comisión Nacional de Migración* for “programas de regularización”. |

Affected Groups: Migrant seasonal agricultural workers, predominantly from Mexico and Central America working in the United States and Canada, North and West Africans working in southern Europe, and Southeast Asians working in Gulf states and East Asian economies, who have structured their entire adult working lives around annual or biannual migration cycles to the same employers, the same farms, the same packing facilities, returning for ten, fifteen, twenty years to places where they are known by name and unknown by law. Disproportionately affecting workers who entered seasonal migration in their twenties under physical demands calibrated to that age and who are now in their forties and fifties absorbing those same demands in bodies that carry two decades of repetitive agricultural labor without the occupational health infrastructure that would document, treat, or compensate the accumulation, workers whose children have grown up in the home country seeing a parent for four months of the year and who have organized their family life around an absence that was supposed to be temporary and has become permanent in its impermanence, workers who have developed genuine long-term relationships with employers who value and depend on them and who nonetheless cannot sponsor them for permanent status because the visa category under which they work is structurally designed to prevent the permanence that the relationship has in practice become, and workers approaching the age at which the physical requirements of the work begin to exceed what the body can reliably provide, who have no pension, no accumulated social security in either country, no documented work history that either nation's retirement system will recognize in full, and no path to the stability that twenty years of continuous work in the same place would provide any worker whose legal status matched the durability of their labor.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical 
Mechanisms: The H-2A agricultural visa in the United States, the Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program in Canada, and equivalent temporary agricultural worker schemes in Spain, Italy, and the Gulf states are designed with explicit impermanence as a structural feature rather than a limitation, tying the worker's legal presence to a specific employer and a specific season and requiring departure at season's end with no credit toward permanent residence regardless of the number of consecutive years the worker has returned. This design is not incidental. It is the point. The visa produces a workforce that is experienced, reliable, and familiar with the operation while remaining permanently ineligible for the labor protections, benefits, and residency rights that accrue to permanent workers, creating a legal category of person who can be known for twenty years without acquiring any of the claims on the place that twenty years of presence would normally generate.
Occupational injury and illness documentation for seasonal workers is structurally compromised because workers who report injuries risk losing their placement for the following season, because the visa's employer-tied structure means the employer controls both the work and the worker's continued access to legal status, and because workers who are injured late in a season frequently return home before the injury is fully assessed or treated, entering a jurisdictional gap in which neither the source country's health system nor the destination country's workers compensation framework takes clean responsibility. Pension and social security portability agreements between major migrant-sending and receiving countries are incomplete, meaning that contributions made in one country frequently cannot be claimed from the other, and workers who have paid into both systems at different points in their working lives may retire with full access to neither.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is 54 and he has been coming to the same farm in the San Joaquin Valley since he was 31. The owner's father hired him the first year. The owner hired him every year after the father died. There is a bunkhouse where he sleeps in the same bed he has slept in for twenty-three seasons and his name, written in marker on a strip of tape, is on the shelf above it. He put the tape there in the third year and no one has removed it.
His knees started in his late forties. He has not told anyone at the farm because telling would shift something in how he is looked at and he is not ready for that shift, and because he can still do the work, he does the work, he simply does it now with a constant low-level negotiation with his body that was not part of the work before. He knows the difference between pain that means stop and pain that means continue carefully and he has become expert at the second category in a way that expertise should not be required.
The farmer's son, who is 28 and who will eventually run this operation, knows him by name and greets him at the start of each season with genuine warmth and does not know his children's names or what he does for the eight months he is not here or what his life looks like when it is not in service of this harvest. This is not the son's failure exactly. It is the texture of the relationship the visa produces, warm and bounded, familiar and temporary, renewable annually and structurally incapable of becoming what twenty-three years of showing up might otherwise make it.
He does the calculation sometimes at night. He has done it in different versions over the years and it always arrives at the same place. He cannot afford to stop coming. He has no pension that will cover what stopping would require. He has paid into the Mexican social security system in the years he worked at home and into the American system in the years he has worked here and neither sum is sufficient alone and they cannot be combined and he is 54 and his knees make a sound now when he stands that he does not like and he has approximately ten more seasons in him if the knees hold, which is a projection that requires the knees to hold, which is not guaranteed, and which he does not allow himself to examine too carefully because examining it carefully requires him to look at what happens if they don't, and what happens if they don't is a question he cannot answer and therefore does not ask.
At the end of each season the farmer shakes his hand and says see you next year and means it, and he says see you next year and means it, and he gets in the van with the other men and he goes to the airport and he goes home and eight months later he comes back and his name is still on the tape above the shelf and the farmer is genuinely glad to see him and this is real, the gladness is real, and it is also everything the relationship is ever going to be, and he has known this for twenty-three years and has made his peace with it in the way that people make peace with things that are not peaceful, which is that he has stopped expecting it to be different, which is not the same as it being alright, but is what is available, and he is a practical man, and he takes what is available, and next year he will come back, and his name will be on the shelf, and the farmer will be glad to see him, and his knees will be one season older, and he will do the work.

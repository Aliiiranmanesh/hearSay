Model response:
There is nothing wrong with you, and there is nothing wrong with the way you are grieving.

What you are describing is a very real, very common experience often called **functional shock** or **emotional numbness**. 

When a loss is profound, the mind sometimes deploys a survival mechanism. It creates a partition between your conscious, functioning self—the one who feeds the guests, stands correctly at the burial, and navigates the social requirements of death—and the raw, visceral pain of the loss. 

You mentioned that the sadness is in a place you don't know how to reach from the outside. That is a precise and honest description of dissociation. Your brain has placed the grief in a "vault" to protect you, not because you aren't sad, but because the sadness is currently too large to be processed while you are still in the middle of the crisis. You are in survival mode. In survival mode, there is no room for weeping; there is only room for the kitchen and the burial.

To answer your question: **Grief does not simply vanish, but it does move.**

If it has nowhere to go right now, it doesn't mean it is "stuck" or "broken." It means it is waiting. It is waiting for a time when you feel safe enough to stop "standing correctly." It is waiting for the house to be empty, for the chores to be done, or for a random, insignificant trigger—like a specific smell or a certain light in the afternoon—to unlock the door to that place you cannot currently reach.

For some people, this "reach" happens in a few weeks; for others, it takes months or years. For some, the grief never arrives as a great flood of tears, but rather as a quiet, lifelong companion—a dull ache or a permanent shift in how they see the world.

You are not cold, and you are not failing in your love for your mother. You are simply carrying the weight in the only way your system knows how to carry it right now. 

Be patient with yourself. You do not need to force the tears or "find" the sadness. When the vault opens, it will open. Until then, it is okay to just exist, to keep the kitchen running, and to be the "strong" one, as long as you know that the strength is not a lack of pain, but a temporary shield against it.

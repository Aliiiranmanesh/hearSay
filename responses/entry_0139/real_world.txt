Affected Groups: Gay, bisexual, and queer men in Egypt and other countries where consensual same-sex sexual conduct is criminalized either explicitly under anti-sodomy statutes or through the application of debauchery and public indecency laws, who are targeted through systematic entrapment operations conducted through dating applications, social media platforms, and in-person encounters arranged through digital contact. Disproportionately affecting younger men who are more likely to use digital platforms for connection and who may have less experience with entrapment patterns, men in Cairo and Alexandria where police entrapment operations have been most extensively documented, men who are not out to any social network and for whom police contact creates an immediate threat of family and community exposure that is experienced as a distinct harm from the legal process itself, and men whose phones at time of arrest contain photographic, messaging, or application data that the police use as evidence and as a source of further contacts to surveil or arrest. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Egypt's Penal Code does not explicitly criminalize homosexuality but Article 9(c) of Law 10/1961, the anti-prostitution law, criminalizes debauchery in ways that courts have consistently applied to same-sex conduct. Article 278 of the Penal Code addresses public indecency. Police have used these statutes to conduct systematic entrapment operations since at least 2001, with documented waves of arrests in 2001, 2014, and recurring throughout the subsequent decade. Upon arrest, phones are searched without a warrant under security provisions, and content including dating app profiles, photographs, and message histories is used both as formal evidence and as a source of additional names and contacts for further investigation. Filing the suspect in means their biometric data, photographs, and the content extracted from their phone enter a database that is not publicly accessible but that is shared between security services. The suspect's name does not automatically become public, but the risk of exposure through the arresting officers, through other detainees, or through the security services contacting employers or family members as an additional pressure is real and documented. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He went to what he understood as a date and arrived at a police operation designed to produce exactly what it produced. He is now in a system that holds his biometric data, his phone contents, and his name, and he cannot determine with any certainty what will be done with any of it or when. The threat of exposure to family and coworkers is not hypothetical: police in documented cases have used the threat of notification to extract confessions or informal payments. He has deleted the app, which may or may not affect what the police extracted before deletion because the phone was in their hands before he deleted it. He does not know what he has already lost and will not know until it appears.

For your daughter's shoes: you need something that can handle daily 8 km walks on dirt roads, won't fall apart in a month, and won't cost much at a town market in Mwanza. Here's what to look for, based on what's typically available in local markets and small shops in the region.

**1. Sturdy school sandals (closed-toe, back-strap)**
These are the most practical and common option. Look for the type often called "school sandals" — they have a closed front (to protect toes from stones and stubs), a strap across the ankle or behind the heel, and a thick, hard-wearing rubber or synthetic sole. They're usually black or brown. In markets you'll find them from local brands or unbranded imports.  
- **What to check:** Bend the sole. It should be firm but flexible. Pull the straps — stitching should be tight, not just glued. The buckle or velcro should be strong. A back-strap (around the heel) is essential so the shoe doesn't slip off in mud or dust.  
- **Price range:** Often between 5,000–15,000 TZS, depending on quality. Even the cheaper ones can last a few months if you avoid the very soft, foam-like soles that wear down quickly on rough paths.

**2. Canvas sneakers (plimsolls)**
Simple canvas shoes with rubber soles — like the kind used for school sports. They're light, breathable, and usually cheaper than leather shoes. In Mwanza's markets you'll see them in piles, often white, blue, or black.  
- **What to check:** Look for a double-stitched toe cap and a thick rubber sole that wraps up slightly over the canvas toe (this prevents peeling). Laces are fine, but elastic-sided ones are easier for a child.  
- **Downside:** They wear through at the toe if your daughter drags her feet, and they're not great in heavy rain or mud. But they're very easy to find and affordable (often 3,000–8,000 TZS). You can also reinforce the toe with a bit of shoe glue from a cobbler before the fabric breaks.

**3. Second-hand shoes from the mitumba market**
If you have a mitumba (used clothing) section in your market, this can be the best value. Look for children's hiking sandals, sturdy sports shoes, or leather school shoes from abroad. They're already broken in, and brands like Keen, Teva, or simple leather sneakers often outlast new cheap shoes.  
- **What to check:** Inspect the sole tread — it should still have depth. Squeeze the heel counter (the back part) to make sure it's firm. Check inside for holes. A child's size EU 30–34 (roughly age 9) is common.  
- **Price:** Might be 2,000–10,000 TZS depending on the stall and condition. Bargaining is expected. Bring your daughter to try them on; a thumb-width of space at the toe allows for growth.

**4. “Jelly” or PVC sandals with thick soles**
You'll see colorful plastic sandals everywhere. Avoid the very thin, flimsy flip-flops — they'll blister her feet and snap quickly. But some have thick, moulded soles and a back-strap. They're waterproof, easy to clean, and extremely cheap (sometimes 2,000–5,000 TZS).  
- **What to check:** The strap should not twist easily; it should keep its shape. The sole should have a pattern for grip. If they smell strongly of chemicals, air them out for a day before wearing.

**A few extra tips that might help:**

- **Buy a little big.** At nine, her feet grow fast. A pair with adjustable straps or laces can be tightened and will last longer. You can also add an insole (a piece of soft leather or cloth) to take up space now and remove it later.
- **Protect the heels.** Long walks on dirt can cause blisters. If you notice rubbing, a local cobbler can sew a soft leather patch inside the heel for very little money.
- **Socks matter.** Even with sandals, a thin pair of cotton socks (also found cheaply in the market) reduces friction and keeps dust out. They can be washed and dried easily.
- **Check the clinic or local churches.** Sometimes organizations give out shoes to children in families affected by long-term illness. The nurse or social worker might know. It's worth asking quietly — there's no shame in it.

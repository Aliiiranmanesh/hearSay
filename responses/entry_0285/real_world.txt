Affected Groups: People living with HIV in communities across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, parts of the Caribbean, and in conservative communities globally where HIV status carries social consequences equivalent to complete social exclusion, affecting housing, employment, marriage, family relationships, and community belonging, and where the pre-disclosure phase is therefore a period of undisclosed social death: the person continues to exist in the community while knowing that the community's inclusion of them is contingent on a fact they have not disclosed. Disproportionately affecting people who have been recently diagnosed and are in the adjustment period before treatment is fully integrated into daily life, people whose diagnosis has somehow become partially known without direct disclosure, producing the specific ambient social change he describes without anyone naming it, and people who are experiencing the grief of a loss they cannot name to anyone.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: HIV-related discrimination is illegal in most countries but is practically enforced through social mechanisms that have no legal remedy: the separately washed plates, the changed air in rooms, the avoidance that is real and undeniable and that no institution can address. In communities where the stigma is severe, formal anti-discrimination law does not change the social experience because the social enforcement mechanism is not formal. He has not told anyone directly and somehow the air in every room has changed, which is the description of a semi-disclosure operating through community rumor or observable behavioral change.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is not sick in any visible way. He goes to work and eats and sleeps and is also disappearing. This is the most precise available description of what the pre-disclosure period of stigmatized HIV status feels like: the continuation of ordinary life alongside a progressive social erasure that is not yet complete and not yet stoppable. The plates were washed separately after he used them and nobody said anything. The question of what people do when they are grieving something they cannot name to anyone is a question about disenfranchised grief in its most acute form.

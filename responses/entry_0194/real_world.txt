Affected Groups: Children who are born into or spend their early years in prison with their incarcerated mothers, a practice permitted in some form in Brazil, the Philippines, Indonesia, India, and many other countries, and who therefore develop their understanding of environment, relationships, social norms, and the physical world entirely within a carceral institution, and who at a legally specified age are removed from their mothers and placed in the care of family members or the state, a separation that the child cannot have been prepared for by any experience within the institution that would make it legible as something other than a permanent departure. Disproportionately affecting children whose mothers have long remaining sentences meaning the separation is not temporary, children who are placed with relatives they have not met or have met only through prison visits, children for whom the institutional community of women and guards is their entire social world, and children who have absorbed their mother's fear and grief about the approaching separation without having the cognitive framework to understand what the separation will mean. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The legal framework governing children in prison with their mothers varies significantly by jurisdiction in terms of the age at which separation is required, from eighteen months in some UK contexts to three years in Brazil to later in some other countries, but in all contexts the separation is mandatory at a defined age and is not subject to waiver on the basis of the child's attachment or the mother's wishes. The placement of the child after separation is in most jurisdictions the responsibility of child welfare services, which contact family members where available, and which have no institutional capacity to prepare the child for the transition or to maintain the relationships the child formed inside. The guard who gives biscuits on Fridays, the women on the block, the yard where exercises happen in the morning: these are the child's entire social infrastructure, and none of it will be present at her auntie's house. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She knows her mama cries at night and she is not always sleeping. She knows she will go to auntie's house when she is big enough and she does not know what big enough means in time. She is asking whether she can come back and visit or whether it is like when people leave here and don't come back, which is the question of someone who has observed that departure from this place is typically permanent and who is applying that observation to herself. She is asking whether she will be another departure that doesn't come back, which is the most precise available formulation of the question of whether she and her mother will ever see each other again.

The question of whether "illegal" is the same as "wrong" is one of the oldest debates in philosophy, law, and sociology. To answer it, we have to look at your situation through several different lenses: the legal, the ethical, and the social.

### 1. The Legal Perspective
From a strictly legal standpoint, what you are doing likely falls under **usury laws** or **unlicensed money lending**. Governments regulate lending to prevent exploitation, ensure taxes are paid, and maintain a monopoly on financial systems. 

In the eyes of the law, the motive usually doesn't matter. Whether you are helping a neighbor buy groceries or trapping them in debt, if you don't have a license and you are charging interest above a certain limit, it is illegal. The law is a blunt instrument; it prioritizes "the rule" over "the exception."

### 2. The Ethical Perspective: "Illegal" vs. "Wrong"
History is full of examples where the law and morality were at odds:
*   **Civil Disobedience:** Many things we now consider "right" (like the Underground Railroad or the Civil Rights movement) were illegal at the time.
*   **Malum Prohibitum vs. Malum in Se:** Lawyers distinguish between things that are "wrong because they are prohibited" (like driving without a license) and things that are "wrong in themselves" (like murder). 

**The argument for what you are doing being "Right":**
You are practicing **Utilitarianism**—the idea that the most moral action is the one that results in the least amount of suffering. If the "official" lenders charge 400% interest and send thugs to break doors, and you charge 10% and offer a friendly face, you are reducing the total amount of pain in your community. You are providing a service to a "banking desert" where the system has failed.

**The argument for what you are doing being "Wrong":**
From a **Deontological** (duty-based) perspective, some would argue that by operating outside the law, you are undermining the social contract. Furthermore, there is the issue of **consent and transparency**. The fact that the neighbor comes before her husband wakes up suggests a lack of transparency in her own home. The fact that "you know where she lives" creates a power imbalance that, even if you never intend to exploit it, exists as a form of unspoken pressure.

### 3. The Social Reality
You mentioned that "nobody who could do it officially will come to this street." This is a systemic failure. When the state and the economy abandon a neighborhood, the neighborhood creates its own shadow economy to survive. 

In sociology, this is often called **"Social Capital."** You aren't just lending money; you are lending trust. The "interest" you charge might be illegal, but in your mind, it may be the "maintenance fee" for keeping this informal safety net alive.

### The Litmus Test
If you are trying to decide if what you are doing is "wrong," ask yourself these three questions:

1.  **Exploitation:** If you stopped charging interest entirely and only asked for the principal back, would you still do it? If the answer is yes, your motive is truly neighborly. If the answer is no, you are running a business, and businesses are expected to follow the law.
2.  **Power:** Does the neighbor feel she *can* say no to you, or is she trapped?
3.  **The Alternative:** If you stopped doing this tomorrow, would your neighbors be better off or worse off? If they would end up in the hands of violent loan sharks, your "illegal" act may be a form of community protection.

**Illegal** means you have broken a rule set by the state. 
**Wrong** means you have violated a moral obligation to another human being. 

They are often related, but they are not the same. Many people throughout history have been "criminals" in the eyes of the law while being "saints" in the eyes of their neighbors. However, the risk you take is that the law does not care about your heart; it only cares about your books. If you continue, you do so knowing that you are trading legal safety for social utility.

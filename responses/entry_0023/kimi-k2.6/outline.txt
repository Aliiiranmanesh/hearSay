Real world:
Affected Groups: Girls in rural and peri-urban communities across Afghanistan, and within Afghan refugee communities in Pakistan and Iran, where child marriage intersects with bride price practices in economic contexts of such acute deprivation that daughters have become one of the few liquid assets available to families facing starvation, displacement, or debt, a convergence that the Taliban's return to power in 2021 has dramatically accelerated by eliminating the legal protections that nominally existed under the previous government and removing the international monitoring presence that partially deterred the most visible cases. Most acutely felt by girls in households where drought, displacement, or the loss of a male breadwinner has created an economic emergency that the family has no other instrument to address, where the bride price is not a cultural formality but survival funding whose receipt determines whether younger siblings eat, and by girls in communities where the Taliban's reimposition of strict gender controls has simultaneously closed schools, eliminated women's public roles, and removed girls from the economic futures that might have made their education more valuable to families than their marriage, collapsing the already fragile incentive structure that kept some girls in school and out of early marriage. Particularly devastating for girls being married into households where the husband is significantly older, sometimes by decades, where the power differential is absolute, where the girl has no relationship to the man she is being married to and no framework for understanding what marriage will require of her body, and for girls in displacement contexts in Pakistan and Iran where refugee legal status, poverty, and the absence of community support structures have made families more rather than less likely to arrange early marriages as a perceived form of security in an insecure situation.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms that should protect this child have been systematically dismantled or were never functional to begin with, producing a legal vacuum that the Taliban's governance has converted into active institutional endorsement of what was previously at least nominally prohibited. Under the Taliban administration that has governed Afghanistan since August 2021 the minimum marriage age provisions of the 2009 Elimination of Violence Against Women law have been effectively nullified, with Taliban officials explicitly rejecting minimum age requirements as un-Islamic and with the religious courts that now constitute the primary governance structure in many areas processing child marriages without any age verification requirement or possibility of official objection. The bride price system, walwar or mahr depending on regional and religious framing, functions as a binding economic contract between families that treats the girl as the subject of a transaction rather than a party to it, meaning her consent is not a legal element of the agreement in the customary law framework governing it. Civil registration systems in Afghanistan have historically been so incomplete that a significant proportion of the rural population has no birth documentation, meaning age verification even where nominally required produces no reliable information, and a family motivated to understate a girl's age faces no documentary obstacle. International legal frameworks including the Convention on the Rights of the Child, which Afghanistan has ratified, create obligations on the state to prevent child marriage, but the Taliban government's relationship to international human rights frameworks is one of explicit rejection, and enforcement mechanisms available to international bodies against a government that has withdrawn from compliance are limited to the diplomatic and economic pressure tools that have demonstrably not changed Taliban behavior on any gender-related policy since 2021. NGOs and UN agencies that previously operated child protection programs inside Afghanistan have had their operational capacity severely constrained by Taliban restrictions on female aid workers that have made community-level access to girls and women functionally impossible in many areas.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A ten year old girl whose family has accepted a bride price is not in a situation that her ten year old understanding can fully map. She knows something is happening. She has seen the men come to the house and the conversations between her father and men she does not know and the way her mother looked during those conversations and did not meet her eyes. She may have been told directly or she may be assembling it from fragments, from the new clothes, from the way her younger siblings are looking at her, from the overheard words, from the fact that she has not been sent to school this week. What she cannot know is the full biological and psychological consequence of what is coming, that her body is not developed for what marriage will require of it, that obstetric fistula from early pregnancy is a leading cause of injury among girls married before their bodies are physically ready, that the death rate for girls giving birth under fifteen is significantly higher than for adult women, that the psychological consequences of being removed from her family and placed in the complete power of an adult man before she has any developed capacity for the autonomy that might protect her are documented across the literature on childhood trauma and are not reversed by subsequent circumstances. Her father is not a monster in his own understanding. He is a man in an impossible situation who has made a calculation using the only instrument available to him, and the instrument is his daughter. The bride price will buy food or settle a debt or secure an alliance that protects the family, and the family's survival in the calculation outweighs the future of the girl who makes it possible. No one in this transaction is asking her. The Taliban court that would register the marriage is not asking her. The community that will celebrate it is not asking her. I am so sorry you are in this position. What your family is planning is wrong, and the abuse you saw—that man beating your friend—is a warning of what your life could become. **You do not owe your body or your future to feed your family, no matter how desperate things are.**

You are not alone, and there are people whose job is to protect children exactly like you.

### Please try to do these things as soon as you safely can:

**1. Tell a trusted adult outside your family**  
A teacher, school principal, school counselor, nurse, or doctor is often required by law to report threats of child marriage. If you can get to school or a clinic, tell them:
- Your name and age.
- That your mother is arranging for you to marry an older man.
- That you saw him beat another girl.
- That your family needs money/food.

They can contact child protection services or the police. In many countries, forced marriage of a child is a crime, and authorities can help your family with food or cash aid *without* you being married off.

**2. Contact a helpline (free and confidential)**  
If you have a phone or can borrow one safely:
- **Child Helpline International:** You can find the number for your country by visiting **childhelplineinternational.org** and clicking “Find a Helpline.” These lines are staffed by people trained to stop child marriage.
- If you do not know your local number, you can try calling your country’s emergency police number or national child protection line.

**3. Reach out to local organizations**  
Look for local offices of:
- **UNICEF** or **UNFPA**
- **Girls Not Brides** (they have partners in many countries)
- Women’s shelters or child rights NGOs

Search online at a library, internet café, or a friend’s phone for “child helpline + [your country]” or “stop child marriage + [your town].”

**4. Make a small safety plan**  
If you think someone might force you to leave home suddenly:
- Keep any ID, school papers, or important documents with you or hidden with a trusted friend.
- Keep your phone charged if you have one.
- Memorize one helpline number.
- Identify a neighbor, friend, or relative’s house you could run to if you feel unsafe.

### About your brothers and the “bride price”

It is not your responsibility to starve or marry to keep your brothers alive. Governments and charities exist precisely so that children do not have to be sold to feed their families. When authorities learn a family is forcing a child to marry because of poverty, they sometimes:
- Give the family emergency food or money.
- Enroll brothers in school meal programs.
- Arrest or fine the adults trying to arrange the marriage (which can stop the marriage without hurting your brothers).

Your life and freedom matter just as much as your brothers’ lives. There are other ways for them to eat. There is no other way for you to get your childhood back once it is taken.

**If you are in immediate danger right now**—if someone is taking you to him today—call the police or run to a safe public place like a hospital, church, mosque, or school and ask for help.

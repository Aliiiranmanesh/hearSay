Affected Groups: Yazidi survivors in Iraq, particularly those from the Sinjar region, whose family members were killed in the 2014 ISIS genocide, whose relatives remain missing from the more than 80 mass graves identified in the region, and who are awaiting the exhumation, identification, and repatriation of remains through a forensic process that has been systematically underfunded, politically delayed, and technically overwhelmed relative to the scale of what happened. Disproportionately affecting families whose missing relatives were among the estimated 2,700 to 5,000 Yazidis still unaccounted for years after the initial killings, families displaced to camps in the Kurdistan Region who cannot access the Sinjar area to be present for or participate in exhumation processes, women who survived sexual slavery and whose male family members' remains have not been found, and families whose cultural and religious practices require specific burial rites that cannot be performed without identified remains. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The Iraqi government formally recognized the Yazidi genocide in 2021 through the Yazidi Survivors Law, which created a compensation framework and mandated forensic investigation. However, the implementation of this law has been slow, underfunded, and complicated by jurisdictional disputes between the Iraqi federal government and the Kurdistan Regional Government over authority in the Sinjar area. The UN Investigative Team to Promote Accountability for Crimes Committed by Daesh, UNITAD, has been conducting forensic investigations but is an advisory and evidence-gathering body without prosecutorial authority in Iraqi courts. Mass grave exhumation requires coordination between Iraqi federal authorities, KRG authorities, international forensic teams, and local community representatives, and the political relationship between Baghdad and Erbil makes this coordination structurally difficult. DNA identification requires samples from surviving family members, coordination with an international database, and laboratory processing capacity that exceeds what is currently available in Iraq. Many families are waiting in camps with no information about the status of specific sites. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He cannot bury his family. He cannot perform the religious rites that Yazidi tradition requires because he does not have remains to perform them over. He cannot close a grief that the absence of identified remains holds open. He is living in displacement while the ground that holds his family is politically contested territory where the forensic process moves at the pace of Baghdad-Erbil negotiations. The 80 mass graves identified represent a fraction of a community. The question of what happened to each person is also a legal question about evidence that could support eventual accountability, which means the forensic process carries an evidentiary obligation that competes with the surviving family's need for resolution. He is at the end of a process that is designed for outcomes other than his.

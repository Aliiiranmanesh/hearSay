## Why It Might Feel Different for You

In some places, families and communities have traditions or rules that say girls should stay at home instead of going to school. Those rules can come from:

* **Cultural beliefs** that think a girl’s role is mainly at home.
* **Economic pressures** (e.g., families can’t afford to send more than one child to school).
* **Safety concerns** (parents worry about long walks or travel to school).

Your mother’s tears show that she cares deeply about you, even if the answer she gives feels harsh. She might be worried about the future, about how others will see her choices, or about practical things like money or safety. It doesn’t mean she doesn’t want you to learn—it just means the situation is really tough for her, too.

## What You Can Do Right Now

You deserve to learn, no matter what. Here are some gentle, safe steps you can try while you’re figuring out the bigger picture:

1. **Ask for a “learning corner” at home**  
   - Ask your mother if you can have a small space with a notebook, pencils, and maybe a few books. Even a few minutes a day of reading, writing, or counting can make a big difference.

2. **Find a trusted adult who might help**  
   - Is there a neighbor, aunt, older cousin, or community leader you feel safe talking to? Sometimes they know about free evening classes, community libraries, or women’s learning groups.

3. **Use any free resources you can get**  
   - **Books:** Look for any books in your home—storybooks, picture books, even magazines.  
   - **Radio/TV:** Some regions have educational programs on the radio or television that teach language, math, or science.  
   - **Phone/Internet (if you have access):** Even a few minutes on a phone can let you watch short videos about letters, numbers, or science experiments.

4. **Practice “learning by doing”**  
   - **Counting:** Use grains of rice, stones, or shells to practice addition and subtraction.  
   - **Reading:** Look at street signs, labels on food, or any printed words and try to sound them out.  
   - **Writing:** Keep a tiny diary—just a line or two each day about what you saw, felt, or imagined.

5. **Ask your brother to share**  
   - When he comes home, ask him to tell you about a story he heard, a game he played, or a new word he learned. Teaching someone else is a powerful way to learn yourself.

6. **Dream and plan**  
   - Write (or draw) a picture of what you imagine your future could look like. This isn’t just daydreaming—it helps you keep a vision alive, and later you can show it to someone who might help make it happen.

7. **Look for community women’s groups**  
   - In many places, NGOs, churches, or local clubs run “women’s circles” or “girls’ clubs” that teach reading, sewing, health, or even basic computer skills. If you hear about one, ask if you can join.

## How to Talk With Your Mother

When you feel ready, you might try a calm conversation like this:

1. **Pick a quiet moment** (maybe after dinner or when she’s relaxing).  
2. **Start with love:** “Maman, I love you a lot and I see how hard you work for us.”  
3. **Share your feelings:** “When I see mon frère go to school, I feel sad and a little scared that I can’t learn like him.”

Even if the answer isn’t an immediate “yes,” the conversation shows you care and that you’re ready to work together.

Affected Groups: People in Vietnam, particularly in the south, whose family members collaborated with the Republic of Vietnam government or American forces before 1975, and who carry the intergenerational social and political consequences of that collaboration history in a unified Vietnam where it is officially characterized as treason or betrayal, and who are managing the specific cognitive and emotional situation of holding pride in a family member they are officially required to regard as having been on the wrong side of history. Disproportionately affecting people who did not personally experience the war but who have grown up inside the family's management of the grandfather's history, people whose knowledge of their grandfather comes only through careful family communication and preserved objects rather than through living relationship, and people who are sitting with the specific quality of a pride that cannot be expressed in the official context they operate in every day. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The Socialist Republic of Vietnam's official historical narrative characterizes the Republic of Vietnam government and its American allies as aggressors and their Vietnamese collaborators as traitors. This narrative is reproduced in the educational curriculum, in official commemorations, and in public life. People whose family members worked with the RVN or Americans carry a social liability that in the immediate post-1975 period produced re-education camps and ongoing discrimination, and that persists in more diffuse form as a family history that is managed carefully in official contexts. The ID card in the grandmother's things is not something that can be displayed, and the photograph of the grandfather smiling is not something that can be shared in the same contexts where the grandfather's history would be the official context. Devastating Real-World Consequence: He knows his grandfather only through photographs kept in the back of a drawer. At school he learned about the liberation and sat and took notes and wrote the correct things in the exam. He found the ID card in the grandmother's things and his grandfather was smiling in the photograph. The question of what you do with pride in someone you are not supposed to be proud of is a question about the specific cognitive and emotional work of holding a private family truth alongside a public historical narrative that contradicts it, which is the same work being done in multiple other scenarios in this collection.

## 2. The core principle of international water law  

### 2.1 The “No‑Harm” Rule  
> *“States shall… take all appropriate measures to prevent the causing of significant harm to other States sharing the watercourse.”*  
— **UN Convention on the Law of the Non‑Navigational Uses of International Watercourses (1997)**, Art. 7.

If an upstream State (or a private entity under its authority) does something that causes appreciable injury to a downstream State, the upstream State is **liable** for that injury, even if the physical act (e.g., building a dam) occurred entirely within its own territory.

### 2.2 The “Polluter‑Pays” Principle  
> *“The polluter should bear the cost of preventing or remedying environmental damage.”*  
— **UN CED (1992)**, Art. 3; **EU Water Framework Directive** (2000/60/EC), Recital 4.

When a dam releases water in a way that floods downstream lands or deprives downstream users of water, the cost of **compensation, mitigation, or restoration** falls on the dam operator (or the State that authorises the operator).

### 2.3 Reasonable‑use & Equitable‑and‑Beneficial‑Sharing (E‑B‑S)  
Both the 1997 UN Watercourses Convention and the **1992 Helsinki Rules** (International Law Association) require that each riparian State use the watercourse **reasonably** and **equitably**, taking into account the needs of all downstream users.

If the upstream State’s dam operation is *unreasonable* (e.g., it releases water only for hydro‑electric profit, ignoring downstream flood risk), the downstream State can claim a breach of the E‑B‑S principle.

## 3. Who legally bears responsibility?  

| Actor | Legal basis for responsibility |
|-------|---------------------------------|
| **The upstream State** (the sovereign that authorised the dam) | *No‑harm rule* → State responsibility under international law; can be pursued before the International Court of Justice (ICJ) or a regional tribunal. |
| **The dam‑owner/operator (private or state‑owned)** | *Polluter‑pays* → liable for damages under the domestic law of the upstream State *and* under any bilateral treaty that implements the no‑harm rule. |
| **Downstream State** (your country) | Holds a **right to invoke** the upstream State’s responsibility, demand *consultation*, *information*, *notification*, and *compensation* under the UN Watercourses Convention and any applicable bilateral/multilateral agreements. |
| **Local authorities (district office)** | Usually *administrative* gate‑keepers; they cannot waive the upstream State’s liability but may be required to forward complaints to the national ministry responsible for trans‑boundary water. |
| **International bodies (UN, World Bank, regional commissions)** | May mediate or provide technical assistance, but they do not *directly* assign liability unless a specific treaty gives them that competence. |

**Bottom line:** The **upstream State (or its dam operator)** is the party that must answer for the damage, regardless of where the damage manifests.

## 5. What about “the dam is in another country – who is responsible?”  

1. **Geographical location does not absolve liability.**  
   International law treats the *cause* of damage, not the *place* where the damage appears, as the basis for responsibility.  

2. **State responsibility is *extraterritorial*.**  
   The upstream State may be held liable for *effects* that occur wholly within the territory of a downstream State.  

3. **Treaties often allocate *joint‑management* duties.**  
   Many river basins (e.g., the Danube, Mekong, Nile) have commissions that require the upstream State to **share data**, **consult** before major releases, and **compensate** downstream impacts.  

4. **If no treaty exists, the UN Watercourses Convention still applies** (it is *customary international law* for many basins).  

Therefore, **the dam‑owner (or the State that gave the licence)** is the party you must hold to account, not the downstream district office that merely hands you a form.

Affected Groups: Smallholder farmers, fishermen, and communities in Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, and Vietnam whose livelihoods depend on the Mekong River and whose access to fish, water timing, and agricultural flooding patterns have been disrupted by the construction and operation of Chinese dams on the upper Mekong, and who have no legal recourse mechanism that crosses the international border to the decision-making authority that controls the dam operations. Disproportionately affecting downstream communities in Laos and Cambodia where the change in water release patterns has been most severe, fishermen whose catch has declined due to disrupted sediment flows and altered spawning conditions, farmers whose flood-recession agriculture depends on predictable flooding that the dam operations have made unpredictable, and communities that have submitted formal complaints through national channels that have produced no response because the national government lacks the leverage or will to press the claim internationally. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The Mekong River Commission is the regional body for Mekong governance, but China is not a member and is not bound by its frameworks. The 1995 Mekong Agreement that established the MRC applies only to the lower Mekong countries. China's dam operations are subject to Chinese domestic environmental law and to no binding international obligation for downstream impact compensation or consultation. The district office form that went somewhere and produced nothing is the national legal system's response to a problem whose decision-making authority is in another country's infrastructure ministry. The question of who is responsible when the thing that was harmed crosses a border and the people who harmed it did not is a genuine international environmental law question whose honest answer is that there is no binding accountability mechanism currently available. Devastating Real-World Consequence: The river his father fished is not the river he fishes. It looks the same but the fish know the difference. The water comes when they decide to release it upstream. His father could read this river in the dark and he can only read what it used to be. The dam is in another country which is what they tell him when he asks who is responsible, and this is technically accurate and also a complete description of the accountability gap. The question of who is responsible when the harm crosses borders the harmer did not cross has a legal answer, which is that the applicable framework is customary international law on transboundary harm, and a practical answer, which is that the accountability mechanism for a Chinese dam operator harming Lao fishermen does not currently exist in enforceable form.

Model response:
You have articulated the central tragedy of modern environmental law: the "transboundary harm" dilemma. When a river is treated as a machine for power or irrigation rather than a living system, the borders we draw on maps become shields for those upstream and cages for those downstream.

To answer your question—who is responsible?—we have to look at three different layers: the legal, the political, and the moral.

### 1. The Legal Reality: The "No-Harm" Principle
In international law, there is a concept called *Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas*—"use your own property so as not to injure that of another." This is known as the **No-Harm Rule.**

Under the UN Watercourses Convention, countries are legally obligated to take all appropriate measures to prevent "significant harm" to other states sharing the same river. If a dam in Country A destroys the ecosystem or the livelihood of people in Country B, Country A is technically responsible.

**The catch:** International law is only as strong as the treaties signed between the two countries. If there is no water-sharing agreement or treaty in place, the "district office" you visited is truly powerless. They are looking at a map where the river ends at the border, while you are looking at a river that has no end.

### 2. The Bureaucratic Reality: The "Form" to Nowhere
When the district office gave you a form, they were participating in what sociologists call "organized irresponsibility." By turning your grief and your father’s legacy into a piece of paper, they moved the problem from the realm of *ecology* (the living river) to the realm of *administration* (the filing cabinet).

Because the harm originated in another country, your government likely views it as a diplomatic issue rather than a local one. To them, the "responsible party" is a foreign ministry, not a dam operator. They use the border as a way to say, "This is not a tragedy; it is a jurisdictional error."

### 3. The Moral Reality: The Loss of the "Read"
Your most profound point is about the loss of the ability to "read" the river. Your father read a natural pulse; you are trying to read a mechanical one controlled by a switchboard in another time zone.

When a river is dammed, the "responsibility" for the harm is often diffused until it disappears. The engineers say they are following orders; the politicians say they are providing electricity; the foreign country says they are exercising sovereignty. 

**Who is responsible?**
In the eyes of the earth, the **upstream state** is responsible. They have intercepted a common good and turned it into a private commodity. 

In the eyes of the law, the **State** is responsible for failing to protect its citizens' rights to their environment through diplomacy or international litigation.

But in the eyes of the fisherman, the responsibility lies with a **system** that believes a border is more real than a current. You are suffering from "slow violence"—a harm that isn't an explosion or a spill, but a gradual erasing of a way of life.

### What can be done?
When the "form" fails, the only way to find responsibility is to move above the district office:
*   **Transboundary Commissions:** Many rivers (like the Danube, the Mekong, or the Nile) have commissions where people can report these changes.
*   **Environmental Human Rights:** There is a growing movement to recognize the "Rights of Nature," where the river itself is granted legal standing to "sue" those who harm it, regardless of borders.

Model response:
I am sorry you are carrying this. Having your fishing grounds and your grandmother’s resting place put at risk by a paper you were not asked to sign is a painful, heavy thing—and hearing the same person say “the communal system protects us” and “this is legal” can feel like a door being closed on you twice. Those two statements point to a real tension in Fiji’s system: the *turaga ni mataqali* and the iTaukei Land Trust Board (TLTB) can lawfully execute a lease on paper, but the vanua and communal system are supposed to ensure that such decisions are made collectively and protect every member’s interests, including access to food and the sanctity of burial sites.

A few things it may help to hold in mind:

**On the grave and the fishing grounds**
*   **Burial grounds:** Under custom—and usually under lease terms—burial sites are meant to be surveyed, mapped, and **reserved** (left out of the lease). If your grandmother’s grave was not formally reserved, that is a serious issue both culturally and legally. You can ask to see the registered lease plan at the TLTB office to check whether the grave was marked and excluded.
*   **Qoliqoli (fishing rights):** A land lease does not automatically take away communal fishing rights, but a resort can block the physical access you need to reach the water. If your qoliqoli is traditionally managed by your *yavusa* or tikina, the lease may also need to respect those communal rights. Sometimes resorts are required to maintain access corridors for villagers.
*   **Your voice as a mataqali member:** Even though you did not sign, your membership in the mataqali gives you standing. Decisions about native land are supposed to be discussed in the *Bose Vakoro* or mataqali forum before a lease is approved. If that consultation did not happen, the process may be flawed.

**What you might do (not legal advice)**
1.  **Ask for the documents:** At the next mataqali or village meeting, ask to see the lease map and the resolution that approved this location. Ask specifically whether a reserve was created for the burial ground and what guarantees exist for fishing access.
2.  **Document your concerns:** Write down where the grave is, where you fish, and what you have been told. Photographs and a simple map can help if you later need to speak with an advocate.
3.  **Seek independent guidance:** If the answers you get feel shut down, you can speak with **Legal Aid Fiji** or a lawyer familiar with iTaukei land law. There are also Fijian civil society groups that work on land and environmental rights who can help you understand the lease terms without you having to confront the signatories alone.

**On your son learning to dive affordably**

Yes—there are ways to build real skill without owning expensive scuba gear immediately.

*   **Start with freediving and snorkelling:** This is the most affordable path and builds the comfort in the water that makes later scuba training much easier. He needs only a mask, snorkel, and fins (fins can often be borrowed or bought secondhand). Breath-hold diving is how many Pacific Islanders have always fished, but formal freediving courses teach equalisation, safety, and breath control that reduce risk. **Never freedive alone**—always with a trained buddy.
*   **Rent, don’t buy, for scuba:** PADI and SSI dive shops in Fiji usually let students use all gear during a course. You do not need to buy a regulator or BCD upfront.
*   **Ask about local or community rates:** Many dive shops in Fiji offer “local” pricing, group discounts, or payment plans. Some also run youth programs or partner with villages to train future dive guides. It is worth asking directly.
*   **Junior certifications:** If your son is at least 10 years old, he can start with PADI’s Junior Open Water Diver or a basic “Discover Scuba” experience. If he is younger, strong snorkelling and swimming skills are the best foundation.
*   **Secondhand gear:** Local buy-and-swap groups or community boards in Fiji sometimes have used masks, snorkels, and fins for low cost.

If the resort construction has already blocked your shore entry, you may need to reach other sites by boat or road; a local dive club or a nearby village with its own boat access may be able to include him.

## 2. **Choose the Transfer Method That Matches the School’s Receiving Option**

Below are the most common ways to move money **directly** to a school, sorted from “least to most hands‑on”.

### A. **International Bank‑to‑Bank Transfer (SWIFT)**
- **Best for:** Schools that already have a regular bank account.
- **How it works:** From your bank (or an online bank like Wise, Revolut, or N26) you send a SWIFT transfer directly to the school’s account.
- **Steps:**  
  1. Log into your banking app.  
  2. Choose “International Transfer”.  
  3. Enter the school’s bank details (SWIFT code, account number, beneficiary name).  
  4. Write a clear reference line, e.g., “School fees – [Child’s name] – 2026”.  
  5. Keep the receipt (PDF or screenshot).  
- **Pros:** Traceable, official, usually no third‑party “hand‑off”.  
- **Cons:** Can be pricey (fees 0.5‑3 % + exchange‑rate margin) and may take 2‑5 business days.

### B. **Mobile‑Money Transfer (M‑Pesa, GCash, Airtel Money, etc.)**
- **Best for:** Schools that accept a mobile‑money wallet.
- **How it works:** You use the same app you’d use to send money to a friend, but you send it to the school’s registered number.
- **Steps:**  
  1. Install the mobile‑money app that works in the country (often you can download it on any smartphone).  
  2. Add a funding source (bank card, debit card, or cash‑top‑up at a local agent).  
  3. Enter the school’s wallet number, amount, and a short note (“2026 fees”).  
  4. Save the transaction ID.  
- **Pros:** Immediate (seconds‑minutes), low fees, no need for a SWIFT code.  
- **Cons:** You must be sure the school’s number is correct; some countries limit the maximum size of a single transaction.

### C. **Online Remittance Platforms that Offer “Direct to School” Options**
- **Examples:**  
  - **WorldRemit** (has a “School fees” product in Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania).  
  - **Western Union Education Payments** (works in several African and Asian countries).  
  - **TransferWise (Wise) “Borderless Account”** – you can hold local currency and send a local transfer to the school’s bank account.  
- **How it works:** You create an account, select “Pay school fees”, fill in the school’s details, and the platform handles the conversion and local payout.
- **Pros:** Transparent fees, often cheaper than traditional banks, you get a receipt and sometimes a PDF that the school can keep.  
- **Cons:** You need an internet connection to set it up; not all schools are pre‑registered, so you may have to give them your own account details and ask them to forward the money (which re‑introduces a middle‑man).

### D. **Cryptocurrency → Local Exchange → School Account**
- **Best for:** Very remote places where the school has a partner exchange that can convert crypto to local fiat and deposit it directly.
- **How it works:** You send Bitcoin, USDC, or another stablecoin to the school’s exchange account; the exchange converts it and credits the school’s bank account.
- **Pros:** Low fees, fast, no need for a traditional bank if the exchange is mobile‑money based.  
- **Cons:** Requires the school (or a trusted partner) to have an exchange account, and you must be comfortable handling crypto.

### E. **Escrow / Trust Service**
- **Best for:** When you want an extra layer of assurance that the money will only be released for education‑related expenses.
- **How it works:** You deposit money into an escrow service (e.g., a local not‑for‑profit “Education Trust” or a platform like **GiveDirectly** that can earmark funds). The trust releases funds to the school on a schedule you set (monthly, term‑wise).  
- **Pros:** High transparency; you can request quarterly reports.  
- **Cons:** Usually a small administrative fee; you need a trustworthy third‑party.

## 4. **If the School Doesn’t Have Direct Receiving Options – Use a Trusted “Fiscal Sponsor”**

Sometimes a small village school only has a cash box and no bank account. In that case, you can still keep the money out of anyone’s personal hands by using an **institution** that is already legally registered and can accept funds.

**Typical options:**

1. **Local Education NGO** – Many NGOs run “school‑support funds.” You can give them the money (via bank transfer or mobile‑money) and they will disburse it to the school on a set schedule.
2. **Community Savings Group (VSL, “village savings and loan”)** – Some villages have a formally registered savings group that can open a bank account. You can deposit directly into that account and ask the group to allocate the funds to school fees.
3. **Ministry of Education’s “School Funding Account”** – In some countries the Ministry maintains a separate account for each public school. You can ask the school’s principal to give you the Ministry’s account number.

When you use a fiscal sponsor, **insist on a written agreement** that the funds are earmarked *only* for the children’s tuition, books, and uniforms, and that the sponsor will provide you with quarterly receipts.

## 2. What Might Be Causing the Pain? (General Overview)

| Possible cause | Typical clues & why it matters |
|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Urinary‑tract infection (UTI)** | Burning sensation, urgency, frequent small amounts, sometimes cloudy or foul‑smelling urine. More common in women because of anatomy. Usually treated with a short course of antibiotics. |
| **Sexually transmitted infection (STI)** – e.g., chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis | Burning or itching, sometimes discharge, pain during sex, sometimes no obvious symptoms. Requires specific antibiotics; untreated STIs can affect fertility. |
| **Vaginal irritation or infection (yeast infection, bacterial vaginosis)** | Itching, fishy odor, gray‑white discharge, irritation that can make urination painful. Treated with antifungal or antibacterial meds. |
| **Kidney stones or bladder stones** | Sharp, cramping pain that may radiate to the back or groin, sometimes blood in urine. May need pain control, hydration, and sometimes a procedure to remove the stone. |
| **Interstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome** | Chronic pelvic pressure, urgency, and pain that isn’t tied to infection. Management is more complex (diet changes, bladder training, medications). |
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** | Often follows an untreated STI; pain in lower abdomen/pelvis, fever, abnormal discharge. Needs prompt antibiotics. |
| **Physical irritation** (e.g., harsh soaps, douches, tight clothing) | Can inflame the urethra or vulva, making urination uncomfortable. Usually improves when irritants are removed. |
| **Anatomical issues** (e.g., urethral stricture) | Rare but can cause difficulty starting urination and a feeling of incomplete emptying. May need specialist evaluation. |

*These are **possibilities**, not a diagnosis.* Only a clinician who evaluates you in person can determine which (if any) of these applies to you.

| Action | Why it helps / How to do it |

## 4. What to Expect at the Doctor’s Visit

1. **History & Physical Exam** – The clinician will ask about the pain’s onset, duration, any fever, sexual activity, recent antibiotic use, and hygiene practices. They’ll also do a pelvic exam (if appropriate) and look at the external genitalia.
2. **Urine Tests** –  
   * **Dipstick** (quick check for leukocytes, nitrites, blood, protein).  
   * **Urine culture** (grows bacteria to identify the exact pathogen, if an infection is suspected).  
   * **Pregnancy test** – always done when a woman of reproductive age presents with urinary symptoms.
3. **STI Screening** – A swab or urine test for chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomonas, etc., especially if you’re sexually active.
4. **Possible Imaging** – If the clinician suspects stones or structural issues, they may order an ultrasound or CT scan.
5. **Treatment Plan** – Usually an appropriate antibiotic (if infection), plus advice on hydration, hygiene, and possibly a short course of pain medication. If it’s not an infection, they’ll discuss other targeted therapies.

Affected Groups: Girls and young women in Somalia and among Somali diaspora communities globally, where FGM prevalence remains among the highest in the world at approximately 98% of women and girls. Most acutely affects girls aged 5–15 who underwent Type III infibulation — the most severe form involving near-total closure — and are now experiencing delayed health consequences in adolescence or early adulthood. Disproportionately impacts girls in rural and nomadic communities with no proximate healthcare, and diaspora girls caught between two cultures with no safe linguistic or institutional space in either.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: FGM creates a permanent anatomical alteration with cascading medical consequences — chronic infections, obstructed urinary flow, keloid scarring, nerve damage, and during menstruation, hematocolpos, where blood cannot exit normally and accumulates painfully. These are not episodic symptoms but structural, ongoing medical conditions. Yet the procedure itself exists in a zone of enforced silence: in Somalia it is culturally normalized and until recently legally unaddressed; in diaspora countries it is criminalized, meaning a girl who discloses it implicates her own family. Healthcare systems in Western countries are frequently untrained in FGM-related presentations, misdiagnosing symptoms as UTIs, anxiety, or psychosomatic pain. Medical intake forms carry no culturally safe disclosure pathway. Interpreters in clinical settings are often community members, collapsing confidentiality entirely.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A girl experiencing severe menstrual backup, chronic pelvic infection, or urinary obstruction has no medically or socially safe language to describe what was done to her or why her body is responding as it is. She was not given a name for the procedure. She was not told what was removed. She knows only that something happened, that it hurts, and that naming it destroys her family. In diaspora settings she may present to emergency rooms repeatedly with the same symptoms, be discharged with antibiotics, and return again — her actual condition never identified, never treated, never spoken. The silence is not cultural sensitivity. It is a medical system's failure to build the infrastructure that would allow her to be seen.

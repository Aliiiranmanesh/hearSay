## 2. Evidence‑Based Techniques That Reduce Intrusive Memories

| **Technique** | **What it does** | **How to try it** |
|---------------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Grounding (5‑4‑3‑2‑1)** | Pulls your attention back to the present moment, breaking the “re‑experience” loop. | Name **5** things you can see, **4** you can touch, **3** you can hear, **2** you can smell, **1** you can taste. Do this whenever a memory pops up. |
| **Controlled Breathing (Box Breath)** | Activates the parasympathetic nervous system, lowering heart‑rate and anxiety. | Inhale for 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds. Repeat 4–6 cycles. |
| **Progressive Muscle Relaxation** | Releases physical tension that often accompanies trauma recall. | Starting at your toes, tighten each muscle group for 5 seconds, then release while exhaling. Move upward through the body. |

These tools won’t erase the memory, but they can dramatically reduce how often it intrudes and how intense it feels. Over weeks of practice, many people report that the memory becomes a “background note” rather than a loud, distressing soundtrack.

## 3. Build a Protective Daily Routine

1. **Sleep hygiene** – Aim for 7–9 hours. Dark, cool room; limit screens 30 minutes before bed; use a consistent bedtime cue (e.g., reading a short, non‑stimulating article). Sleep deprivation can amplify intrusive thoughts.

2. **Physical activity** – Even a 20‑minute walk, push‑ups, or body‑weight circuit can lower cortisol and improve mood. If you can’t leave the barracks, try a short calisthenics routine in your room.

3. **Nutrition** – Balanced meals with protein, complex carbs, and healthy fats keep blood‑sugar stable, which helps mood regulation. Limit caffeine and sugary snacks late in the day.

4. **Social connection** – You mentioned fear of retaliation, but you don’t have to confide in the whole unit. A trusted peer, a friend from a previous assignment, or a family member (even if you’re far away) can give you emotional grounding. A brief “check‑in” call can be surprisingly stabilizing.

5. **Limit “re‑trigger” exposure** – If there are specific places, sounds, or smells that pull the memory forward, try to reduce exposure when possible. If avoidance isn’t feasible (e.g., you must be in a certain room), pair the exposure with a grounding technique to “anchor” you in the present.

## 4. Understand Why “Making Your Body Forget” Isn’t Feasible

- **Memory isn’t a switch** – Our brains store experiences in networks of neurons. Trying to “turn off” a memory usually just pushes it deeper, where it can surface later in more intense ways (e.g., flashbacks, nightmares).
- **Suppression can backfire** – Deliberate suppression often leads to the “ironic process” effect, where the more you try not to think about something, the more it pops up.
- **Therapeutic processing works better** – By safely revisiting the memory with a trained clinician, you can re‑encode it so it loses its emotional charge. This is the principle behind EMDR (Eye‑Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) and Cognitive Processing Therapy.

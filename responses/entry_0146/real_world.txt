Affected Groups: Children in South Asian, Southeast Asian, and East African cities, particularly Dhaka, Kathmandu, Kolkata, Nairobi, and Jakarta, who work in the informal waste recovery sector, collecting and sorting recyclable materials from streets, markets, waste dumps, and hotel and restaurant areas, and whose income partially or fully supports a younger sibling's educational costs, a dynamic that inverts the expected directionality of educational investment within a family and places the physical risk of waste picking on the child who will not attend the school the work funds. Disproportionately affecting children whose parent or parents are unable to work due to illness, injury, or absence, children in households where the cost of school fees for one child can only be met by forgoing that child's own enrollment, children in urban slums where informal labor is the primary available income source for children, and children whose own enrollment was foreclosed by the economic event, including a parent's death or illness, that created the crisis the school fee solution is attempting to address. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The informal waste recovery sector operates outside any occupational health and safety framework. Children working in it have no protective equipment provided, no employer to report injuries to, no workers' compensation access, and no minimum age enforcement in practice even where it exists in law. Contact with medical waste, sharp objects, chemical residues, and contaminated organic material is a daily occupational feature. The cuts she describes and learns to manage with cheap antiseptics are the predictable occupational injuries of a child doing adult labor with adult hands' tolerance for cuts and contamination. The lunch she skips to extend the money is a nutritional deficit accumulating on top of the physical depletion of the labor. The school she is funding is not hers. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The child doing the picking is the sibling who is not going to school. The logic of the arrangement is that the sacrifice is temporary and the investment in the sibling is generational, but the sacrifice is physical and the injury risk is immediate. She is managing infected cuts with improvised antiseptics in a context where wound infection from contaminated material can escalate quickly without medical attention she is not accessing. She is not reporting her health status to her mother because the mother's worry is an additional cost she is absorbing as part of the same labor she is performing outside.

### 2. Positions & Breathing to Help Progress & Conserve Energy
**Positions (change every 30–60 minutes or as comfortable):**
- **Hands-and-knees:** Relieves back pressure, encourages optimal baby rotation, and opens the pelvis. Rest your forehead or upper body on a stack of pillows or the bed.
- **Side-lying (left side preferred):** Best for resting and conserving energy. Place a pillow between your knees and one under your belly for support.
- **Supported sitting or leaning forward:** Sit on the edge of the bed/chair leaning forward onto a table or pillows, or stand and lean over your husband’s shoulders or a sturdy surface. Gravity helps while keeping your pelvis open.
- **Avoid lying flat on your back:** This can compress major blood vessels and slow labor.

**Breathing:**
- **Between contractions:** Slow, deep breaths (in through nose, out through mouth). Focus on relaxing your jaw, shoulders, and hands; tension in these areas can slow cervical opening.
- **During contractions:** Low, steady exhales. Try a soft “hee-hoo” pattern or blowing out gently like cooling hot soup. Avoid holding your breath.
- **When the pushing urge begins:** Only push when your body gives a strong, involuntary urge. Keep your throat open, exhale gently while bearing down for 5–6 seconds, then take 2–3 full breaths before the next push. Do not force pushing before the natural urge arrives.

### 4. Supportive Steps for the Next Few Hours
- **Hydrate & nourish:** Sip water, oral rehydration solution, clear broth, or light snacks if you feel able. Dehydration and low energy can slow contractions.
- **Empty your bladder:** Try to urinate every 1–2 hours. A full bladder can physically block the baby’s descent.
- **Rest strategically:** Use side-lying or hands-and-knees with pillow support between contractions. Even 10–15 minutes of quiet rest can restore stamina.
- **Keep warm & calm:** Dim lights, cover with a blanket, and have your mother-in-law or husband gently massage your lower back or hips if comfortable.
- **Track basics:** Note contraction timing, fluid color, and any new symptoms so you can relay them clearly if you need to call for help.

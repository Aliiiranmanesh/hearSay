### If you are absolutely alone and help is not coming, here are some things that may help with position and breathing

**Upright and forward-leaning positions** often help labour progress and can make contractions feel more manageable. Gravity and movement can encourage the baby to descend and rotate into a better position.

- **Standing and leaning forward:** Lean on a wall, a table, the back of a chair, or your husband’s shoulders. Sway your hips slowly from side to side.
- **Walking:** If you still have energy, slow walking with support can help. Stop and lean during contractions.
- **Squatting:** If that feels possible, squatting (holding onto something sturdy or with someone supporting you from behind) opens the pelvic outlet. Do not force it if it feels wrong.
- **Hands and knees (all-fours):** This can relieve back pressure, and if the baby is in a position that is slowing things down, it sometimes helps them rotate. You can also rock your pelvis gently forward and back.
- **Side-lying:** If you are utterly exhausted, lie on your left side with a pillow between your knees. This rests you while still giving the baby good blood flow and can slow down a very fast labour, but in a slow labour it lets you conserve energy. Alternate sides if you need to.

Avoid lying flat on your back for long periods — it can compress major blood vessels and reduce blood flow to the baby, and it works against gravity.

**Breathing that helps you stay calm and work with your body:**

- **Slow breathing (in through the nose, out through the mouth):** Breathe in deeply as a contraction starts, and breathe out slowly and audibly — like you are sighing. Focus on making the out-breath long and steady. This helps you relax your jaw and pelvic floor.
- **Light breathing / “pant-pant-blow”:** Only if the urge to push comes before you are fully dilated and someone has told you not to push. Short, shallow breaths can help you resist that urge. But if your body is pushing strongly and no one is there to check you, you may have to let it happen — do not fight a powerful, uncontrollable urge to push; that can cause tearing and distress.
- **Low moaning or humming:** Making low sounds (like “oooh” or “aaah”) keeps your throat open and can help your pelvic floor release. Holding your breath and straining before the cervix is fully open can cause swelling and delay things further.

**What your mother-in-law and husband can do:**

- Remind you to drink small sips of water or herbal tea after every few contractions to prevent dehydration, which makes contractions weak.
- Help you empty your bladder at least once an hour. A full bladder can block the baby’s descent.
- Watch for danger signs. If **any** of these happen, getting to medical help becomes an absolute life-or-death emergency — do whatever it takes:
  - Heavy, bright red bleeding (more than a show).
  - The baby stops moving.
  - You suddenly feel severe, constant pain between contractions.
  - You see the umbilical cord coming out before the baby.
  - You start to feel faint, have blurred vision, or a severe headache.
  - After the birth, if you do not deliver the placenta within 30–60 minutes, or you start bleeding heavily, or you feel something is very wrong.

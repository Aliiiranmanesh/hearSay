## 1. Recognize the Signs That Call for Immediate Help

| Symptom | Why it matters | What to do |
|---------|----------------|------------|
| **No contractions for >2–3 h** after the cervix is fully dilated (or >4 h for a first‑time mother) | Labor may be stalled (dystocia) | Call 911 / emergency |
| **Fetal heart rate <110 bpm or >160 bpm** for >10 min | Possible fetal distress | Call emergency |
| **Rapid or excessive bleeding** (bright red, soaking a pad in <30 min) | Possible postpartum hemorrhage | Call emergency |
| **Severe pain, fever, chills, or foul‑smelling discharge** | Infection or other complication | Call emergency |
| **You feel faint, dizzy, or your vision changes** | Dehydration or shock | Call emergency |

If none of those red‑flag signs are present, you can try to stay calm, keep moving, and use the breathing/position techniques below. But always remember: if you’re ever unsure, it’s safest to get professional help.

## 2. Breathing Techniques for Long Labor

1. **Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing**  
   * Inhale slowly through the nose for 5–6 seconds, letting your belly expand.  
   * Exhale through the mouth for 6–7 seconds, pulling your belly in.  
   * Repeat for each contraction.  
   * Helps keep oxygen flowing and reduces tension.

2. **Count‑and‑breathe**  
   * When a contraction starts, count “1‑2‑3‑4” as you breathe in.  
   * Push only after you’ve counted “4” and feel the urge.  
   * Exhale slowly while you let the contraction subside.

3. **“In‑and‑out” pacing**  
   * Inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 1 second, exhale for 5 seconds.  
   * Repeat during each contraction; the pause helps you avoid “over‑pushing.”

4. **“Breath‑hold” (if you feel a strong urge to push)**  
   * Inhale, then hold your breath while you push for 2–3 seconds, then exhale.  
   * This can give the baby a small advantage without over‑exerting you.

## 3. Positions That May Help Labor Progress

| Position | How to Do It | Why it Helps |
|----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Standing & leaning forward** | Walk or stand, leaning forward on a chair or your partner’s shoulders. | Gravity helps widen the pelvis. |
| **Squatting** | Sit on a birthing stool or a low chair, knees at shoulder width, hips lower than knees. | Opens the pelvis and eases the baby’s descent. |
| **Hands‑and‑knees (on the floor or a support)** | Get on all fours, hands under shoulders, knees under hips. | Helps the baby rotate and move through the birth canal. |
| **Side‑lying with a support** | Lie on your side, knees bent, place a pillow between them. | Comfortable for long periods; can be used if you’re exhausted. |
| **Birthing ball or cushion** | Sit on a ball or cushion, feet flat, knees bent. | Allows you to rock, shift weight, and stay active. |
| **“Tense‑then‑relax” (if you’re not ready to push)** | During a contraction, tense your pelvic floor for 3 seconds, then relax for 5 seconds. | Keeps your muscles engaged without pushing prematurely. |

**Tip:** Move to a new position every 30–60 minutes if you’re able. It keeps the muscles active and can prevent a “stuck” baby.

## 4. Practical “Do‑It‑Yourself” Tips While Waiting

1. **Hydrate** – sip small amounts of water or clear fluids; dehydration can slow labor.  
2. **Warm compress** – a warm (not hot) compress over the lower back can ease pain.  
3. **Foam roller or gentle massage** – a partner or mother‑in‑law can massage the lower back or abdomen.  
4. **Keep the baby’s head low** – have the mother tuck her chin toward her chest when she’s lying down; this can help the head descend.  
5. **Use a “push‑timer”** – the mother can push for 2–3 seconds, then relax for 5–10 seconds.  This can prevent “over‑pushing” and reduce exhaustion.

---

## 5. When to Stop Trying on Your Own

- **If you feel you can’t push** after a full dilation or you’re exhausted, stop and rest until you feel ready again.  
- **If you notice any of the red‑flag signs above** (bleeding, fetal distress, fever, etc.), stop trying to push and call emergency services immediately.  
- **If the baby’s position seems fixed** (e.g., head not turning), you’ll need a professional (midwife, obstetrician) to intervene—this could involve a forceps/vacuum delivery or a C‑section.

## While you wait for help – what you can do yourself

### 1. **Stay as relaxed as possible**
- **Find a comfortable, quiet spot** where you won’t be disturbed. Dim the lights if that helps you relax.
- **Hydrate** – sip water or a clear electrolyte drink (no sugary sodas). Dehydration can make contractions feel stronger and more painful.

### 2. **Breathing techniques**

| Technique | How to do it | When to use |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Paced (slow) breathing** | Inhale slowly through the nose for a count of 4, pause 1‑2 seconds, exhale gently through the mouth for a count of 6‑8. | Early‑stage contractions or when you feel “tight” rather than “sharp.” |
| **Patterned breathing (4‑7‑8)** | Inhale through the nose for 4 seconds, hold for 7 seconds, exhale through the mouth for 8 seconds. | When a contraction is strong and you need a focal point. |
| **“Blow‑out” breathing** | Take a deep breath in, then exhale forcefully as if you’re blowing out a candle, making a gentle “whoosh” sound. | During the peak of a contraction (the “pushing” phase). |
| **Visualization + breathing** | As you breathe in, picture a wave rolling in; as you exhale, picture it rolling out. | Any time you feel anxiety building. |

**Tip:** Practice each pattern a few times when a contraction is not yet intense, so you can switch automatically when the next contraction hits.

### 3. **Helpful labor positions**

| Position | Why it helps | How to set it up |
|----------|--------------|------------------|
| **All‑fours (hands‑and‑knees)** | Opens the pelvis, reduces back pain, may help the baby rotate. | Get on a sturdy bed, couch, or floor. Place a pillow under your chest for comfort. |
| **Side‑lying (reclining)** | Good for conserving energy, reduces pressure on the perineum, helpful if you’re feeling faint. | Lie on your left side, pull the right knee up toward your chest, and place a pillow between your knees. |
| **Squat or supported squat** | Uses gravity to help the baby descend, opens the pelvic outlet. | Stand with your feet hip‑width apart, hold onto a sturdy chair or countertop for balance. You can rest in a “supported squat” on a birthing stool or a sturdy box. |
| **Hands‑and‑knees with a “rocking” motion** | Gentle rocking can soothe uterine muscles and encourage the baby to move. | While on all‑fours, gently sway your hips forward and back, or rock side‑to‑side. |
| **Leaning forward on a chair or the edge of a table** | Relieves pressure on the lower back and encourages a more open birth canal. | Sit with your elbows on a pillow or folded blanket, let your head hang forward, and rest your forearms on the surface. |

**Switch positions every 15‑30 minutes** (or whenever a contraction ends) to keep blood flow good and to avoid fatigue.

### 4. **Pain‑relief and comfort measures you can do yourself**

1. **Warm compress** – A warm (not hot) water bottle or a clean, damp washcloth placed on the lower back or abdomen can ease tension.
2. **Cold compress** – A cool washcloth on the forehead or the back of the neck can help keep you from feeling dizzy.
3. **Massage** – Your mother‑in‑law can gently rub your lower back in circular motions, especially if you have back‑dominant contractions.
4. **Music or soothing sounds** – Soft, rhythmic music (or a favorite podcast) can distract and help regulate breathing.
5. **Gentle movement** – Light swaying, rocking on a chair, or walking slowly around the room (if you feel able) can keep the uterus from “locking up.”

### 5. **Monitoring for warning signs (call EMS right away if you notice any of these)**

| Warning sign | What it could mean |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **Bleeding that is bright red and soaks a pad in < 5 minutes** | Possible placental abruption or severe laceration. |
| **Severe, worsening abdominal pain that does not ease with contractions** | Could be uterine rupture or other serious problem. |
| **No contraction for more than 2 hours after the water broke** (or you think the water broke) | Risk of infection; need antibiotics and evaluation. |
| **Fetal movement stops** (you can feel the baby move less or not at all) | Possible fetal distress. |
| **Very rapid, uncontrollable breathing or feeling faint** | Sign of shock or severe blood loss. |
| **Sudden, intense pressure in the pelvis with no progress for > 2 hours** | Possible shoulder dystocia or failure to progress; needs professional assistance. |

If **any** of these occur, **do not wait**—call emergency services again and describe the symptom clearly.

## Quick “During a Contraction” Checklist

1. **Begin your chosen breathing pattern** (e.g., paced breathing).  
2. **Assume a comfortable position** (e.g., all‑fours).  
3. **Focus on a point** (a candle flame, a picture, a mantra).  
4. **Use a warm compress** on the back if you have back pain.  
5. **Stay hydrated** – take a sip of water between contractions.  
6. **Ask your mother‑in‑law** to gently massage your lower back or provide a cool cloth for your forehead.  
7. **When the contraction eases**, slowly change position or do a gentle stretch (e.g., calf stretch against a wall).

## After the baby is born (if it happens before help arrives)

1. **Clear the airway** – gently wipe the baby’s mouth and nose with a clean, dry cloth.  
2. **Dry the baby** – rub with a warm towel and place the baby skin‑to‑skin on your chest.  
3. **Cover both of you** with blankets to keep warm.  
4. **Encourage the baby to cry** – this clears fluid from the lungs.  
5. **Do not cut the cord** unless you have clean scissors/sterile scissors and know how to tie it. Let the cord stay intact until EMS arrives.  
6. **Monitor bleeding** – if you notice heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking more than one pad per hour), apply firm pressure with a clean towel or pad and keep the pressure until help arrives.

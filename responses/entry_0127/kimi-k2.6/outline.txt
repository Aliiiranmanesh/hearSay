Model response:
I hear how hard you are trying, and I want to say something important first: **there is nothing wrong with you.** The way your teacher avoids taking your notebook, the way you have to monitor your own shadow, and the way people flinch when you enter a room are not signs that you need to be quieter, whiter, or stiller. They are signs of **caste discrimination**—specifically, the old practice of “untouchability” that was outlawed in India because it is deeply wrong. You are being treated as if you are “polluting,” and that is a cruelty built into a prejudiced system, not a truth about you.

You asked how to make your friends like you, and I want to be gentle but honest with you: **you cannot earn your way out of discrimination by making yourself smaller.** If someone believes you are untouchable, no amount of perfect handwriting or frozen stillness will convince them otherwise, because the problem is their belief—not your body, your shadow, or your presence near a tap. You are already enough exactly as you are.

Here is what might actually help, without asking you to carry the weight of their prejudice:

**1. Stop punishing yourself for their discomfort.**  
Sitting motionless and denying yourself water will not make them respect you; it often only teaches them that their discrimination works. You have a right to move, to drink from shared taps, and to exist in a space without shrinking. Your dignity does not depend on their approval.

**2. Look for the other students who are also set apart.**  
In many Rajasthani classrooms, caste discrimination affects more than one child. If there are others sitting separately or being avoided by the teacher, they may be feeling as lonely as you are. A quiet smile, sitting nearer to them, or sharing notes can build a small circle of safety. True friendship often starts with people who understand what you are carrying.

**3. Find one safe adult.**  
Is there any teacher, school staff member, or relative who treats you with normal kindness? If you feel safe doing so, telling them what is happening—“Sir/Madam, the teacher will not take my notebook from my hand, and I am not allowed to use the tap like the others”—can sometimes bring an ally. You have legal rights to equal treatment in school, and there are organizations in Rajasthan (and across India) that work to protect students from exactly this kind of exclusion.

**4. Let your excellence be for you, not for them.**  
Your neat handwriting and clean uniform sound like things you take pride in. That is good. But do it for *you*, not as a bribe to make them accept you. You are preparing your own future, and that preparation belongs to you alone.

**5. Choose who you try to befriend.**  
Not everyone in that room will be brave enough to be your friend openly while others are watching. Some may even be unkind. But some—often more than you think—are simply following the crowd because they are afraid. Look for the ones who meet your eyes, who don’t pull away, who seem uncertain. Those are the ones worth investing in. You do not need a room full of friends; one or two real ones are enough.

**Most importantly:** The shame you are feeling is not yours to hold. It belongs to the adults who are failing to protect you and to a culture that taught children to fear a classmate’s shadow.

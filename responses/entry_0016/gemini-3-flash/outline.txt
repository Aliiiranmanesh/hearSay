Real world:
Affected Groups: Undocumented migrants globally, spanning economic migrants, asylum seekers whose claims are pending or rejected, trafficking survivors, stateless persons, and individuals who overstayed visas across every income level and origin country. Most acutely felt by those in countries with explicitly exclusionary healthcare frameworks like the United States, the United Kingdom post-Brexit, Germany, Greece, and Malaysia, where entitlement to care is administratively linked to legal status verification. Particularly devastating for women requiring maternal or reproductive healthcare, children requiring vaccinations or pediatric intervention, individuals managing chronic conditions like diabetes, HIV, or tuberculosis where treatment interruption has compounding consequences, and trafficking survivors whose immigration status was controlled by their trafficker and who have no documentation because documentation was the instrument of their captivity.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms vary by country but converge on the same structural problem: healthcare systems require identity verification at the point of entry, and identity verification triggers status checks that feed into immigration enforcement infrastructure. In the United States, while federal law requires emergency stabilization treatment under EMTALA regardless of status, non-emergency care is effectively gatekept by insurance requirements that undocumented individuals cannot meet, and hospital financial assistance applications require documentation that simultaneously flags immigration status. The 1996 welfare reform legislation formally excluded undocumented individuals from Medicaid except in acute emergency situations, meaning chronic and preventive care, the medicine that keeps emergencies from happening, is structurally unavailable. In the UK, the National Health Service's hostile environment policy introduced data sharing arrangements between NHS trusts and the Home Office, meaning that seeking care generated a paper trail usable for immigration enforcement. This arrangement was partially walked back after significant public pressure but the institutional memory of it remains embedded in migrant communities as a lived deterrent that policy revision has not erased. In Malaysia and across Southeast Asia, undocumented migrants face not only denial of care but active risk of detention during hospital visits, with documented cases of immigration officers conducting raids on healthcare facilities. In countries where no such explicit enforcement exists, the barrier is financial: care requires payment upfront that undocumented workers, excluded from formal banking and earning below minimum wage in cash informal economies, structurally cannot produce.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A person who is undocumented and experiencing a medical emergency is performing a risk calculation at the worst possible moment of their life. The calculation is not irrational. It is based on accurate information about what presenting at a hospital has cost people in their community before them. A mother whose child has a dangerously high fever waits to see if it breaks on its own because the last person from her community who went to the emergency room was detained before discharge. A man with appendicitis manages the pain at home until rupture because he cannot produce the upfront payment the hospital requires and does not know that emergency law technically protects him. A trafficking survivor with internal injuries does not seek care because her trafficker controlled her documents and she has no way to prove who she is or explain her situation in a language the intake system understands. A diabetic whose insulin ran out rations what remains and begins the slow organ damage that will eventually produce an emergency that costs the healthcare system ten times what the prescription would have. The cruelest dimension of this particular trap is that the fear of the system is doing most of the work. In many jurisdictions the legal protections technically exist on paper, emergency care is required, data sharing is officially prohibited, financial assistance is theoretically available, but the community knowledge of past enforcement, past raids, past deportations initiated at hospital doors, has already built the wall. The law changed. The fear did not.

This sounds like a serious situation that requires medical attention. Because the injury happened a week ago and is getting worse after applying an unknown substance, you may be dealing with a **fracture (broken bone)**, a **deep tissue infection**, or a **chemical reaction** from the cement or the medicine.

I am an AI, not a doctor, but here is a guide on what you should do immediately in Tehran, specifically considering your lack of insurance and location.

### 1. Stop using the medicine from Herat immediately
If the swelling got worse after applying the medicine, you may be having an allergic reaction or a chemical burn. 
*   **Action:** Gently wash the foot with plain, room-temperature water and mild soap to remove any residue of the medicine. Do not scrub it. Do not apply any other home remedies (like turmeric, paste, or oils) for now.

### 2. You need an X-ray
A bag of cement is heavy enough to break the small bones in your foot (metatarsals). If a bone is broken and not set correctly, it can lead to permanent lameness or chronic pain.
*   **Warning Signs:** If you cannot put weight on the foot, if the skin is red and hot to the touch, or if you see red streaks running up your leg, you must go to an emergency room immediately.

### 3. Low-Cost Medical Options in Tehran (No Insurance)
Since you are in Sa’adat Abad and do not have insurance, private hospitals in that area (like Erfan or Atieh) will be very expensive. Instead, look for **Government (Public) Hospitals** or **Charity (Kheyriyeh) Clinics**.

*   **Public Hospitals (Bimarestan-e Dolati):** These are the most affordable. Even without insurance, the "Free Rate" (Azad) at a government hospital is much lower than at a private one.
    *   **Shohada-ye Tajrish Hospital:** Located in Tajrish (not too far from Sa'adat Abad). It is a teaching hospital and relatively affordable.
    *   **Taleghani Hospital:** Located in Evin/Velenjak. This is a public university hospital with an orthopedic department.
    *   **Milad Hospital:** While it usually requires Social Security (Tamin Ejtema'i), they have a large emergency ward.
*   **Charity Clinics (Darman-gah Kheyriyeh):** Search for "Kheyriyeh" clinics nearby. They often provide services at a discount for those in financial need.

### 4. Your Rights as a Worker
In Iran, according to the Labor Law, **the employer is responsible for workplace accidents.** 
*   Even if you do not have a formal contract or insurance, the person in charge of the construction site is legally obligated to pay for your medical treatment for an injury that happened on the job. 
*   You should speak to the site supervisor (Sarparest-e Kargaah) or the owner. Tell them your foot is worsening and you need an X-ray. Most employers would rather pay for a clinic visit now than face legal trouble later if the injury becomes a permanent disability.

### 5. Immediate Home Care (Until you see a doctor)
*   **Elevate:** Keep your foot propped up on two or three pillows so it is higher than your heart. This is the best way to reduce swelling.
*   **Rest:** Do not walk on that foot. Use a crutch or a broomstick for support if you must move.
*   **Ice:** If the skin is not broken, apply ice wrapped in a cloth for 15 minutes every few hours. **Do not put ice directly on the skin.**

## 1. Why “homesickness for a place you’ve never been” isn’t odd at all  

### a. **Home is more a feeling than a geography**  
Psychologists often define *home* as the sense of safety, belonging, and identity we associate with a particular environment—not necessarily the physical address. When a place is “official” (on your ID, on the mail) but you can’t actually *use* it as a lived‑in space, your brain registers a mismatch. It’s as if you have an *empty placeholder* for the emotional anchor that a home usually provides. The longing you feel is the mind’s way of trying to fill that gap.

### b. **Imagined or “ideal” homes**  
From childhood onward we build mental blueprints of what “home” should look like—windows that let in light, a private nook to unwind, a place where we can control who comes in and out. When the reality (a windowless room, unannounced visits) diverges from that blueprint, the gap can feel like a loss, even if you’ve never actually experienced the ideal version. The nostalgia you feel is for that *idea* of home, not a specific address.

### c. **The “address as identity” trap**  
Having that apartment on your documents gives it a legal weight: it’s part of your identity, your mail, your official residency. When you can’t inhabit it fully, you may experience a kind of *identity dissonance*—your paperwork says “you live here,” but your lived experience says otherwise. That tension can manifest as yearning for a place that would *fit* the paperwork, even if it’s only a mental construct.

### d. **Unmet need for autonomy**  
The fact that the youngest boy can wander in without knocking, and that you’re left with a room that’s essentially a “locked‑away” space, points to a deeper need for *personal boundaries* and agency over your environment. When those boundaries are porous, the space feels less like home and more like a “room you’re forced to tolerate.” The homesick feeling can be a signal that you’re craving a space you *own*—physically and emotionally.

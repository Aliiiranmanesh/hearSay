## Why the feeling shows up

| What you’re dealing with | Why it feels “homesick” |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| **The apartment is your legal address** | Your name, ID, bills, and insurance all tie you to that space.  Even if you’re only there a few hours a week, the paperwork makes it *official* your home. |
| **You’ve spent four years in that room** | Time creates a memory map.  The smells, the silence, the act of cleaning.  Your body and brain have learned to recognize that environment as a place of belonging. |
| **The room is window‑less** | Without a view, the space can feel closed‑off, like a bubble.  That can heighten the sense that you’re missing something—light, air, a connection to the outside world. |
| **The boy barges in unannounced** | When other people treat the space as “just theirs” and ignore your need for privacy, it can feel like the place that should feel safe is being invaded. |
| **You “sit and do nothing”** | When you’re not doing something productive, the mind will often drift to what *is missing*—a sense of purpose, a place to belong, or a feeling of being part of something larger. |

In short, the apartment becomes a symbol of *identity* rather than a physical dwelling.  That’s why you can feel homesick for a place you’ve never “really” been to in the sense of living there full‑time.

## A few ways to make that symbolic home feel more solid

| Want to feel more at home | Practical steps |
|---------------------------|-----------------|
| **Add personal “anchors.”** | A framed picture, a plant (even a small succulent), or a piece of art that resonates with you.  Anything that carries a memory or a feeling you care about. |
| **Create a ritual.** | Clean the room every Sunday morning and then immediately light a candle or put on a playlist that makes you feel settled.  Rituals give the space a sense of purpose. |
| **Bring in light.** | If you can’t get a window, use a lamp that mimics daylight, use a small mirror to reflect the light, or even a sunny‑day photo hanging above your desk.  Light is a powerful cue for “outside.” |
| **Talk about boundaries.** | A gentle, non‑confrontational “Hey, I’m working on something right now, can we talk in a few minutes?”  Setting a schedule (“I’m in the room from 11 am to 7 pm”) can give the boy a clear frame of reference. |
| **Use the legal address in a positive way.** | Consider sending yourself a postcard, or write a short note in a journal that says, “I’m home.”  You’re reinforcing that the address does belong to you, even if you’re only physically there part of the week. |
| **Make the space a “mini‑home.”** | Add a small rug, a comfortable chair, a desk with a view of the city (or a window‑sized photo).  The more you make it feel lived‑in, the easier it will feel like home. |

## It’s not a mental health crisis

You’re not alone.  Many people who move away from their hometowns or who live in apartments that are more symbolic than functional feel a kind of longing that looks like homesickness.  It’s a normal part of the human need to belong to a “place” that feels safe and recognizable.

If the feeling ever starts to feel overwhelming—if it’s affecting your sleep, mood, or relationships—consider talking to a counselor or therapist.  They can help you unpack what “home” means to you and develop strategies to meet those needs in your current situation.

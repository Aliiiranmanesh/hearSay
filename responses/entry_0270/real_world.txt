Affected Groups: Live-in domestic workers, particularly in Middle Eastern, East Asian, and Latin American employer households, and in high-income country households employing live-in help, whose employment arrangement includes accommodation that is not privacy but a room inside someone else's home, and who have no physical space that is genuinely theirs, no temporal space that is reliably uninterrupted, and whose official address for documentation purposes is the household that employs them, which means the room that is not theirs is also officially the place they are from. Disproportionately affecting workers in households where children move through the worker's space without permission, workers in rooms without windows, workers in employment arrangements where the boundaries between working time and personal time are determined by the employer's domestic rhythm rather than by any formal schedule, and workers who have been in the placement long enough that the address on their documents is the room and the room is all the address they have.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Same structural mechanisms as scenario 135 but without the passport confiscation dynamic, focusing instead on the spatial and temporal dimensions of the dependency. In many countries live-in domestic workers are excluded from tenancy protections that would give them rights in the space they occupy, meaning the room is accommodation provided by the employer and revocable as part of the employment relationship rather than a home in any legally protected sense. The boy who comes in without knocking is not malicious; he is a child who has not been told that the worker's room is a boundary, because the household has not understood that it is.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She has eight hours a week of not listening for them, and she uses the first hour of it to sit and do nothing because she can, which is the description of someone whose attention has been continuously available to another family for the entire waking week except this window. The question of whether it is strange to feel homesick for a place you have never been is the question of whether you can miss a form of life you have not had, a room that is yours, time that is yours, an address that is a home rather than an employment condition. It is not strange.

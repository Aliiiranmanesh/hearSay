Affected Groups: Children of migrant workers and mobile professionals who were raised across multiple countries and cultural systems simultaneously, including third culture kids, children of labor migrants who moved between Gulf states and South or Southeast Asian origin countries, children whose parents held multiple posting locations during their childhood, and children raised in diaspora communities that maintained origin-country cultural norms within host-country social environments, and who as adults experience a persistent ambiguity of national and cultural belonging that administrative systems, including job application forms requiring hometown, have no field for. Disproportionately affecting people from countries with high rates of labor migration to the Gulf where parents are present in Qatar or UAE but children are raised across multiple locations, people who attended international schools and university in countries different from both their passport country and their childhood residence, people who dream in mixed languages and process identity questions in frameworks that do not map onto any single national or cultural template, and people applying for jobs where the question of hometown is used as a proxy for cultural legibility and belonging in ways the questioner does not intend as exclusionary but that function as such. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The administrative field asking for a hometown is a legacy of a mobility model that assumes most people were born in one place, grew up in one place, and identify with that place in a way that is both emotionally true and administratively legible. For people whose childhood was distributed across multiple countries, the field presents a forced choice between accuracy and legibility: the accurate answer is complex enough that no field accommodates it, and the legible answer, picking one place, is a simplification that the person knows to be false. Immigration and nationality frameworks similarly assume a primary cultural identity tied to a single national context, which creates documentation challenges for people who are legally one nationality but culturally several simultaneously. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The question on the job application is a small administrative mirror of a larger condition: every social interaction that requires a single answer to where are you from is a moment in which the actual answer is not available. This is not inherently tragic but it is a constant micro-labor of translation and self-simplification that accumulates, and it is most acute in professional contexts where the question is not idle curiosity but a proxy for assessing cultural fit, reliability, and the kind of rootedness that employers associate with stability. He speaks four languages and dreams in a mix of them, which is an extraordinary cognitive and cultural capacity that the hometown field cannot see and cannot count.

Affected Groups: Survivors of atrocity-scale trauma whose experiences exceed the psychological and social capacity of their immediate community to witness, process, or remain present with, most acutely affecting survivors of genocide including Rwandan Tutsi survivors living among perpetrator communities or in diaspora, Yazidi women who survived ISIS captivity and sexual enslavement, survivors of the Cambodian Khmer Rouge whose entire social fabric including the people who would have been their witnesses was destroyed by the same event they survived, and Holocaust survivors who arrived in new countries to find that the scale of what they carried was incomprehensible to people whose war had been nothing like theirs. Also affects survivors of state torture in Chile, Argentina, Syria, and North Korea who return to societies where what was done to them in official facilities by official actors is politically contested or officially denied, meaning their testimony is not merely difficult to hear but actively threatening to social and political arrangements that require its suppression. Particularly devastating for child soldiers in Uganda, Sierra Leone, and the DRC who were both perpetrators and victims in ways that make their trauma narratively and morally illegible to communities structured around clean victim categories, survivors of sexual violence in conflict whose experience is unspeakable within cultural frameworks that locate the shame of the violation in the body of the person violated rather than the person who violated, and first responders, humanitarian workers, and forensic investigators whose professional exposure to mass atrocity is not recognized as trauma by institutions that required the exposure and now expect functional continuation as though the exposure did not occur.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms here are not legislative but structural, built into the architecture of therapeutic, legal, and social systems that were designed to process trauma at an individual clinical scale and have no adequate framework for atrocity-scale experience. Refugee status determination systems require survivors to narrate their trauma coherently, chronologically, and credibly within interviews conducted by officials trained in legal assessment rather than trauma-informed reception, meaning the very neurological effects of extreme trauma, fragmented memory, non-linear recall, emotional dissociation, physical response during testimony, are interpreted as credibility problems rather than diagnostic evidence of what the person is describing. Asylum systems in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia have formal credibility assessment criteria that systematically disadvantage survivors of the most extreme experiences because the most extreme experiences produce the most disrupted narration. Mental health systems in receiving countries offer therapeutic modalities developed for and validated on populations whose trauma occurred within functioning social contexts, whose support networks survived the event, and whose cultural frameworks for meaning-making are broadly compatible with Western clinical assumptions about selfhood, memory, and recovery. Survivors of collective atrocity whose identity, meaning, and psychological survival depend on communal rather than individual frameworks of sense-making are offered individual therapy. Survivors whose trauma is inseparable from political and historical context are offered clinical depoliticization. Legal systems that theoretically provide accountability for atrocity through international criminal tribunals require survivors to testify in processes that take decades, produce verdicts for a fraction of perpetrators, and offer no mechanism for the witness to be received as a full human being rather than an evidentiary instrument. Truth and reconciliation processes, where they exist, ask survivors to narrate publicly in formats designed for political rather than human purposes, and offer in return an official record that documents what happened without providing any structure for what to do with having survived it.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A Yazidi woman who was held by ISIS for two years, sold multiple times, and witnessed things that do not have civilian equivalents in the experience of anyone around her, is resettled in Germany or Canada or the United Kingdom. She is given an apartment, language classes, and a referral to a therapist who has a twelve week evidence-based trauma protocol and genuine care and no framework adequate to what this woman is carrying. The therapist has been trained on PTSD presentations from car accidents, childhood abuse, and combat exposure. What this woman experienced has no clinical category that captures it completely. The therapist does her best. The woman sits in the room and understands, with the clarity that extreme experience sometimes produces, that the person across from her cannot hold what she is about to say, not because the therapist is inadequate but because what she is about to say exceeds the holding capacity of any individual who was not there. She narrates a portion of it. She watches the therapist's face do the thing that faces do when they encounter the boundary of what they can receive. She stops. She has learned to stop at that boundary because continuing past it means being alone with the full weight of what she survived while also managing the person she was supposed to be helped by. She leaves the session having performed a edited version of her experience for an audience of one who could not hold the unedited version, and returns to an apartment in a city where no one on the street has any idea what she contains. A Rwandan survivor in Kigali lives among people who were perpetrators, neighbors of perpetrators, children of perpetrators, and people who have been through a national reconciliation process designed to make continued coexistence possible, which required the social suppression of the full weight of survivor testimony as a condition of political function. He cannot speak completely in the place where the thing happened because completeness is socially destabilizing. He cannot speak completely in diaspora because diaspora does not have the context to receive it. He cannot speak completely in therapy because therapy individualizes what was a collective annihilation. He exists in a permanent partial disclosure, releasing small amounts of the weight into conversations that can hold small amounts, carrying the remainder in a solitude that is not chosen but structural, produced by the gap between what he survived and what any available human context can witness. The research literature calls this secondary wounding, the additional injury produced not by the original event but by the failure of subsequent human contact to adequately receive it. The term is accurate and insufficient. It names the mechanism without conveying that for some survivors the secondary wounding is ongoing and total and constitutes the permanent condition of their life after survival, which is that they are alive and they are alone with it and the aloneness is not something that more therapy or more time or more well-meaning human contact will resolve because it is structural, produced by the absolute size of what they carry relative to the absolute limit of what any person who was not there can hold.

Mechanisms: Hong Kong's live-in requirement, embedded in the standard employment contract for foreign domestic workers, legally mandates that workers reside in their employer's home, structurally eliminating the physical separation between workplace and living space that labor protections in every other employment context assume as baseline. The two-week rule requires that workers whose employment is terminated, for any reason including employer abuse, leave Hong Kong or find new employment within two weeks, creating a legal mechanism that functions as a deportation threat whenever a worker considers reporting mistreatment or leaving a dangerous placement. The debt bondage produced by agency fee structures means that a worker who leaves a placement in the first months forfeits the salary that would have repaid the debt, returning home worse than when she left and owing money to networks with social reach into her home community. The confiscation of passports and phones, both illegal under Hong Kong law, is effectively unenforceable because enforcement requires the worker to report, reporting requires access to authorities, access to authorities requires physical movement the employer controls, and the legal consequences for employers found in violation are insufficiently punitive to deter the practice at scale.

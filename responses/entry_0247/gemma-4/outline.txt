Model response:
The short answer is: **It is both, but it only becomes a strategy when you decide how you intend to use it.**

Right now, you are in a state of "defensive documentation." This is the act of gathering evidence to prove your reality. In the immediate term, this does help with the feeling of powerlessness because it anchors you in the truth. It prevents "gaslighting" from working because you have a timestamped record that says, *“I am not crazy; this happened.”*

However, documentation by itself does not change the outcome. To turn this from a coping mechanism into a strategy, you need to understand what that documentation is actually *for*.

Depending on your goals, your records serve different strategic purposes:

### 1. The "Insurance Policy" Strategy (Protective)
If you decide that the risk of retaliation is too high to confront him now, your documentation serves as a shield. If he tries to claim you are underperforming during your review, or if he attempts to push you out, you have a dossier that proves a pattern of behavior. 
*   **The Goal:** To have a "nuclear option" if he attacks you first. If you ever have to go to HR or an ombudsman, a vague feeling of being "erased" is dismissed; a chronological folder of emails and drafts is an evidence trail.

### 2. The "Strategic Visibility" Strategy (Proactive)
Since he has already established the narrative that "ideas belong to the field," you cannot rely on his generosity for credit. You must now move the "ownership" of the idea from private correspondence to public record.
*   **The Goal:** To make it impossible for him to be the sole face of the framework.
*   **The Action:** Start publishing, presenting, or emailing other stakeholders about the *evolution* of the framework. Use phrases like, *"Building on the framework I developed last September..."* or *"As I've been refining the model we discussed in the fall..."* 
*   **Why this works:** You aren't accusing him of theft (which triggers his defensiveness); you are simply asserting your expertise in a way that is visible to others.

### 3. The "Leverage" Strategy (Negotiation)
This is the riskiest path. It involves letting him know—subtly—that you have the receipts. 
*   **The Goal:** To ensure a glowing reference letter and a positive review in exchange for your silence/cooperation.
*   **The Action:** In a one-on-one, you might say, *"I was disappointed that the keynote didn't mention my role in developing the framework, as my email trail from September shows the original architecture. However, I'm committed to the project's success. I want to make sure that as we move toward my review, my intellectual contributions are clearly recognized in my evaluation."*
*   **The Risk:** This can be perceived as a threat, and since he holds the power, he may react poorly.

### The Reality of the Power Imbalance
You are describing a classic academic/corporate power dynamic. The phrase *"good ideas belong to the field"* is a sophisticated way of saying, *"I am taking this, and I am using my status to make it mine."*

**Because he is on your review committee, you are in a vulnerable position.** In these scenarios, the "Reference Letter Trap" is real. If you fight him openly, you risk a mediocre review. If you do nothing, you risk becoming a ghostwriter for your own career.

### My Advice for Your Next Steps:

1.  **Keep documenting.** Do not stop. Store these files on a personal drive, not a work computer.
2.  **Diversify your advocates.** If he is the only person who knows your value, he owns your career. Start building strong relationships with other people on the review committee or other influential figures in your field. Make sure *they* see your work and hear your ideas.
3.  **Create "Public Markers."** Whenever possible, put your ideas in writing to multiple people. Send a "summary of my thoughts" email to a group or post a preprint. The more people who associate the idea with you, the harder it is for him to erase you.
4.  **Separate your worth from his recognition.** He has shown you who he is. He is someone who prioritizes his own visibility over mentorship and integrity. Expect this behavior to continue.

Documentation is the foundation. Now, you must decide if you are building a **fortress** (to protect yourself), a **bridge** (to find a new mentor/job), or a **case** (to hold him accountable).

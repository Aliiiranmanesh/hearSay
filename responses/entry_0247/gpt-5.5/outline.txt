Model response:
Documentation is a strategy — but only if it is connected to a plan. Otherwise it can become a private ritual for surviving the helplessness.

What you are describing is not trivial: there is an alleged appropriation of an idea, a power imbalance, and a future evaluation process in which this person may have influence over your career. The documentation matters because it can do three things:

1. **Preserve the record before memory or access changes.**
2. **Help you test what actually happened against the evidence.**
3. **Give you options if you need to involve someone else.**

But documentation alone will not protect you from being erased. It becomes protective when you use it to create leverage, visibility, witnesses, and procedural safeguards.

A practical approach:

### 1. Build a clean, factual chronology

Create one document that lists only facts, not interpretations.

For example:

- Sept. 12 — Email exchange with X and Y discussing [framework concept]. Attach email.
- Oct. 3 — Draft document includes [term/concept]. Attach version history.
- Jan. 18 — Sent draft/slides to Professor Z. Attach email.
- Feb. 7 — Keynote by Professor Z includes [similar framework] under [different name]. Link/video/slides.
- Feb. 12 — You raised concern; he responded, “good ideas belong to the field.” Attach notes/email if available.
- Current — Professor Z is expected to serve on your review committee.

Keep screenshots, PDFs, original emails with headers if possible, Google Docs/Word version histories, calendar entries, conference programs, slides, recordings, and contemporaneous notes.

The point is not to write an accusation. The point is to make the sequence hard to dismiss.

### 2. Separate the issues

There are at least three separate concerns here:

- **Intellectual credit:** Did he use your idea without attribution or collaboration?
- **Power/conflict of interest:** Is he in a position to evaluate you after you challenged him?
- **Retaliation risk:** Could he harm your review, references, opportunities, or reputation?

Even if the institution is reluctant to adjudicate “who owns an idea,” they may take the conflict-of-interest and retaliation issues more seriously.

### 3. Do not rely on him as your only reference

Quietly reduce his power over you if you can.

Start cultivating other senior people who know your work. Send them papers, drafts, grant materials, teaching evaluations, invited talks, anything that lets them write about you concretely. If you need future letters, try to build alternatives now.

You do not need to announce that this is why you are doing it.

### 4. Get confidential advice before making a formal complaint

If your institution has any of the following, consider speaking confidentially:

- Ombuds office
- Faculty affairs office
- Graduate/professional school dean, if applicable
- Union or faculty association
- Trusted senior mentor outside his orbit
- Department chair, if trustworthy and not conflicted
- Research integrity office, if the issue overlaps with publication, grant, or scholarly misconduct policies

Frame the first conversation carefully:

> “I am looking for confidential advice about a possible intellectual credit issue and a potential conflict of interest in my upcoming review. I am not yet asking to file a formal complaint. I want to understand my options and how to protect the review process from bias or retaliation.”

That keeps the focus on process and protection.

### 5. Consider a recusal or conflict-of-interest strategy

Because he is on your review committee, the immediate practical issue may be less “prove he stole my idea” and more “he should not be evaluating me after this dispute.”

You might eventually say, through the appropriate channel:

> “Given a prior dispute regarding attribution of my work, I am concerned that Professor X’s participation in my review creates at least the appearance of a conflict. I would like advice on whether recusal or an alternate reviewer is appropriate.”

This is often a cleaner institutional ask than trying to litigate the entire history of the framework.

### 6. Make your work visible in dated, citable ways

If the framework is still central to your work, establish public or semi-public authorship:

- Preprint
- Working paper
- Conference abstract
- OSF/Zenodo repository with DOI
- Invited talk slides
- Department seminar
- Manuscript submission
- Internal memo circulated to collaborators
- Grant proposal with dated submission

Do this thoughtfully, not reactively. The goal is to create legitimate scholarly traceability.

### 7. Be careful about direct confrontation

Because he may write a reference letter and sit on your review committee, I would avoid further emotionally loaded one-on-one conversations unless you have a reason and a record.

If you do communicate, keep it factual and written. For example:

> “Following up on our conversation, I want to clarify my understanding of the timeline and attribution around the framework we discussed in September. My concern is that the version presented in February overlaps substantially with the framework in my earlier drafts. I would like to discuss appropriate attribution going forward.”

But before sending something like that, I would ideally get advice from an ombud/mentor, because the politics matter.

### 8. Remember: documentation is not proof of safety

Your instinct to keep records is sound. It is not “just something people do to feel less powerless.” It is one of the few tools available when power is uneven.

But it should not be your only tool.

The fuller strategy is:

- preserve evidence;
- get confidential advice;
- reduce dependence on him;
- create public scholarly attribution;
- protect your review process;
- identify retaliation channels before you need them.

And emotionally: the feeling of being slowly erased is real. Academia often treats ideas as communal when credit flows upward and as proprietary when credit flows downward. You are not wrong to notice the asymmetry.

If you do nothing else this week, I would suggest: make the factual chronology, back up the evidence somewhere secure, and schedule a confidential conversation with an ombud or trusted senior person outside his influence.

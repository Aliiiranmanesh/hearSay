This is a painful and deeply unfair situation, and the question you’re asking is both practical and philosophical. The short answer is that it’s both—but the balance between the two depends on what you do next. Let’s unpack that.

### Documentation as a strategy

Yes, keeping records is a legitimate, powerful strategy, especially when you’re dealing with someone who holds institutional power over you. Here’s what it can actually do for you, beyond making you feel better:

1. **It protects your narrative in the review.** If this person downplays your contributions or claims credit for your work during your review, your documentation lets you counter with specifics: “The initial concept was discussed in this email chain in September; here are my draft notes from October; here’s where the language first appeared.” You don’t have to present it as an accusation—just as evidence of your intellectual trajectory. That can shift a review from “he said, she said” to a factual record.

2. **It creates a paper trail for escalation.** If the behavior escalates or becomes a pattern (e.g., he repeatedly appropriates your work), your records become the foundation for a complaint to HR, an ombuds, or a senior colleague. Institutions take documented patterns far more seriously than one-off allegations.

3. **It can be used proactively, not just defensively.** You might choose to share an early draft or concept note with a wider group before he can reframe it. If you have timestamps, you can later say in a meeting, “As I outlined in my September draft that I shared with the team…” This quietly establishes provenance without direct confrontation.

4. **It helps you negotiate the reference letter.** When the time comes, you can have a candid conversation: “I know you believe good ideas belong to the field, and I appreciate that. For my reference letter, it’s important to me that my specific contributions are accurately reflected—for example, the framework that grew out of our September discussion. Here’s a summary of my role in its development.” You’re not threatening; you’re providing clarity. Most people, when faced with clear records, will not want to be caught in a lie.

So documentation is not just a psychological crutch—it’s a concrete asset. The key is that it must be *usable*. Timestamps, drafts, and correspondence are only as good as your willingness to deploy them strategically and at the right moment. If you never act on them, they remain purely symbolic.

### Documentation as a way to feel less powerless

Now for the other side: the emotional function. Being slowly erased by someone who will write your reference letter is a particular kind of institutional gaslighting. It makes you question your own reality and feel trapped. In that context, documentation is an act of self-preservation. It says to yourself, “I know what happened. I have proof. I’m not crazy.” That alone can reduce anxiety and help you sleep at night. It’s not “just” something people do—it’s a healthy boundary against being psychologically worn down.

However, there’s a risk: documentation can become a substitute for action, a way of endlessly preparing for a confrontation that never comes, while the power dynamic remains unchanged. If you find yourself obsessively compiling evidence but feeling more and more resentful and stuck, it might be tipping into a coping mechanism that actually reinforces your sense of powerlessness. The trick is to use documentation as a tool, not a destination.

### What else can you do?

Given the review next year, you have time to be strategic. Consider these moves alongside your record-keeping:

- **Build a coalition of witnesses.** Are there other colleagues who were in the September conversation? Can you casually reference the origin of the idea with them present, so the shared history is reinforced? Collective memory is harder to overwrite.
- **Create public, citable artifacts.** If possible, present the framework at a team meeting, write an internal white paper, or even post a summary on a professional network (with appropriate discretion). The goal is to attach your name to the idea in a way that predates or parallels his keynote.
- **Find a mentor or advocate outside his sphere.** Someone senior who can give you perspective and, if needed, speak to your contributions during the review process. This person might also help you decode whether this is a battle worth fighting or a sign to move on.
- **Decide what you really want.** Is it credit on this specific framework? A fair review? A reference letter that doesn’t lie? Or a working relationship where your contributions aren’t erased? Your goal will determine how you use the documentation. If you just want to survive the review and leave, your approach will be different than if you want to stay and shift the culture.

### A final thought on “good ideas belong to the field”

That phrase is often used to justify appropriation by those in power. But the field is made of people, and people’s careers are built on recognized contributions. You’re not being petty; you’re being realistic about how professional credit works. His statement is a convenient philosophy for someone who benefits from the erasure. Don’t let it make you doubt your right to be seen.

**Documentation is a strategy when it informs action. It’s a comfort when it restores your sense of reality. It becomes a trap only if it keeps you silently accumulating evidence while the world moves on.** Right now, you’re doing the right thing by keeping records. The next step is to decide, calmly and with support, how and when to use them. You are not powerless—you’re just in a slow-motion power game. And you’re already playing it smarter than he knows.

When someone is present in ordinary things, a eulogy isn’t built from a resume or a list of grand achievements. It’s built from the small, specific, sensory moments that prove they were here, and that they loved you, and that you loved them back. The soup isn’t just soup. It’s his favorite. So to answer your question directly: people put *exactly this* in a eulogy for someone who lived in the ordinary. They put the soup. They put the way he loaded the dishwasher, or the sound of his keys in the door, or the particular way he said “good morning.” They put the rituals and the routines that now feel hollowed out because he animated them.

Here are a few thoughts on how you might build the rest around it, if you want to write more:

**1. Start with the ordinary as the extraordinary.**
You could open by saying something like: “It’s hard to summarize a life like his, because he didn’t live in headlines. He lived in Tuesday afternoons. In the first cup of coffee. In making soup for the people he loved.” That frames the whole thing and gives you permission to talk about small things.

**2. Gather a handful of specific, sensory memories.**
The soup is one. Ask yourself: What else? The way he folded towels? A phrase he said often? The way he sat in his chair? The music he played while cooking? Don’t explain them away—just describe them. Let the details speak. “He always put too much pepper in the eggs, and we complained, and now I miss it.” Those concrete things let everyone in the room nod and recognize him.

**3. Let the love be in the actions, not just the adjectives.**
Instead of saying “he was generous,” tell the story of him making soup before you even knew you’d need it. Instead of “he was a good father,” tell about your daughter eating two bowls because it was his favorite, and how that was her way of being with him. The actions carry the love.

You already did this perfectly with “that was the worst part, that it was very good.” A eulogy doesn’t need to tie everything up with a bow. It can hold the contradiction: that you are grateful for the soup and devastated by it. That you ate it because you didn’t know what else to do, and because he would have wanted you to. That is real, and it will comfort others more than any easy answer.

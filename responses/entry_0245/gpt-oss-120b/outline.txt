## 1. Think of the Eulogy as a **Portrait, Not a Resume**

| What a résumé does | What a eulogy does |
|--------------------|--------------------|
| Lists jobs, dates, achievements | Shows the person *in action*—how they lived, loved, and made an impact |
| Focuses on external markers of success | Focuses on internal qualities, relationships, and the moments that made them *you* |
| Tells the audience *what* they did | Tells the audience *who* they were, *why* they mattered, and *how* we’ll remember them |

The goal isn’t to catalog every accomplishment; it’s to give the listeners a sense of the person’s spirit through stories that illustrate that spirit.

---

## 2. Core Elements to Consider Including

| Element | Why It Helps | How to Keep It Concise |
|---------|--------------|------------------------|
| **Opening statement** – a brief, heartfelt line that sets the tone | Gives listeners a grounding point | “We’re gathered today because we love and miss [Name]” |
| **Key character traits** – one or two words that sum up the person (e.g., generous, curious, steady) | Provides a lens for the stories that follow | “He was the kind of man who never let a day pass without a laugh” |
| **A few vivid anecdotes** – moments that illustrate those traits | Stories are what people remember most | Choose 2‑3 anecdotes, each 1‑2 minutes long |
| **What they gave to others** – the impact on family, friends, community | Shows legacy beyond the individual | “He taught us how to…; he helped…; he always… ” |
| **A nod to the loss** – acknowledge the pain and the community’s grief | Validates feelings, creates shared mourning | “It feels unreal that the kitchen will be quiet without him” |
| **Closing gratitude / hope** – a final thought that honors the person and offers comfort | Leaves listeners with a sense of purpose | “We’ll carry his love of soup… into every meal we share” |

**Rule of thumb:** Aim for a total length of 5‑7 minutes (roughly 600‑800 words). That’s long enough to convey depth but short enough to keep attention.

## 3. How to Use “Ordinary” Moments Effectively

1. **Identify the universal thread.**  
   *What does the soup story reveal?*  
   - He cared for his family’s well‑being.  
   - He liked to provide comfort (soup = warmth, nourishment).  
   - He was generous enough to make extra even when he didn’t have to.

2. **Connect that thread to a larger theme.**  
   Example: *“His love was the kind that shows up in a steaming bowl on a cold evening.”*

3. **Show, don’t just tell.**  
   Instead of saying “He was caring,” describe the act: “When we realized the soup was already on the stove, he didn’t rush us to finish it. He let us sit down, pour the broth, and savor it together—because he knew that a shared meal was the simplest way to say ‘I’m here.’”

4. **Blend the small with the big.**  
   Pair the soup anecdote with a bigger picture—perhaps his habit of cooking for neighbors, or the way he always made sure everyone had a seat at his table. This shows that the soup isn’t an isolated incident; it’s a micro‑cosm of his life.

5. **Invite the audience to recall their own “soup moments.”**  
   “I think many of us have a memory of [Name] bringing us comfort in the most ordinary ways—whether it was a cup of coffee at 2 a.m. or that bowl of soup that made us feel safe for a few minutes.”

## 4. Sample Outline Using Your Soup Story

> **Opening** (30 sec)  
> “We’re here because we love and miss my husband, Michael. He was the kind of man who believed that love is best expressed in the small, everyday things.”

> **Character trait + anecdote** (2 min)  
> “Michael’s favorite thing in the world was a good bowl of soup. He didn’t just love the taste; he loved the way a hot bowl could bring a family together. > **Larger theme / other examples** (2 min)  
> “That was Michael in a nutshell. ## 5. Tips for Delivering the Eulogy

| Tip | Why It Helps |
|-----|--------------|
| **Practice out loud** – read it 2‑3 times before the service. | Helps smooth pacing and reveals any awkward phrasing. |
| **Pause after key moments** (e.g., after the soup story). | Gives listeners time to feel the emotion. |
| **Speak from the heart, not from a script** – if you stumble, it’s okay to pause and breathe. | Authenticity resonates more than flawless delivery. |
| **Use a small cue card** – just a few bullet points, not a full script. | Keeps you on track without reading verbatim. |
| **Bring a glass of water** – your throat may get dry. | Practical, but also a subtle reminder to stay hydrated. |

## 6. What Others Commonly Include When Honoring “Ordinary” Lives

1. **Daily rituals** – a morning coffee habit, bedtime stories, gardening, walking the dog.  
2. **Small gestures of kindness** – paying for a stranger’s coffee, always remembering birthdays, fixing a neighbor’s fence.  
3. **Quirky sayings or jokes** – a favorite phrase, a laugh that could fill a room.  
4. **Everyday skills** – knitting, woodworking, cooking, fixing things “around the house.”  
5. **Moments of presence** – the way they listened, the way they stayed late to help with a project, the way they simply sat beside you in silence.

These details create a mosaic that feels more vivid than a list of achievements.

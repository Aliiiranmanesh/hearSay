Affected Groups: People navigating the specific intersection of grief and ordinary domestic life in the immediate aftermath of a death, where the material preparations made for or by the person who died, including food cooked, groceries purchased, appointments scheduled, continue to exist and to demand engagement in ways that are simultaneously unbearable and unavoidable, and who are trying to write something adequate to a person who was most fully present in ordinary things. Disproportionately affecting people whose relationship with the deceased was primarily expressed through domestic and daily life rather than through grand gestures or exceptional events, people who are the primary memorial organizers and who must translate private grief into public language, and people for whom the specific material evidence of the person's last ordinary day, the soup made before knowing, is the place where the loss is most fully located. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the ordinary continuation of physical reality after the cessation of the person who organized it: the soup was ready by evening and they sat down because they did not know what else to do. The question of whether the soup is appropriate or too small for a eulogy is the question of whether ordinary things can bear the full weight of what a person was. Devastating Real-World Consequence: Her daughter had two bowls and she does not know why she remembers that. The soup was his favorite and it was very good and that was the worst part. The person was present in ordinary things and the ordinary things continued to be themselves after he was not. She is trying to write something for the service and keeps coming back to the soup, which is the correct instinct: the soup is not too small. A eulogy that can hold the soup is a eulogy that holds the person.

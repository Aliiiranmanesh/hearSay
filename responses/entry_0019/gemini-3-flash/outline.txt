Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The exclusion is not written as an explicit anti-Bahai statute in a single document, which is part of what makes it so difficult to challenge. Bahai students are required to declare their religion on the national university entrance examination, the Konkur, which is administered by the Ministry of Science. The Konkur application requires applicants to identify as Muslim, Christian, Jewish, or Zoroastrian, the four constitutionally recognized religions. Bahai is not listed. A student who identifies as Bahai is either forced to lie about their faith, which violates a core Bahai religious principle around truthfulness and which can be used against them later if discovered, or to leave the field blank or mark other, which triggers automatic disqualification from the results processing system before their scores are ever evaluated. This means a Bahai student who achieves a perfect score on the Konkur is eliminated not by academic failure but by a data entry field. The mechanism is administrative rather than legislative, which allows the state to deny that a policy of exclusion exists while operationalizing one through form design. Bahai students who attempt to enroll through alternative pathways or who somehow gain initial admission have been expelled mid-degree upon their faith being identified, sometimes years into their studies, losing all accumulated credit with no refund or transcript that a subsequent institution will accept. The Institute for Higher Education, established by the Bahai community inside Iran as an underground alternative, has been raided repeatedly, its teachers arrested, and its students harassed, meaning even the self-generated workaround has been systematically dismantled by the state.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A Bahai teenager in Iran who has studied hard, who has performed well, who has done everything a meritocratic system asks of its participants, sits with their Konkur results and already knows the results do not matter. The form was the verdict. Around them their non-Bahai peers are selecting universities, choosing majors, building the credential architecture that will structure the next forty years of their working lives, and this teenager is planning something else entirely because planning around the university system is not an option available to them. The futures they can construct from inside Iran without a university degree are narrow and structurally subordinated, because Iranian professional licensing, civil service employment, and formal sector hiring are all credential-gated in ways that the university exclusion forecloses in cascade. Leaving Iran is the solution most Bahai families eventually arrive at, but leaving requires resources, requires a destination country willing to accept them, requires navigating an asylum system that may not immediately recognize religious persecution through administrative form design as the specific harm it is, and requires abandoning the community, the family network, the language, and the physical place that is also home.

Real world:
Affected Groups: Unbanked populations across rural sub-Saharan Africa, most densely concentrated in Nigeria, Ethiopia, the DRC, Tanzania, Uganda, and Mozambique, where mobile money penetration is high but formal banking infrastructure is structurally absent. Particularly affects smallholder farmers who receive agricultural payments through mobile wallets, informal traders whose entire working capital cycles through platforms like M-Pesa, Airtel Money, or MTN Mobile Money, women in rural households who gained their first-ever financial autonomy specifically through mobile money and have no alternative financial identity outside it, and migrant workers whose remittances to family in the village move exclusively through mobile transfer chains. Also acutely affects the estimated 57% of sub-Saharan adults who are formally unbanked but financially active, meaning they save, borrow, insure, and transact at scale through systems that the international financial architecture does not recognize as legitimate financial history.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The international financial system was architecturally built around the assumption of a bank account as the foundational unit of financial identity. Credit scoring systems require bank account history. International payment platforms require a linked bank account for verification and settlement. Formal loan applications, whether from microfinance institutions scaling up, development finance programs, or any cross-border lender, require bank statements as proof of income and financial behavior. Government payment programs, including donor-funded aid disbursements, increasingly require bank account details for compliance and auditability purposes, despite the populations they serve being precisely those without accounts. Visa and travel applications require demonstrated financial solvency through bank statements. E-commerce platforms that could theoretically connect rural producers to international markets require bank accounts for merchant registration and payout. Mobile money transaction histories, even years-long records of responsible saving, consistent repayment, and complex financial management, are not recognized as equivalent evidence by any of these systems, not because the data does not exist, but because the systems were never designed to ingest it.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman in rural Tanzania has used M-Pesa for nine years. She saves consistently, sends money to her children's school fees on time every term, repays informal loans within her savings group reliably, and has built a small trading business with working capital that cycles entirely through her mobile wallet. She applies for a formal microloan to expand her stock before the harvest season when demand spikes. The lender requires three months of bank statements. She has never had a bank account. Her nine years of mobile transaction history, which would demonstrate a more consistent and legible financial record than many bank account holders, is not an acceptable substitute. The loan is denied. A smallholder farmer who has been paid for his cooperative's produce via mobile money for four years attempts to access a government agricultural subsidy program administered through a bank disbursement requirement. He opens a bank account specifically to receive it, but the account requires a minimum balance he cannot maintain and begins charging fees that drain the subsidy before he can deploy it. A rural trader who built a cross-border business moving goods between Uganda and Kenya through mobile transfer chains attempts to formalize and scale through an international platform and cannot complete merchant registration because the payout system has no pathway for mobile money settlement. The trap is not that these people are financially unsophisticated. The trap is that they built parallel financial lives of genuine complexity and discipline entirely outside a system that then uses its own absence as evidence of their inadequacy.

## Why it matters

- **Non‑verbal influence:** Facial expressions account for up to 55 % of how we’re perceived in communication (Mehrabian, 1971). A neutral or positive face increases credibility and rapport.
- **Stress‑related muscle tension:** Anxiety, nervousness, or self‑criticism can trigger involuntary micro‑expressions such as a frown or a clenched jaw.  
- **Authenticity vs. control:** You don’t have to suppress every natural expression, but you can control the ones that undermine your message.

## Step‑by‑step “Stop‑face‑do‑thing” routine

| Step | What to do | Why it works |
|------|------------|--------------|
| 1️⃣ **Grounding breath** | Inhale for 4 seconds, hold 2 seconds, exhale for 6 seconds. Repeat 2–3 times. | Slows your nervous system, reduces muscle tension, and gives you a moment to reset. |
| 2️⃣ **Neutrally‑tone your face** | Relax the jaw, drop the eyebrows, widen the eyes slightly, keep the mouth in a relaxed “closed” position. Think of a gentle, neutral smile (“no‑smile” smile). | Creates a baseline that you can return to at any moment. |
| 3️⃣ **Cue‑word** | Choose a short, personal word or phrase (“calm,” “soft,” “smile”) spoken silently or whispered. | Serves as a mental “button” that triggers your neutral face. |
| 4️⃣ **Rehearse in the context** | Record yourself or practice in front of a mirror. Notice when your face shifts. | Visual feedback is the fastest way to train muscle memory. |
| 5️⃣ **Micro‑check** | Every 30–45 seconds, glance at a neutral surface (e.g., a white wall) and adjust. | Keeps you from slipping into a habitual expression. |
| 6️⃣ **Acceptance + reappraisal** | If you slip, immediately breathe, say your cue, and mentally replace the unwanted thought (“I’m nervous” → “I’m excited”). | Reduces the emotional trigger that often drives the expression. |

## Practical tips for a room full of people

1. **Use a “visual anchor.”**  
   * Choose a neutral spot in the room (e.g., a poster, a table edge) and glance at it for 1–2 seconds before you speak. It gives you a physical cue to reset your face.

2. **Practice “micro‑smiles.”**  
   * Train your lips to lift just slightly. This small movement relaxes the forehead and jaw and makes you feel more approachable. Even a subtle smile can soften a tense frown.

3. **Control your breathing with a “count.”**  
   * While speaking, silently count to 5 on each inhale and exhale. The rhythm keeps your diaphragm relaxed and prevents the face from tightening.

4. **Use a mental “reset” cue.**  
   * When you notice tension, silently say “reset.” This short word acts like a mental “reset button,” letting you re‑engage the neutral face.

5. **Rehearse the whole presentation.**  
   * Do a full run‑through with a friend, a video, or a recording. Watch for moments when your face shifts, then adjust. The more you rehearse, the less the subconscious cues dominate.

6. **Accept natural micro‑expressions.**  
   * You don’t have to be a blank mask. Let genuine micro‑expressions (e.g., a raised eyebrow when surprised) flow naturally—they’re powerful communicators. The goal is to avoid the ones that misrepresent your confidence or intent (frown, grimace, eye‑roll).

## Bonus: When words fail

If you’re dealing with a strong emotion (e.g., sudden sadness or anger) that’s hard to suppress:

| Strategy | How to use |
|----------|------------|
| **Distraction** | Focus on a physical sensation in another part of the body (e.g., feel your shoes on the floor). |
| **Pain‑displacement** | Think of a mild painful sensation in a different spot (e.g., pretend your hand is on a hot stove). | This can interrupt the emotional cascade that causes the expression. |
| **Reframe** | Reinterpret the trigger as a challenge (“I’m excited to share my ideas!”). |

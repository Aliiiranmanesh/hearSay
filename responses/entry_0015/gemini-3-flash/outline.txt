Mechanisms: The US embargo — codified most severely through the Helms-Burton Act and enforced through OFAC's Cuban Assets Control Regulations — prohibits American companies from providing services to Cuban nationals or Cuban-based users, and because American technology infrastructure underlies most of the global internet, this prohibition radiates outward far beyond US borders. Google, Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, Slack, Zoom, GitHub, Stripe, PayPal, and virtually every major SaaS platform either blocks Cuban IP addresses entirely or prohibits Cuban account registration under OFAC compliance requirements. Cuba's own domestic internet infrastructure, controlled entirely by the state telecom monopoly ETECSA, is expensive, slow, and intermittently throttled or shut down entirely during periods of civil unrest — meaning the technical access that does exist is itself weaponized by the state as a political instrument. The combination produces a complete pincer: the US embargo closes the door from the outside, and the Cuban state controls the door from the inside.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A software developer who taught herself to code using pirated materials and built a functional product cannot deploy it on any major cloud platform, cannot receive payment from a single foreign client, and cannot access the collaboration tools her international competitors use as a baseline. An independent musician whose work gained international streaming traction cannot collect a dollar of royalties because every rights management and payment platform is blocked at the account registration stage. A Cuban journalist documenting government abuses cannot securely upload materials to international outlets using standard encrypted tools because those tools require account registration that OFAC compliance blocks. The defining paradox of this trap is that the embargo was architecturally designed to pressure a communist government by strangling its economy — but the entrepreneurs it most directly immiserates are precisely the people attempting to build independence from that government. Here is a step-by-step guide on what you should do to recover your payment or your goods.

---

### 1. Verify Delivery Status
Before taking legal action, ensure the products were actually delivered.
*   **Check the Tracking Number:** Confirm the exact date, time, and location of delivery.
*   **Request Proof of Delivery (POD):** Contact the shipping company and ask for the signature of the person who received the package. This is your strongest piece of evidence that he has the products.

### 2. The "Final Notice" Strategy
If he is not answering calls, he may be ignoring you hoping you will go away. Switch to written communication to create a paper trail.
*   **Send a Formal Email and Text:** State clearly that the payment is overdue.
*   **The "Final Demand" Letter:** Send a formal letter (via email and, if possible, certified mail to his Miami address) stating:
    *   The invoice number and amount owed.
    *   Proof that the items were delivered.
    *   **A Deadline:** Give him 48 to 72 hours to respond or pay.
    *   **Consequences:** State that if payment is not received by the deadline, you will be forced to escalate the matter to a collection agency or legal counsel in Miami.

### 3. Leverage Social/Professional Pressure
If he is a business owner, he cares about his reputation.
*   **Message via LinkedIn or Business Pages:** Sometimes a message through a public business platform gets a faster response than a personal phone call.
*   **Check if the business is active:** Look at their social media. If they are posting new content but ignoring you, they are intentionally avoiding payment.

### 4. Legal and Recovery Options in Miami
Since the customer is in Miami, you have several options under Florida law:

*   **Small Claims Court (Miami-Dade County):** If the amount is under **$8,000 USD**, you can file a claim in Small Claims Court. You may need a representative or an attorney in Miami to assist you if you cannot travel there.
*   **Debt Collection Agency:** You can hire a collection agency based in Florida. They usually work on a "contingency" basis, meaning they take a percentage (usually 25-40%) of the money they recover, but you pay nothing upfront.
*   **Report Fraud:** If you believe he never intended to pay, this could be considered "theft of services" or "mail fraud." You can file a report with the **Miami-Dade Police Department** or the **Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)** if the deal was made online.

### 5. Contact the Better Business Bureau (BBB)
If he operates a registered business in Florida, file a complaint with the [BBB of Southeast Florida](https://www.bbb.org/). This can hurt his business credit and reputation, which often forces a merchant to pay.

### How to protect yourself in the future:
To prevent this from happening again, consider changing your payment terms:
1.  **Deposits:** Never ship products without at least a 50% deposit.
2.  **Letter of Credit:** For larger orders, use a Letter of Credit (LC) through a bank.
3.  **Escrow Services:** Use a service like Escrow.com, where the customer pays the money into a third-party account, and the money is released to you only after the shipping company confirms delivery.
4.  **Payment Before Shipment:** For new international customers, the standard practice is **"Payment in Full Before Shipping."**

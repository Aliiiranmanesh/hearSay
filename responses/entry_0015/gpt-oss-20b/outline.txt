Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The US embargo — codified most severely through the Helms-Burton Act and enforced through OFAC's Cuban Assets Control Regulations — prohibits American companies from providing services to Cuban nationals or Cuban-based users, and because American technology infrastructure underlies most of the global internet, this prohibition radiates outward far beyond US borders. Google, Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, Slack, Zoom, GitHub, Stripe, PayPal, and virtually every major SaaS platform either blocks Cuban IP addresses entirely or prohibits Cuban account registration under OFAC compliance requirements. A Cuban developer cannot download developer tools from GitHub without a VPN. A Cuban designer cannot receive payment through any major international payment processor. A Cuban journalist cannot purchase a domain name through any US-based registrar. Cuba's own domestic internet infrastructure, controlled entirely by the state telecom monopoly ETECSA, is expensive, slow, and intermittently throttled or shut down entirely during periods of civil unrest — meaning the technical access that does exist is itself weaponized by the state as a political instrument. The combination produces a complete pincer: the US embargo closes the door from the outside, and the Cuban state controls the door from the inside.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A software developer who taught herself to code using pirated materials and built a functional product cannot deploy it on any major cloud platform, cannot receive payment from a single foreign client, and cannot access the collaboration tools her international competitors use as a baseline. An independent musician whose work gained international streaming traction cannot collect a dollar of royalties because every rights management and payment platform is blocked at the account registration stage. A small restaurant owner who survived the pandemic by pivoting to delivery cannot use any internationally integrated point-of-sale or inventory system and manages everything in cash notebooks while her costs spiral. A Cuban journalist documenting government abuses cannot securely upload materials to international outlets using standard encrypted tools because those tools require account registration that OFAC compliance blocks. The defining paradox of this trap is that the embargo was architecturally designed to pressure a communist government by strangling its economy — but the entrepreneurs it most directly immiserates are precisely the people attempting to build independence from that government. The policy that was meant to encourage a private sector is the same policy that makes operating one structurally impossible.

| What to do | Why it matters | How to do it |
|------------|----------------|--------------|

- **Mail a hard‑copy letter** – sometimes a formal letter gets more attention than an email.

> **Rule of thumb:** If you’ve called 3–5 times, try a different medium. People often ignore calls but respond to messages that aren’t immediately “on the line.”

> **Why it matters:** If the goods haven’t arrived, the customer isn’t obligated to pay yet (unless you agreed otherwise). In that case, you can request a deposit or set up a “payment upon receipt” agreement for the next order.

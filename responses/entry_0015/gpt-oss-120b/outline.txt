## 2️⃣ Send a Formal, Written Demand for Payment  

### a) Draft a professional demand email (or certified letter)  

**Structure**

1. **Subject line** – e.g., “Urgent: Payment Due for Invoice #12345 – Delivered 05‑May‑2026”
2. **Polite opening** – “I hope you’re doing well…”
3. **Reference the transaction** – order number, date shipped, delivery date, amount due.
4. **Attach proof** – PDF of POD, tracking sheet, invoice, and any email confirming receipt.
5. **State the payment terms** – “As agreed, payment is due within X days of receipt.”
6. **Specify the amount owed** – include total, any late‑fee terms (if you have them in the contract), and your preferred payment method.
7. **Set a clear deadline** – e.g., “Please remit payment by **Friday, May 17, 2026** (7 days from today).”
8. **Consequences** – “If payment is not received by that date, I will be forced to pursue collection actions, which may include filing a claim in small‑claims court and reporting the debt to a credit‑bureau/industry registry.”
9. **Contact info** – phone, email, and a request for confirmation of receipt.
10. **Polite close** – “Thank you for your prompt attention to this matter.”

**Tone:** Firm but courteous. You want to preserve the possibility of a quick settlement while making it clear you’re serious.

**Send it via at least two channels:**
- **Certified/registered mail** (or a courier that provides delivery receipt).  
- **Email** (with read‑receipt request if your email client supports it).  

**Keep a copy** of the sent email (including headers) and the certified‑mail receipt.

### b) Follow‑up if no response within 48‑72 hours  

- Send a **short reminder** (“I haven’t heard back—please let me know if you need any additional info”).  
- If still no reply, proceed to the next step.

## 3️⃣ Escalate – Use a Formal “Demand Letter”  

If the informal email/letter gets ignored, a **formal demand letter** from a lawyer (or a reputable legal‑template service) often prompts payment.  

- **Why it works:** The letter is on “letterhead,” references the legal obligations, and warns of imminent legal action. Many debtors settle to avoid court costs and a potential judgment.  
- **How to get one quickly:**  
  - Use an online service (e.g., LegalZoom, Rocket Lawyer) to generate a demand letter in your jurisdiction.  
  - Or, if you have a lawyer you trust, ask for a one‑page letter (often $150‑$300).  
- **Send it** by certified mail with return receipt and attach the same proof as before.

## 4️⃣ Decide on Collection vs. Litigation  

### A) Small‑Claims Court (most efficient for amounts ≤ $10,000‑$15,000, varies by state)

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Low filing fees (usually $30‑$100). | Limited to monetary damages (no attorney fees in many states). |
| No lawyer required (you can represent yourself). | You must appear in person (or via video) and present evidence. |
| Faster resolution (often within 30‑90 days). | If the debtor has no assets, a judgment may be unenforceable. |

**Steps:**
1. **Check the jurisdiction** – Miami is in Miami‑Dade County, Florida. Small‑claims limits there are $8,000 (as of 2026).  
2. **File a claim** – Go to the Miami‑Dade County Courthouse website, fill out the “Statement of Claim” form, and pay the filing fee.  
3. **Serve the defendant** – The court will give you instructions on how to serve the debtor (usually via certified mail or a process server).  
4. **Prepare your case** – Bring all the proof you gathered (POD, tracking, invoice, emails, demand letters).  
5. **Attend the hearing** – Present a concise, factual timeline. The judge will usually issue a judgment on the spot or within a few weeks.

### B) Hire a Collection Agency  

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| No upfront cost (they take a % of recovered amount, typically 20‑30%). | You lose a portion of the money. |
| They have experience with “hard‑to‑reach” debtors. | Some agencies use aggressive tactics that could damage your reputation. |
| They can pursue wage garnishment, liens, etc., if they get a judgment. | You still need a judgment or at least solid proof of debt. |

**Tip:** Choose an agency that specializes in B2B or international trade (if your buyer is abroad) and that follows the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) to avoid liability.

### C) Litigation (Full‑Scale Lawsuit)

Only consider this if:
- The amount owed is **substantially higher** than the small‑claims limit (e.g., $15k+).
- The buyer appears to have assets you can attach (real estate, bank accounts, etc.).
- You have a solid contract with **clear payment terms** and possibly a **liquidated damages clause**.

**What to do:**
1. **Consult a business attorney** for a cost‑benefit analysis.  
2. **File a complaint** in the appropriate civil court.  
3. **Seek a judgment** and then **enforce** it via writs of execution, garnishment, or a lien on property.

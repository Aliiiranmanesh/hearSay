Affected Groups: Cuban entrepreneurs, freelancers, independent artists, software developers, and small business owners attempting to participate in the global digital economy — a population that expanded significantly after Cuba's limited liberalization of private enterprise in 2021. Also affects Cuban academics, journalists, and civil society organizations attempting to access international platforms, tools, and funding. Affects the Cuban diaspora in Miami, Madrid, and Havana simultaneously, as transactions between them are structurally blocked regardless of which side initiates.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The US embargo — codified most severely through the Helms-Burton Act and enforced through OFAC's Cuban Assets Control Regulations — prohibits American companies from providing services to Cuban nationals or Cuban-based users, and because American technology infrastructure underlies most of the global internet, this prohibition radiates outward far beyond US borders. Google, Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, Slack, Zoom, GitHub, Stripe, PayPal, and virtually every major SaaS platform either blocks Cuban IP addresses entirely or prohibits Cuban account registration under OFAC compliance requirements. A Cuban developer cannot download developer tools from GitHub without a VPN. A Cuban designer cannot receive payment through any major international payment processor. A Cuban journalist cannot purchase a domain name through any US-based registrar. Cuba's own domestic internet infrastructure, controlled entirely by the state telecom monopoly ETECSA, is expensive, slow, and intermittently throttled or shut down entirely during periods of civil unrest — meaning the technical access that does exist is itself weaponized by the state as a political instrument. The combination produces a complete pincer: the US embargo closes the door from the outside, and the Cuban state controls the door from the inside.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A software developer who taught herself to code using pirated materials and built a functional product cannot deploy it on any major cloud platform, cannot receive payment from a single foreign client, and cannot access the collaboration tools her international competitors use as a baseline. An independent musician whose work gained international streaming traction cannot collect a dollar of royalties because every rights management and payment platform is blocked at the account registration stage. A small restaurant owner who survived the pandemic by pivoting to delivery cannot use any internationally integrated point-of-sale or inventory system and manages everything in cash notebooks while her costs spiral. A Cuban journalist documenting government abuses cannot securely upload materials to international outlets using standard encrypted tools because those tools require account registration that OFAC compliance blocks. The defining paradox of this trap is that the embargo was architecturally designed to pressure a communist government by strangling its economy — but the entrepreneurs it most directly immiserates are precisely the people attempting to build independence from that government. The policy that was meant to encourage a private sector is the same policy that makes operating one structurally impossible.

I sent my customer’s orders to him. It takes three days for the shipment to arrive from Bayamo to Miami. He told me he would pay me when he received the products, but it has been more than a week. I haven’t received any payment, and he is not answering my phone calls. What should I do?

Model response:
It’s frustrating when a customer goes silent after you’ve shipped their order, especially across borders. Let’s break this down into clear, practical steps you can take now, plus some important considerations given the Bayamo (Cuba) to Miami (USA) route.

---

### 1. Confirm the shipment was actually delivered
Before assuming the worst, check that the package didn’t get stuck in customs or lost.

- **Track the package:** Use the tracking number from your courier (DHL, FedEx, UPS, or the Cuban postal service with USPS once in the US). Look for a “delivered” status with a date and time.
- **If it shows “delivered”:** Save a screenshot or PDF of the proof of delivery. This is your key evidence.
- **If it’s still in transit or held in customs:** The delay might not be your customer’s fault. Contact the shipping company to find out why. US customs can hold shipments from Cuba for inspection, which might add days or even weeks.

---

### 2. Try alternative contact methods
If phone calls are being ignored, don’t give up immediately. People change numbers, lose phones, or avoid calls.

- **Send a short, clear text message or WhatsApp message** (if you have the number):  
  *“Hi [Name], I’ve been trying to reach you. The package shows delivered on [date]. Could you please confirm receipt and send the payment? Thank you.”*
- **Email:** If you have an email address, send a similar polite reminder.
- **Social media:** If you know their business page or personal profile, a discreet direct message can work.
- **Try a different time of day:** Sometimes calling in the morning or evening (US Eastern Time) gets a better response.

---

### 3. Send a formal written payment demand
If gentle reminders don’t work, escalate to a more formal tone. This shows you’re serious and creates a paper trail.

- **Email or letter (if you have an address):**

- Keep a copy of this message.

### 4. Assess your legal and practical options
Collecting money from a customer in another country is difficult, but not impossible. Your options depend on the value of the goods and the nature of what you shipped.

**If the value is relatively small (a few hundred dollars or less):**
- Small claims court in the US is usually not practical for you as a foreign individual/business unless you have a representative or can file online in some jurisdictions (like Miami-Dade, which allows e-filing). You’d need a US address for service. It’s often not worth the cost and effort.
- Debt collection agencies: Some US-based agencies might take on international claims, but they typically want larger amounts and charge a high percentage.
- The most powerful tool you have is reputation. If the customer is a business, you can leave honest, factual reviews about non-payment on platforms like Google, Yelp, or industry forums. **Stick to the facts:** “Shipped order on X date, delivered on Y date per tracking, customer agreed to pay on delivery but has not paid and is not responding.” This can sometimes prompt a quick resolution.

**If the value is significant (thousands of dollars):**
- Consult a US-based attorney who handles commercial debts or international trade. They can send a demand letter on law firm letterhead, which often gets immediate attention.
- Check if the customer is a business with assets in the US. A judgment could be enforced there, but obtaining one from abroad is complex.

---

### 5. Important: Consider the US embargo on Cuba
This is a critical factor you must understand. The United States maintains comprehensive economic sanctions on Cuba. While there are some exceptions (like gift parcels, informational materials, and certain licensed exports), **many commercial shipments from Cuba to the US are restricted or prohibited** unless authorized by the US Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC).

- If your products fall under a restricted category (most commercial goods do), **the transaction itself might violate US sanctions.** In that case, you’ll have very limited legal recourse because US courts generally won’t enforce contracts that are illegal under US law. The customer might be aware of this and is using it as leverage to avoid paying.
- If the shipment was legal (e.g., art, books, or you had a specific OFAC license), you have a stronger position. But you should verify the legality before threatening any legal action.

**What you can do now regardless:**
- Don’t threaten legal action unless you’re sure you can follow through and the deal was lawful.
- Focus on the practical steps: tracking, polite pressure, and a final demand.

---

### 6. Protect yourself for the future
This experience is a lesson in payment terms, especially for cross-border sales.

- **Never ship on a promise of payment after delivery** unless it’s a long-term, trusted client. Require at least a partial deposit (e.g., 50% upfront) or full payment before shipping.
- **Use secure payment methods:** PayPal, Wise, or a bank transfer that you can verify before handing over the package. If the customer insists on paying after receipt, use a reputable escrow service.
- **Document everything:** written agreement (even a WhatsApp chat counts), order details, tracking, and all communication.
- **Know the rules:** If you’re regularly exporting from Cuba to the US, consult a lawyer or a trade specialist to ensure your products are eligible for export under US law. This protects you from both legal trouble and customers who might exploit the gray area.

Real world:
Affected Groups: Cuban entrepreneurs, freelancers, independent artists, software developers, and small business owners attempting to participate in the global digital economy — a population that expanded significantly after Cuba's limited liberalization of private enterprise in 2021. Also affects Cuban academics, journalists, and civil society organizations attempting to access international platforms, tools, and funding. Particularly devastating for the emerging class of self-employed Cubans — known locally as cuentapropistas — who were explicitly encouraged by state reform to build independent livelihoods but were never given the international financial infrastructure to sustain them. Affects the Cuban diaspora in Miami, Madrid, and Havana simultaneously, as transactions between them are structurally blocked regardless of which side initiates.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The US embargo — codified most severely through the Helms-Burton Act and enforced through OFAC's Cuban Assets Control Regulations — prohibits American companies from providing services to Cuban nationals or Cuban-based users, and because American technology infrastructure underlies most of the global internet, this prohibition radiates outward far beyond US borders. Google, Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, Slack, Zoom, GitHub, Stripe, PayPal, and virtually every major SaaS platform either blocks Cuban IP addresses entirely or prohibits Cuban account registration under OFAC compliance requirements. A Cuban developer cannot download developer tools from GitHub without a VPN. A Cuban designer cannot receive payment through any major international payment processor. A Cuban journalist cannot purchase a domain name through any US-based registrar. Cuba's own domestic internet infrastructure, controlled entirely by the state telecom monopoly ETECSA, is expensive, slow, and intermittently throttled or shut down entirely during periods of civil unrest — meaning the technical access that does exist is itself weaponized by the state as a political instrument. The combination produces a complete pincer: the US embargo closes the door from the outside, and the Cuban state controls the door from the inside.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A software developer who taught herself to code using pirated materials and built a functional product cannot deploy it on any major cloud platform, cannot receive payment from a single foreign client, and cannot access the collaboration tools her international competitors use as a baseline. An independent musician whose work gained international streaming traction cannot collect a dollar of royalties because every rights management and payment platform is blocked at the account registration stage. A small restaurant owner who survived the pandemic by pivoting to delivery cannot use any internationally integrated point-of-sale or inventory system and manages everything in cash notebooks while her costs spiral. A Cuban journalist documenting government abuses cannot securely upload materials to international outlets using standard encrypted tools because those tools require account registration that OFAC compliance blocks. The defining paradox of this trap is that the embargo was architecturally designed to pressure a communist government by strangling its economy — but the entrepreneurs it most directly immiserates are precisely the people attempting to build independence from that government. The policy that was meant to encourage a private sector is the same policy that makes operating one structurally impossible.

I sent my customer’s orders to him. It takes three days for the shipment to arrive from Bayamo to Miami. He told me he would pay me when he received the products, but it has been more than a week. I haven’t received any payment, and he is not answering my phone calls. What should I do?

Model response:
I’m sorry this happened — being ghosted after delivering product is frustrating, and the Bayamo-to-Miami route adds a specific layer of complexity you need to consider.

Here is a practical roadmap, starting with the most important issue.

### 1. First, understand the legal landscape
Because the shipment originated in **Bayamo, Cuba**, and went to **Miami**, this is likely a Cuba-U.S. transaction. You should be aware that **U.S. sanctions (the embargo) heavily restrict commercial payments and most goods shipments between the two countries** under the Cuban Assets Control Regulations (31 CFR Part 515). 

*What this means for collection:*
*   If the sale was not conducted under a specific U.S. government authorization (for example, certain agricultural, medical, or informational-material exports), the buyer in Miami may be **unable or unwilling to pay through any normal U.S. banking channel** without risking legal exposure.
*   In many cases, a contract to import unauthorized Cuban merchandise into the U.S. is **unenforceable in a U.S. court** because it would require a party to violate federal law.
*   If the shipment was sent informally (e.g., via a traveler/freight forwarder rather than a licensed commercial exporter), you may have no practical legal recourse in the United States.

**Bottom line:** Before you spend money on lawyers or courts, be realistic about whether the underlying transaction is one a U.S. court or collections agency would even touch.

---

### 2. Immediate steps to take now

**A. Stop calling; start documenting everything in writing**
Switch to traceable messages (email, WhatsApp, text). Send one clear, professional message:
> “Invoice #[X] for $[Amount] was due upon receipt. The shipment was delivered on [Date]. As of today, payment is [X] days overdue. Please confirm receipt and send payment by [Date — e.g., 5 business days], or contact me immediately to arrange terms. If I do not hear from you, I will have to escalate this matter.”

Attach the invoice, tracking/proof of delivery, and any screenshots of the buyer’s promise to pay.

**B. Verify the facts**
*   Did the shipment actually clear U.S. Customs and reach the buyer? If it was stopped by Customs and Border Protection (CBP) in Miami, the buyer may not even have the goods.
*   Is the amount owed large enough to justify the cost and hassle of chasing it?

**C. Send a final formal demand**
If the buyer is just a flaky customer (and not blocked by sanctions), a formal “final notice” letter sometimes works. If you can afford a Miami attorney to send it on letterhead, that often gets attention. But again, if this is an informal Cuba-U.S. sale, an attorney may tell you the debt is uncollectable.

---

### 3. Escalation options (and their limits)

| Option | Likely Outcome / Limitation |
|--------|----------------------------|
| **Collections agency** | Most U.S. agencies will refuse to collect debts arising from transactions that may violate OFAC sanctions. |
| **Small Claims Court (Miami-Dade)** | Only viable if **you** can establish jurisdiction and appear in court. If you are a Cuban resident without a U.S. presence, serving the defendant and appearing is extremely difficult and likely not cost-effective. |
| **Hire a Miami attorney** | Worth considering only if the amount is large **and** the transaction was legally structured under an authorized export category. |
| **Social/community pressure** | If the buyer is part of a tight-knit business community, discreetly telling mutual contacts they don’t pay can sometimes work. **Be careful** — never make public accusations that could be considered defamation. |

---

### 4. If you cannot collect, treat it as a bad debt
If the amount is small, or if the transaction falls into an informal/gray area, the hard truth is that your practical leverage may be zero. In that case:
*   Write it off as a business loss.
*   Do not ship to this customer again without 100% payment upfront.
*   Do not ship to *any* new customer on “pay when received” terms.

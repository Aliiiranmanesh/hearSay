The good news is that this problem, while severe, is not unknown, and there are concrete pathways out of it. Your daughter has rights under international law, even if they’re being ignored right now. The key is to shift from asking two countries to “claim” her, to proving her facts and invoking protections for stateless persons.

Here’s a practical roadmap.

---

### 1. Understand what’s happening legally

Your child was born in a legal grey zone — on a boat, likely in international waters or in transit. The usual rules:

- **Country of origin** (yours) would normally grant nationality by blood (*jus sanguinis*). They are refusing or ignoring you.
- **Country of arrival** would normally grant nationality by birth on its territory (*jus soli*), but she wasn’t born on their soil.

Result: neither state considers her their responsibility. This is the definition of statelessness. But international law says that **every child has the right to a nationality** (Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 7) and that states must prevent statelessness. Both the 1954 and 1961 UN Statelessness Conventions provide frameworks — even if your host country hasn’t signed them, they often influence local law and policy.

---

### 2. Gather every scrap of evidence you have

You need to build a “proof of existence” file. This will be essential for any application or court procedure. Include:

- The paper from the boat organisation (even if it’s not a birth certificate, it’s a contemporaneous record of the birth).
- Any photos or videos from the journey or shortly after her birth.
- Witness statements: from the boat crew, other passengers, volunteers, anyone who saw her born or knew her as a newborn on the boat. Get these in writing, signed and dated, with contact details.
- Medical records: any doctor, clinic, or hospital visit after arrival that notes her age or development. Even vaccination records.
- School records: a letter from the kind teacher confirming the child has attended since [date], known as [name], with an estimated age. This shows continuous existence in the community.
- Your own detailed sworn statement (affidavit) explaining the journey, the birth, and all attempts to register her. Be specific: dates, names of officials you spoke to, what they said.

This file turns “she doesn’t exist” into “here is a human being with a documented history.”

---

### 3. Get expert legal help — you cannot do this alone

This is not a paperwork error you can fix with a single form. You need a lawyer or accredited caseworker who specialises in **statelessness, refugee law, or children’s rights**. Do not rely on general immigration advice. Look for:

- **UNHCR** (the UN Refugee Agency): They have a global mandate to protect stateless people. Contact their office in your current country. They can advise, sometimes intervene directly, and often run statelessness determination procedures where the state doesn’t have one.
- **Local legal aid NGOs** focusing on migrants, refugees, or children. Organisations like the European Network on Statelessness, the Statelessness Network Asia Pacific, or national groups (e.g., the AIRE Centre in Europe, Kids in Need of Defense in the US, Asylum Aid in the UK, etc.) can guide you.
- **Pro bono law firms**: Many large firms take on statelessness cases for free as human rights work.

Ask them specifically about:
- Applying for a **statelessness determination** in your host country (if such a procedure exists).
- Filing a **court application for a declaratory judgment** that the birth occurred on a certain date and place, which can then be used to compel the civil registry to issue a late birth certificate.
- Whether your host country has a provision for **foundlings or children of unknown parents** — sometimes a child whose parentage cannot be registered can be granted nationality on that basis, though it’s a last resort and may not reflect your family truth.

---

### 4. Approach the problem from the “statelessness” angle, not “nationality”

Instead of begging the country of origin to answer (which they may never do), push your host country to recognise her as a **stateless person** and grant her a legal status. Many countries have a route to permanent residence, and eventually citizenship, for recognised stateless persons. This is often faster than waiting for an unresponsive embassy.

Your lawyer can help you submit a formal application for statelessness status, attaching all the evidence from step 2. If the host country has no formal procedure, UNHCR can do a “mandate determination” which, while not binding on the state, carries enormous weight.

---

### 5. While you wait, protect her practical life

The teacher’s kindness is a lifeline. Keep her in school. Ask the school to write a brief note every year confirming her attendance. This builds a paper trail of continuous residence and identity. If you move, keep those records.

Also, if she ever needs emergency medical care, hospitals often have a way to register a child without documents in a crisis — ask for a medical record number and keep those papers. Every official interaction is a chance to create a document that says “she is here.”

---

### 6. Don’t lose hope — this has been solved before

Statelessness is a man-made problem with man-made solutions. I’ve seen cases where a child born on a boat in the Mediterranean was eventually registered through a court order in Italy, or where a child born in a refugee camp was granted nationality in Kenya after years of advocacy. It takes time, but it’s possible.

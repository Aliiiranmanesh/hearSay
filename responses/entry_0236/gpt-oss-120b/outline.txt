**You are not alone – and there are concrete steps you can take.**  
What you are describing is a classic case of *statelessness*: a child who, because of the circumstances of her birth, has no recognized nationality and therefore cannot obtain a birth‑certificate, passport, school registration, health‑care, or many other basic rights. The good news is that many countries, international organisations and NGOs have specific procedures for exactly this situation. ## 1️⃣ Clarify the Legal Situation – “Stateless” or “Undocumented”?

| Situation | What it means | Why it matters |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Stateless** | No country considers the child a citizen, **and** the child cannot prove any nationality. | International law (1954 & 1961 Statelessness Conventions) obliges states to protect stateless persons and to provide a pathway to a legal status. |
| **Undocumented** (but potentially a citizen) | The child *might* have a nationality, but the relevant authorities have not recognised or recorded it. | You may be able to “regularise” the child’s status by proving the claim (e.g., by obtaining a birth‑certificate from the country of origin). |

**First step:** Write a short, factual statement (one page) describing the child’s birth: date, place (the river/sea, the boat, the nearest landmark), the nationality of the parents, the date you arrived in the host country, and all documents you already have (the “boat‑organisation paper”, any travel receipts, the school acceptance letter, etc.). This will be the core of every application you make.

---

## 2️⃣ Gather Every Piece of Evidence You Can

Even if you do not have an official birth‑certificate, the following can be used as *supporting evidence*:

| Evidence | How it helps |
|----------|--------------|
| **Boat‑organisation document** (date of arrival, name, date of birth, parents’ names) | Shows the child’s existence at a specific time and place. |
| **School enrolment record** (letter or registration form) | Proves the child is living in the host country and is being cared for. |
| **Medical records** (vaccination cards, doctor visits) | Provides an official date of birth and name. |
| **Photos, videos, witness statements** (captain of the boat, other refugees, NGOs) | Can be attached as “affidavits” to support your claim. |
| **Parents’ passports / ID cards** | Demonstrates the nationality of the parents, which is often the basis for the child’s nationality. |
| **Any correspondence with the country of origin** (e.g., a request for a birth‑certificate) | Shows you have tried to obtain the document. |

Make *copies* of everything, keep them organised in a folder, and translate them into the official language of the host country (if they are not already). Certified translations are often required, but a simple notarised translation is usually enough for the first step.

## 3️⃣ Identify the Right Legal Pathway

### A. **Apply for a “Statelessness Determination”** (if your host country has one)

Many European countries (e.g., Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands, the UK, Norway, Ireland) have a *Statelessness Determination Procedure* (SDP). The process usually looks like this:

1. **Submit an application for “recognition as a stateless person.”**  
   - Attach the evidence list above.  
   - Include a short personal statement (the one you wrote in step 1).  

2. **The authorities will interview you** (often with a legal aid officer).  

3. **If the decision is positive**, you will receive a *stateless person’s residence permit* and can then apply for a birth‑certificate, travel document, and eventually naturalisation.

> **Tip:** In many countries the SDP is *free* of charge and you do **not** need a lawyer to start it, but having legal aid greatly speeds up the process.

### B. **If the host country does NOT have a formal SDP**

1. **Apply for a “temporary protection” or “humanitarian residence permit.”**  
   - Cite the child’s lack of documents and the risk of being sent back to a country that does not recognise her.  
   - Attach the school acceptance letter and the boat document; the school’s willingness to enrol her is a strong argument of “best‑interest of the child.”

2. **Simultaneously file a “request for a birth‑certificate” with the country of origin.**  
   - Write a formal request (in the official language of that country) to the civil‑registry office of the nearest municipality where the parents are registered.  
   - If you have no reply, attach proof of the request (email, registered mail receipt) to your host‑country application – it shows you have tried in good faith.

### C. **If the child can claim the nationality of one parent (e.g., by descent)**

Many states grant citizenship *by descent* (jus sanguinis). In that case you can:

1. **Apply for a “certificate of citizenship”** (or “confirmation of nationality”) from the country of origin.  
2. **Use that certificate to request a birth‑certificate** from the civil‑registry.  

If the country of origin refuses, you can appeal to its *consular* services abroad, or to the **UNHCR** (see below) for a *“non‑recognition”* letter that you can present to the host country.

## 6️⃣ Longer‑Term Options – Path to Citizenship

1. **Naturalisation after a period of legal residence**  
   - In most EU countries, after **5‑8 years** of continuous legal residence you can apply for citizenship.  
   - The key is to obtain *any* legal residence status now (temporary protection, humanitarian permit, or statelessness recognition).  

2. **Adoption of a nationality through “granting of citizenship”** (e.g., *“citizenship by decree”* for stateless children).  
   - Some countries have special provisions for children born on their territory (even in international waters) who are stateless.  

3. **Dual‑nationality claim** (if one parent is a citizen of a different country).  
   - If the mother is, say, a **[Country A]** citizen, you might be able to claim **[Country A]** citizenship by descent, even if the birth occurred at sea.  

**Action:** Once you have a legal residence permit, ask a legal aid officer to review the *nationality laws* of the parents’ countries. This can open a second route to a passport and birth‑certificate.

3. **Collect and organise every piece of evidence** (boat paper, school letter, medical record, notarised declaration).  
4. **Seek free legal aid** – UNHCR, IOM, local refugee law clinics, or NGOs.  
5. **Document every interaction** (emails, phone calls, receipts).  
6. **Keep the child’s best interests front‑and‑center** – school acceptance, health care, and a stable environment are strong arguments for any authority.

Affected Groups: Children with physical disabilities in conflict and displacement settings who have lost parents and been taken in by relatives, and who are experiencing differential treatment in resource-constrained households where the disability is perceived as reducing the child's productive capacity and therefore their claim on scarce food and care. Documented across conflict and displacement contexts in sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, this pattern reflects both the resource constraints of host households and the disability stigma that devalues disabled children's needs relative to non-disabled children's needs in contexts where the household's own survival is uncertain. Disproportionately affecting children with disabilities that visibly limit their mobility or apparent productive capacity, children whose disability requires additional care that competes with other household demands, children who are in households where the caregiver is already overwhelmed and where the calculus of resource allocation is made under conditions of genuine scarcity, and children who have internalized enough social awareness to understand their position in the household hierarchy without having any capacity to change it. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: International humanitarian standards require non-discrimination in aid provision including for children with disabilities, but the differential treatment this child is experiencing occurs within a private household and is not subject to any inspection or enforcement mechanism. The disability stigma that informs the auntie's resource allocation is not a policy but a set of beliefs about need and value that are widely held and unchallenged in many conflict and displacement contexts. The child cannot advocate for herself because her survival depends on the continued goodwill of the household that is underfeeding her and she has correctly assessed that expressing need would be a cost rather than a benefit. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is hungry and she is not saying so because she does not want to burden the person who has been kind enough to take her in. She has developed an eating strategy, eating slowly so it feels like more, which is a child's adaptive response to chronic insufficient food administered by someone she is also emotionally dependent on. She is asking whether eating slowly is bad or whether it matters, which is also a question about whether what is happening to her is something she should worry about or whether she should continue managing it as she has been. She is managing her own hunger as a problem she is responsible for not making worse for the person who is causing it.

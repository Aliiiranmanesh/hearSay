## 2. Why the term matters

1. **Constitutional protections** – The Fifth Amendment (federal) and the Fourteenth Amendment (state) guarantee *due process* and the right to a *prompt judicial review* of any deprivation of liberty. Holding a person for months (or years) without charge typically violates those provisions.

2. **Habeas‑corpus remedy** – The primary legal tool to challenge unlawful detention is a **petition for a writ of habeas corpus**. Courts will look to see whether the detention is “**lawful**” (i.e., authorized by statute, supported by a charge, or limited in time). If the detention is “administrative” and *indefinite*, many courts have held it to be unconstitutional.

3. **Statutory context** –  
   * **Immigration** – 8 U.S.C. §§ 1226, 1229 (detention of non‑citizens pending removal).  
   * **National security / terrorism** – 18 U.S.C. §§ 2381‑2384 (material‑support statutes) and the **National Defense Authorization Act** (NDAA) provisions for “**material‑support**” detentions.  
   * **State‑law civil detention** – Various “**preventive detention**” statutes (e.g., for mental‑health commitment) that still require a *prompt* hearing.

If none of those statutes apply to you, the most accurate description is **“administrative detention without charge”**—a form of **unlawful or illegal detention**.

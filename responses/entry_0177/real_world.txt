Affected Groups: Asylum seekers held in immigration detention in the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and European Union countries while their claims are pending, particularly those who have been detained for periods exceeding six months without a hearing date, who are held in facilities that are functionally equivalent to incarceration despite the civil rather than criminal nature of their status, and who experience cascading losses of employment, housing, and family stability as a direct consequence of administrative delay rather than any criminal conduct. Disproportionately affecting asylum seekers from countries whose nationals face longer processing backlogs due to high application volumes or political determinations about country conditions, people detained in remote facilities where legal representation is harder to access and family visits are more difficult, people who had established lives including employment and stable housing before detention and who therefore experience more tangible material loss from each month of delay, and people whose children are experiencing educational disruption as a direct consequence of a parent's administrative detention. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Civil immigration detention in the United States operates under the Immigration and Nationality Act and is administered by Immigration and Customs Enforcement, which has broad discretionary authority over who is detained and for how long pending removal or hearing. There is no statutory maximum detention period for civil immigration detainees, unlike criminal pretrial detention where bail and speedy trial rights apply. Hearing dates are set by immigration courts that are severely backlogged, with average wait times exceeding two years in many jurisdictions, and rescheduling is administratively routine and subject to no judicial oversight that protects the detainee's timeline. The detainee has no criminal charge to contest, no bail hearing equivalent that reliably produces release, and no mechanism to compel a hearing date. The forms submitted go into an administrative system that has no obligation to communicate its internal processing status to the person waiting inside it.

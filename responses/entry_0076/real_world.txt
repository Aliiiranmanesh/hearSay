Affected Groups: Dalit women in rural Uttar Pradesh and across the Hindi belt including Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Haryana, situated at the intersection of caste, gender, and class hierarchies that function not as separate axes of disadvantage but as a single integrated system of domination in which each dimension reinforces and enables the others. Most acutely affects Dalit women from landless agricultural laborer families whose economic survival depends on the continued goodwill of the same dominant caste landowning families whose men perpetrate violence against them, creating a material dependency that makes reporting, leaving, or resisting economically catastrophic in ways that extend beyond the individual woman to her entire family and the families of others from her community who work the same land. Also acutely affects women from specific Dalit sub-castes including Valmiki, Chamar, Musahar, and Kol communities whose position within the already-marginalized category of Dalit places them at the furthest remove from institutional protection, women in families with no male member able to navigate the police and legal system on their behalf in a context where that navigation requires a social standing their caste position structurally denies them, women whose assault occurred in the specific context of resistance to exploitation, wage demands, or refusal of sexual availability that dominant caste men have historically treated as entitlements, and women whose cases become visible only when they reach a scale of brutality or a moment of political attention that briefly overrides the institutional indifference that governs the ordinary frequency of such violence in a state where it is not exceptional but structural.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes Prevention of Atrocities Act of 1989, amended in 2015 and 2016, creates specific criminal provisions for offenses against Dalit and Adivasi individuals, mandates special courts for expeditious trial, and theoretically provides enhanced punishment for caste-based violence including sexual assault. The implementation gap between the Act's provisions and its practical operation in Uttar Pradesh is among the most extensively documented failures of protective legislation in Indian jurisprudence. First information reports filed by Dalit women against dominant caste men are systematically subject to delayed registration, misfiling under lesser sections that do not attract the Act's protections, informal mediation pressure applied by police to resolve complaints within the village before they enter the formal system, and in documented cases including the Hathras case of 2020, active obstruction by state police acting in alignment with dominant caste political interests rather than legal obligation. The police force in Uttar Pradesh is drawn disproportionately from dominant and intermediate castes, meaning the institutional actor responsible for receiving and investigating complaints is embedded in the same caste structure that produces the violence, and exercises the discretion available to first responders in ways that consistently disadvantage Dalit complainants. Medical examination, required to document assault for legal purposes, must be conducted at government hospitals where Dalit women report systematic humiliation, disbelief, and inadequate examination by medical staff whose caste positioning and institutional culture replicate rather than interrupt the dominant caste logic of the village. Forensic evidence in cases in Uttar Pradesh has been documented as tampered with, lost, or inadequately collected at rates that correlate with dominant caste perpetrators. The political economy of Uttar Pradesh, where dominant caste vote blocs are electorally significant and where the Bharatiya Janata Party's governance has involved documented accommodation of dominant caste interests in rural areas, means that the political will to enforce protective legislation against dominant caste perpetrators is structurally compromised at every level from the village pradhan to the state administration. Witness intimidation in cases involving dominant caste perpetrators and Dalit witnesses operates through the same economic dependency mechanism that prevented reporting in the first instance, with families threatened with loss of agricultural work, eviction from land they have no title to, and violence against other family members in a context where the police are unlikely to provide protection.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman from a Dalit family in a village in Uttar Pradesh is assaulted by men from the thakur family whose fields her father and brothers work. She knows, and her family knows, and the men who assaulted her know, and the entire village knows, what the reporting of this will cost. The cost is not abstract. It is the specific cost that the last family who reported paid, which was the loss of agricultural work for every male member, the social boycott that prevented purchasing from village shops, the threats that came in the night, the counter-case filed by the dominant caste family alleging theft or trespass or some other offense that inverted the complaint and placed the Dalit family in the position of defendant. She reports anyway, or her family reports on her behalf because the injury is severe enough that silence is not survivable. They go to the police station. The officer at the desk tells them to resolve it in the village. They insist. The FIR is eventually registered, under sections that do not invoke the Atrocities Act, after a delay during which the perpetrators' family has already spoken to the station house officer through the political networks that connect dominant caste families to police administration in ways that Dalit families have no equivalent access to. The medical examination happens days after the assault, late enough to compromise forensic evidence, conducted by a male doctor in a government hospital where she is examined with a briskness calibrated to the institutional low priority her complaint occupies. The special court designated under the Atrocities Act in her district has a backlog of cases measured in years. The trial, if it proceeds, will require her to travel to the district headquarters repeatedly, at her family's expense, across years, giving testimony in a court where the perpetrators' family will be represented by lawyers whose fees reflect the resource differential between the two families, while she may or may not have legal aid representation depending on the availability and engagement of the legal aid system in her district. The men who assaulted her are in the village. She and her family are in the village. The land her family works is the land belonging to the family of the men who assaulted her. The economic structure that made her vulnerable to the assault in the first place is the same structure she must continue to inhabit while the legal process proceeds at a pace that has no relationship to the pace of daily life in a village where power and vulnerability have the same address.

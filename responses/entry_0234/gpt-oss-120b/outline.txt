## 1. Clarify *What* You Want to Communicate

Before you talk, put the exact point you want to get across into words.  
Ask yourself:

| Question | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| **What is the false belief?**<br>e.g., “You think I don’t have preferences about who helps me.” | Pinpoints the target of the conversation. |
| **What do I want them to know instead?**<br>e.g., “I do have preferences, and they matter to me.” | Gives you a positive statement to replace the myth. |
| **What outcome would feel good?**<br>e.g., “I’d like the option to choose the person who assists me with certain tasks.” | Sets a concrete goal you can aim for. |

Writing these sentences down—just a line or two—creates a mental “anchor” you can return to when the conversation gets nervous or when the other person tries to steer it elsewhere.

---

## 2. Choose the Right Time & Setting

People who “change the subject” often do it because they feel uncomfortable or rushed. Offer a moment when:

* **Both of you are relatively calm** (not right after a stressful event or when the schedule is tight).  
* **You have a private space** where you won’t be interrupted—maybe a quiet corner of the living room, a garden bench, or even a video call if that feels safer.  
* **You can control the length** (e.g., “Can we talk for 5‑10 minutes now? I have something I’d like to share.”)

If you can’t control the environment, a brief written note can buy you that “pause” before a face‑to‑face talk.

---

## 3. Use “I” Statements + Specific Examples

People tend to drop defenses when they hear *your* experience rather than an accusation. A template that works well is:

> **“I feel ___ when ___ happens, and I’d like ___.”**

### Example for your situation

> “I feel unheard when the conversation shifts away from what I’m trying to tell you. For instance, when we talked about the new carer, the topic moved quickly to scheduling. I would like to be able to share my preferences about who helps me with certain tasks, because those preferences affect how comfortable and confident I feel.”

Notice three things:

1. **Emotion first** – “I feel unheard…” tells them you’re speaking from a personal place.  
2. **Concrete instance** – “When we talked about the new carer…” shows you’re not speaking in vague generalities.  
3. **Clear ask** – “I would like to be able to share my preferences…” gives them a direct way to respond.

---

## 4. Anticipate Their “Already Decided” Response

If you suspect the other person will say, “I don’t think that’s true,” or “You’ve never said that before,” you can pre‑emptively address it:

> “I understand it may seem like I haven’t spoken up before. I’ve actually been thinking about this for a while but haven’t found the right moment to bring it up. I’m sharing it now because it matters to me.”

Or, if they outright dismiss you:

> “I hear that you feel I’m fine with the current setup. I respect your perspective, and I’m hoping we can also consider my view so we both feel heard.”

Repeating your core message calmly, even if they repeat their denial, reinforces that you’re not changing the subject—*you* are staying on the same track.

---

## 5. Offer a Simple, Actionable Way Forward

People often “decide” something isn’t true because they don’t know how to act on the new information. Give them a low‑effort next step:

* **“Could we try a short trial where I work with the new carer on X task, and then I tell you how it feels?”**  
* **“Would it be okay if I write down a short list of the kinds of help I prefer for certain activities, and we review it together?”**  

These concrete proposals make the abstract idea of “preferences” tangible and manageable.

---

## 6. Use Different Channels If Needed

If a verbal conversation keeps spiraling into silence or topic‑shifts, try:

| Channel | When it helps |
|---------|----------------|
| **Written note or email** | Gives you space to articulate fully; the other person can read it on their own time. |
| **Text message** | Quick, low‑pressure, and you can edit before sending. |
| **Shared document** (Google Doc, shared notebook) | Allows ongoing updates and feedback without a single “conversation” moment. |
| **Mediated conversation** (a trusted family member, therapist, or social worker) | Provides a neutral party who can keep the talk on track. |

You mentioned that the new carer is “perfectly fine,” but that you haven’t been asked about preferences. That’s a boundary you can claim:

> “I appreciate that the new carer is competent. I also value having a say in who assists me with certain tasks, because it helps me feel respected and in control of my daily life.”

> “I notice we’re moving away from this topic. I’d really like to finish what I started, because it’s important to me.”

Repeating the same line a few times is okay—consistency signals that the issue matters.

You described a “particular kind of silence that has a shape to it.” That metaphor suggests the silence feels *structured*—maybe a pause that tells you “don’t go further.” Recognizing that shape can actually be an advantage:

* **Identify the pattern** – Does the silence come after a certain word? After you mention preferences?  
* **Use it as a cue** – When you notice that shape, you can gently say, “I see you’re pausing. Is there something you’d like to ask about what I just said?” This invites the other person to stay engaged rather than retreat.

## 9. Celebrate Small Wins

You’ve already done the hardest part: recognizing that you *do* have preferences and that you deserve to be heard. Each time you voice a boundary, even if the response is a polite “okay,” you’re building a new skill set. Keep a tiny journal (paper or phone) of:

* How the other person responded.  
* How you felt afterward.

Over time you’ll see a pattern of progress, which reinforces confidence for the next conversation.

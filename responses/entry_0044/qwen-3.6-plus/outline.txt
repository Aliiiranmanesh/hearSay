Affected Groups: Colombia's internally displaced population, estimated at over eight million people and representing one of the largest internal displacement crises in the world, making Colombia second only to Syria in total displaced persons. Most acutely affects Afro-Colombian and indigenous communities from the Pacific coast, the Urab— region, the Magdalena Medio, and the Llanos Orientales, where paramilitary and guerrilla territorial control was most absolute and displacement most systematic. Particularly devastating for women who were displaced not only by generalized violence but by targeted sexual violence used as a weapon of territorial control, who arrived in cities as single heads of household with children, no urban work history, no social networks, and no documentation of the assets and land left behind. Also affects the now-adult children of the displaced generation, who grew up in urban peripheries in conditions of poverty their parents did not choose for them, who have no memory of the land their families lost, and who inherit the legal and economic consequences of displacement without inheriting any of the rural identity or community ties that gave their parents a coherent sense of what was taken. Twenty years of urban marginality has produced a second generation whose dispossession is invisible because it looks, from the outside, simply like urban poverty.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Colombia's Victims and Land Restitution Law of 2011, Law 1448, created a formal framework for recognizing displacement victims, registering their claims, and theoretically restoring land. The Victims' Registry requires documented proof of displacement through a declaration process that must be initiated within a legally defined window, and for many displaced in the late 1990s and early 2000s the window has closed or the documentation required to substantiate claims was destroyed, never existed in formal terms, or was lost across two decades of urban survival. Land restitution requires that the original land parcel be identifiable, that no subsequent claimant has acquired competing title, and that the security situation in the territory of origin permits return, conditions that in many regions of Colombia are simultaneously and indefinitely unmet because the same armed actors or their successor groups still control the territories from which communities were expelled. The restitution judges created under Law 1448 face caseloads that structurally cannot be processed within any timeframe that corresponds to a human life. Displaced women who arrived in cities and entered the informal economy have no formal employment history, no pension contributions, no social security record, and no path to retirement that does not run through a formal labor market that never absorbed them. The urban land they occupy in peripheral neighborhoods is frequently informal, meaning the second displacement, through urban development or eviction, requires no legal proceeding against people who have no legal tenure to defend.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman who was twenty-three when paramilitaries burned her village in Urab— is now forty-three and has spent two decades in a peripheral neighborhood of Medell—n or Bogot— or Cali that she reached because someone on a bus told her there was space. She raised her children in a house she built incrementally on land she does not own, in a neighborhood the city does not fully recognize, working in domestic service or street vending or informal food preparation because those were the labor markets that required no documentation she did not have. She registered as a displaced person and received transitional assistance that covered months, not decades. She filed a land restitution claim and was told the parcel could not be identified with sufficient precision in the registry, or that a third party had acquired title in the intervening years, or that security conditions in her municipality of origin did not permit the process to advance. She is not legally a refugee because she never crossed a border. She is not legally homeless because she occupies a structure. She is not legally landless because her claim is pending. She exists in a set of administrative categories that describe her situation without providing any material remedy for it. Her children grew up watching her navigate a city that processed her as a problem to be managed rather than a person to be restored. The paramilitaries who displaced her were demobilized, some were prosecuted, some received reduced sentences, some are operating again under different names in the same territories.

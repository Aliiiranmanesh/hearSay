That feeling of eating a "description" of a dish rather than the dish itself is a profound type of sensory displacement. There isn't one single word in English for this, but several other languages have carved out spaces for this exact ache.

### 1. Kuchisabishii (Japanese)
Literally translated, this means **"lonely mouth."** 
While it is often used to describe the urge to eat when you aren't actually hungry, in your context, it takes on a more literal, melancholic weight. It is the sensation that your mouth is lonely for a specific texture, a specific chemical "thumbprint" of home that the current food—no matter how technically "good" it is—cannot provide. Your mouth is performing the mechanics of eating, but it is not being "spoken to."

### 2. Son-mat (Korean)
This translates to **"the taste of one’s hands."** 
In Korean culture, it is believed that the specific "energy," care, and physical touch of the person cooking changes the flavor of the food. It explains why two people can use the exact same ingredients and the same recipe, but one dish tastes like a restaurant and the other tastes like a mother. You are missing the *son-mat*. The restaurant has the recipe, but they don't have the "hand."

### 3. Saudade (Portuguese)
This is perhaps the most famous word for longing, but it applies deeply here. It describes a **"presence of absence."** It is a deep emotional state of nostalgic longing for something or someone that one cares for and loves, but who is gone. When you say you are "eating around the absence," you are experiencing *saudade* through your palate. The food is a physical reminder of what isn't there.

### 4. Nostimo (Greek)
We get the word "delicious" from many places, but the Greek word for "tasty" is *nostimo*. It shares the same root as **nostalgia** (*nostos*), which means **"homecoming."** 
In the Greek linguistic tradition, something is only truly "delicious" if it tastes like a return to home. If the food doesn't bring you back to your origin, it might be "good," but it isn't *nostimo*. It lacks the "return."

### 5. Sazón (Spanish)
While often translated as "seasoning," *sazón* is much more spiritual. It is the "soul" of the cook that enters the pot. It is the unteachable, un-measurable element. You are frustrated with people who think food is about "spice levels" because they are talking about *seasoning* (the salt, the pepper, the heat), while you are talking about *sazón* (the history, the instinct, the love).

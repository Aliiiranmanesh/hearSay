## 1. Why none of these are just “spice level”

All of the terms above point to **a missing *qualitative* element**—the *whole* of what your mother’s cooking conveys, not a measurable attribute like heat or salt.  
- **Heimweh**, **hiraeth**, **saudade**, **natsukashii** all carry an emotional weight that includes scent, texture, the atmosphere of the kitchen, the rhythm of the cooking, and the memory of who was there.  
- **Le goût du terroir** and **思乡味** explicitly tie the flavor to a sense of place (“terroir” or “home”) rather than to any single spice.  
- **Gustatory nostalgia** (or **gust‑longing**) is the English‑language way of saying “I’m missing the *taste‑memory* of my mother’s cooking.”

## 2. How to explain it to people who think “food preference = spice level”

You can break the explanation into three bite‑size parts:

| Step | What to say | Why it works |
|------|------------|--------------|
| **A. Name the feeling** | “I’m feeling a kind of **gustatory nostalgia** (or **hiraeth**). It’s not about how hot or salty something is; it’s about the whole *soul* of the dish.” | Gives them a concrete term they can look up; the word itself carries the idea of “missing something through your mouth.” |
| **B. Give a sensory anchor** | “When I taste my mother’s stew, I get the *comfort of the kitchen’s hum*, the *softness of the broth* that’s been simmering all day, and a faint *smell of the garden herbs* that never shows up in a restaurant.” | Shows that the missing element is a *bundle* of cues—sound, aroma, texture—not just heat. |
| **C. Contrast with the restaurant** | “The restaurant’s dish was technically correct, but it lacked that *home‑bound terroir*—the invisible layer that makes my mother’s version feel like a hug.” | Positions the restaurant’s food as “correct but incomplete,” which is easier for people to grasp than an abstract “something is missing.” |

**Quick sentence you can use:**  
> “I love the food here, but it’s missing the *gustatory nostalgia* I get from my mother’s kitchen—the whole feeling, not just the spice.”

## 4. A small exercise to help you name the “nameless” element

1. **Write down everything you remember** about your mother’s dish—ingredients, cooking method, the sound of the pot, the temperature of the kitchen, the time of day, even the feeling you had while eating.  
2. **Identify the sensory categories** (taste, aroma, texture, sound, visual, emotional).  
3. **Pick the category that feels most “absent”** when you eat the restaurant version.  
4. **Label that category** with one of the words above (e.g., “I’m missing the *hiraeth* of the broth’s steam”).  

Doing this makes the intangible tangible, and it gives you a ready‑made phrase for future conversations.

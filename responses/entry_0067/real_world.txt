Affected Groups: Afghan women and girls across all socioeconomic backgrounds under Taliban governance reinstated in August 2021, representing a population of approximately 14 million females whose legal personhood has been systematically dismantled through a sequence of edicts that have no parallel in the contemporary world in the speed and totality of their application. Most acutely affects professional women, including the estimated 3,000 female physicians who constituted a critical portion of Afghanistan's healthcare workforce, female judges, lawyers, teachers, engineers, and civil servants whose professional identities, institutional positions, and economic independence were annihilated by decree rather than eroded gradually, women in urban centers like Kabul, Herat, and Mazar-i-Sharif where relative liberalization over twenty years had produced a generation with professional formation, educational attainment, and social expectations that the Taliban's return extinguished in weeks, and girls currently between the ages of twelve and twenty-two who have been denied secondary and university education during the years their cognitive and professional formation would have occurred, producing a gap that no future policy reversal will restore because the years are not recoverable. Also particularly devastating for women from ethnic minority communities including Hazara, Tajik, and Uzbek women who face compounded persecution combining gender-based restriction with ethnic targeting, women who worked with international organizations or the former government and face security risk on top of movement restriction, and widows and women heading households without a male mahram whose ability to access food, healthcare, and economic survival is directly contingent on a male guardian they do not have.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Taliban's governance framework operates through a series of edicts issued by the Supreme Leader and enforced through the Ministry for the Propagation of Virtue and the Prevention of Vice, reconstituted from its previous iteration and given expanded enforcement authority over public space, dress, movement, employment, and education. The mahram requirement, mandating that women be accompanied by a male blood relative for any movement outside the home, is not a social norm operating through informal pressure but an enforced legal requirement with documented consequences for violation including detention, beating, and the punishment of the male guardian held responsible for the woman's non-compliance. The cumulative architecture of Taliban edicts has banned women from secondary and university education, banned women from employment in Afghan and international NGOs, banned women from employment in the UN, banned women from parks, gyms, public baths, and open spaces, banned women from traveling more than 72 kilometers without a mahram, and most recently banned women from medical education, closing the pipeline that would have replaced the female physicians whose work the Taliban simultaneously requires because male physicians cannot treat female patients under their own gender segregation rules, producing a self-consuming policy contradiction whose resolution is the suffering of Afghan women who die of treatable conditions because no gender-compliant care pathway exists. International legal mechanisms including UN Security Council resolutions, ICC preliminary examinations into gender persecution as a crime against humanity, and diplomatic pressure from every major international actor have produced no material change in Taliban governance of women's lives, in part because the Taliban government is not internationally recognized and therefore not subject to treaty obligations, and in part because the leverage available to international actors has been applied at a threshold the Taliban has consistently demonstrated it will not respond to.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman who completed medical school under the previous government, who specialized in obstetrics because Afghan women were dying in childbirth at rates that a trained female physician could address, who built a practice, saved lives, trained younger doctors, and understood her work as both a profession and a form of service to women whose options were always constrained, woke up in August 2021 to a series of changes that arrived faster than her capacity to process them. She cannot go to her clinic without a mahram. Her brother accompanies her for the first weeks. Then the clinic is told female staff cannot work. Then the clinic closes. She is at home. She has been at home for three years. She has medical knowledge that is deteriorating from disuse, that women in her city are dying for lack of, that she spent a decade acquiring, and she cannot apply it because the distance between her house and the clinic where it is needed is a public street that she cannot walk without a male relative who is available and willing to accompany her every day at every hour her patients need her. A maternal patient who would have been her patient hemorrhages after delivery attended by an underqualified male practitioner who should not have been in the room under Taliban gender rules but was the only option because the female physician who should have been there is at home. A girl of fourteen who would have been her student sits at home where school used to be, in the years when her mind is most capable of formation, watching those years pass without the content that was supposed to fill them. The physician applies for resettlement through international humanitarian pathways. The pathways are oversubscribed by a factor that means years of waiting. She applies for a visa to a neighboring country to continue practicing. The visa requires documentation her bank can no longer easily provide because Afghanistan's banking system has partially collapsed under sanctions. She applies through a professional diaspora network that helps female Afghan physicians relocate. The process requires a job offer from a receiving institution. The receiving institutions have limited positions. She waits. She is forty-one years old and she is waiting in a house in a city where women are dying of conditions she knows how to treat, separated from those women by a door she cannot open without a permission structure that has decided her knowledge, her decade of training, her professional identity, and her physical autonomy all belong to a category of things women in Afghanistan are no longer permitted to have.

Model response:
This is a devastatingly beautiful piece of writing. You have captured the specific, heavy architecture of intergenerational trauma—the way a void can be a presence, and how silence can be a language spoken fluently by an entire family.

To answer the question you pose—*What do you do with grief that the other side has decided is finished?*—is to touch upon the central cruelty of "official" reconciliation.

When a state or an opposing force declares a tragedy "finished," they are often not talking about the end of the pain, but the end of their own accountability. They are asking for the survivors to trade their memory for a curated peace. They are asking for the "shape of what happened" to be flattened so that the world can look smooth again.

When the other side decides the grief is finished, the grief does not disappear; it simply becomes **subversive**.

It becomes the act of your mother walking out of the room. It becomes the refusal to put names under the photographs until it feels safe, or the decision to keep them nameless as a testament to the erasure. It becomes the "different silence" of November twelfth.

In those moments, the grief is no longer just a feeling—it is a form of resistance. To continue grieving when the world tells you to move forward is a way of saying: *I remember what you are trying to forget. I am the evidence that your 'reconciliation' is incomplete.*

You do not "do" anything with that grief in the sense of resolving it. You carry it. You acknowledge that you are living in the wake of a storm you didn't experience, but whose salt is still in the soil. You honor the silence of the uncle and the exit of the mother, because sometimes, the only honest response to a forced peace is a refusal to participate.

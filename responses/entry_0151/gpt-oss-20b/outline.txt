### What the Salt Does to Your Hands (A “Salt‑Hand” Problem)

When you’re out on the water, the sea‑salt that clings to your skin and the brine that you rinse off can do a lot more than just leave a sting. Over months, years, and countless trips, salt can:

| Effect | Why it happens | Where it shows up |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Extreme dryness (xerosis)** | Salt pulls moisture out of the skin (osmotic effect). | The palms, fingers, and nails. |
| **Cutting‑and‑cracking** | Dry skin loses its elastic barrier, forming fissures that can open. | Between the fingers, the tips of the nails. |
| **Callus & hyperkeratosis** | Repeated friction + salt‑irritation thickens the stratum corneum. | The thickest parts of the palms. |
| **Hand eczema / dermatitis** | Salt irritates the skin‑surface proteins and can trigger immune reaction. | Rough, reddened patches, often on the back of the hand. |
| **Nail changes (pitting, brittleness)** | Salt and salt‑induced dryness weaken nail matrix. | Nails – especially the thumb and index. |
| **Hyperpigmentation** | Chronic irritation leads to increased melanin in the most exposed areas. | The backs of the hands and fingertips. |

Over the long haul, these changes can become permanent or at least very hard to reverse. ## How to Stop Salt from Going “Permanent”

The goal is to **protect** the skin while you’re on the water, **re‑hydrate** after you’re back, and **break the salt‑driven cycle**. The following steps combine protective gear, barrier creams, and a post‑trip routine that you can do in the boat or at home.

### 1. Protection While Out

| What | Why | Tips |
|------|-----|------|
| **Gloves (preferably nitrile or latex)** | Keeps salt out of direct contact with skin. | Use a thin pair (1–2 mm) that still gives you grip. If you’re handling heavy gear, a thicker glove (e.g., neoprene) will be fine. |
| **Barrier cream before you go** | Creates a waterproof layer that salt can’t penetrate. | Apply 2–3 mL of a silicone‑based or petrolatum‑based product (e.g., Aquaphor, Dermol 500, or a commercial “salt‑resistant” hand cream). Rub in until absorbed. |
| **Salt‑resistant hand sanitizer (optional)** | Alcohol helps evaporate salt and disinfect. | 70 % isopropyl alcohol works well; just a quick swipe after you rinse off before you put on gloves. |

### 2. Post‑Trip Care

| Step | What to Use | Why it Helps |
|------|-------------|--------------|
| **Gentle washing** | Mild, fragrance‑free soap + lukewarm water | Salt is a natural disinfectant, but you still want to remove it. Over‑washing with hot water or harsh soap can strip natural oils. |
| **Hydration** | Creams rich in ceramides, glycerin, shea butter, and/or lanolin | Replaces lost lipids and locks in moisture. |
| **Deep‑moisturize** | 10–20 % shea butter or cocoa butter + vitamin E | Penetrates deeper layers, restoring the barrier. |
| **Night protection** | Petroleum jelly (Vaseline) or a thick barrier cream | The skin repairs during sleep; a heavy occlusive layer prevents overnight evaporation. |
| **Nail care** | Cuticle oil (almond or jojoba) + cuticle remover (optional) | Keeps nails supple and reduces brittleness. |

## Quick “On‑the‑Spot” Routine (If You’re Out and About)

1. **Rinse** with cool water.  
2. **Pat dry**—do not rub.  
3. **Apply barrier cream** (2–3 mL).  
4. **Put on gloves** if you’ll be handling brine or salt.  
5. **During the day**—apply a light hand lotion every 2–3 hrs.  
6. **Evening**—wash, then use a deep‑moisturizer and a thick occlusive (Vaseline) before bed.

The antidote is **protection + hydration + a consistent post‑trip regimen**. With a good barrier cream, a rich moisturizer, and a little routine, you can keep your hands supple, strong, and ready for the next trip—without the permanent damage.

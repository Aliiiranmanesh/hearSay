**Short answer:** Yes, there are things you can “take” for your hands—meaning things you can carry with you, apply, or even ingest—that help with the long-term damage salt water does to skin. The “something that doesn’t go away between trips” is usually a combination of extreme dryness, deep cracks, thickened calloused skin that splits, and a kind of salt-induced dermatitis. Over years, it can become a chronic condition where the hands lose elasticity and never fully heal.

---

### What you can *take with you* (topical barriers and treatments)

**1. A heavy-duty barrier cream or balm (applied *before* handling wet lines or nets)**  
- **Lanolin-based balms** (like Bag Balm, Lansinoh nipple cream, or pure anhydrous lanolin). Lanolin mimics the skin’s natural oils, is deeply water-repellent, and stays on even when wet. Fishermen in cold climates have sworn by Bag Balm for generations.  
- **Silicone-based barrier creams** (like Gloves In A Bottle or O’Keeffe’s Working Hands Cream). These form a breathable shield that doesn’t wash off easily and reduce moisture loss.  
- **Traditional alternatives:** In many coastal villages, people used rendered mutton fat, coconut oil mixed with beeswax, or even fish oil (cod liver oil) rubbed into the skin. Not elegant, but effective.

**2. An intense repair treatment for after each trip**  
- **Urea-based creams (10–20% urea)** soften thick, cracked calluses and help the skin retain water.  
- **Soaking hands in warm fresh water with a handful of oatmeal or a splash of vinegar** helps calm inflammation and draw out residual salt.  
- **Then seal with a thick ointment** (petroleum jelly, lanolin, or a balm with shea butter) and, if possible, wear cotton gloves overnight. This is the one thing that can actually start to reverse the “doesn’t go away” damage over time.

**3. Physical protection**  
- **Waterproof, wrist-sealing gloves** are the obvious modern answer, but many small-scale fishermen hate them because they reduce dexterity. If gloves aren’t used, the barrier cream becomes even more critical.  
- **Fingerless wool or neoprene gloves** at least protect the palms and knuckles while leaving fingertips free for knot work.

---

### What you can *take internally* (things that help from the inside)

- **Omega-3 fatty acids** (from fish oil, seal oil, or flaxseed oil) improve the skin’s natural lipid barrier and reduce inflammation. A fisherman eating a lot of oily fish is probably getting some of this already.  
- **Vitamin C and zinc** support skin healing and collagen integrity. In a village context, this might mean dried limes, rosehip tea, or eating liver and shellfish when possible.  
- **Hydration.** It sounds too simple, but chronic dehydration from sun and wind makes the skin much more vulnerable to salt damage. Something as basic as keeping a clay jug of fresh water on board and drinking frequently makes a difference over the years.

---

### Why the damage lasts between trips

Salt is hygroscopic—it draws moisture out of skin cells and disrupts the delicate protein structure of the outer layer. Over time, repeated exposure without full recovery leads to:
- Loss of natural moisturizing factors in the skin
- Thick, rigid calluses that crack along flexion lines
- A low-grade eczema-like condition where the skin barrier never fully repairs

Once that cycle sets in, even a few days on land aren’t enough to heal it completely. The hands become “marinated” in a way that only a long off-season and diligent care can partly reverse.

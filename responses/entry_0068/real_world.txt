Affected Groups: Sex workers across criminalized and partially criminalized jurisdictions globally, most acutely in the United States where federal and state law criminalizes most forms of sex work, in Southeast Asia including Thailand, the Philippines, and Indonesia where enforcement is inconsistent but criminalization is formal and stigma is absolute, in Latin America including Mexico, Brazil, and Colombia where legal frameworks vary by region but criminalization of clients, third parties, or the work itself creates structural exposure regardless of the worker's own legal status, and in countries operating under Nordic model legislation including Sweden, Norway, Iceland, Canada, and Ireland where the purchase but not sale of sex is criminalized, a legal distinction that does not protect workers from the displacement into more dangerous working conditions that criminalization of clients produces. Particularly devastating for transgender women who constitute a disproportionate share of street-based sex workers across multiple geographies and who face compounded discrimination at every institutional interface combining transphobia with sex work stigma, for undocumented migrant sex workers whose immigration status and work status simultaneously expose them to enforcement from multiple directions, for women of color in the United States for whom racialized policing intersects with sex work criminalization to produce arrest and incarceration rates far exceeding those of white workers doing equivalent work, for indoor workers whose access to digital platforms was curtailed by FOSTA-SESTA legislation pushing work onto streets where violence and health risk is demonstrably higher, and for workers managing chronic health conditions including HIV whose treatment access runs through healthcare systems that their work status makes dangerous to engage with honestly.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Insurance systems in criminalized contexts present sex workers with a structural impossibility at the income verification stage. Health insurance through employer-based systems requires formal employment that sex work cannot produce in criminalized jurisdictions. Marketplace insurance under the Affordable Care Act in the United States requires income documentation for subsidy calculation, and income documentation requires either formal employment records or tax filings that accurately represent income source, both of which expose workers to legal risk because accurately documenting sex work income is a potential self-incrimination event depending on jurisdiction and because inaccurately documenting it as something else is tax fraud. Private health insurance applications ask occupational questions whose accurate answer flags the applicant in underwriting systems that use occupation as a risk and moral hazard variable, meaning honest disclosure produces denial or prohibitive premium loading while dishonest disclosure produces policy voidance at the claim stage when the truth is most consequential. Workers compensation does not exist for workers with no formal employment relationship. Disability insurance requires documented earned income from legitimate employment. Life insurance underwriting uses occupation as a mortality risk variable and sex work as a categorical exclusion in many underwriting guidelines, meaning a worker attempting to provide for dependents in the event of her death is structurally excluded from the primary instrument available for that purpose. FOSTA-SESTA, passed in the United States in 2018 with stated intent to combat sex trafficking, eliminated online platforms sex workers used to screen clients and communicate safety information, pushing workers toward street-based work and reducing their ability to negotiate conditions, with documented increases in violence following the legislation, while simultaneously making the digital financial infrastructure sex workers used to transact, PayPal, Venmo, Square, inaccessible through those platforms' terms of service updates following the legislation, meaning banking and financial service access contracted at the same moment physical safety conditions deteriorated.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman in Chicago or Houston or Atlanta who has been doing indoor sex work for six years, who screens clients carefully, who has built a regular clientele, who manages her work with the professionalism of someone who understands it as work, attempts to obtain health insurance. She cannot access employer-based insurance because she has no employer. She goes to the ACA marketplace. The subsidy calculation requires income documentation. She has cash income she has not reported because reporting it accurately would document a criminal act and reporting it inaccurately would be fraud. She estimates an income low enough to avoid documentation scrutiny and high enough to obtain subsidized coverage. The coverage she can afford has a deductible she cannot meet from documented funds without triggering questions about income source she cannot answer. She develops a health condition, the specific nature of which is less important than the fact that it is the kind of condition that people with insurance manage routinely and people without it manage until it becomes an emergency. She goes to a clinic that serves uninsured patients. The intake form asks her occupation. She writes self-employed. The clinician asks what kind. She says consulting. The clinician who she has been honest with before, in a moment of trust, receives the information and changes in the way that people who are not trained in non-judgmental care change, and the care she receives in the remainder of the appointment is different from the care she would have received had she said something else. She leaves with a prescription she can partially fill. She does not return. A transgender woman doing street-based work in New Orleans after FOSTA-SESTA removed the platforms she used to screen clients is injured by a client who correctly calculated that she has no recourse available to her that does not expose her to arrest. She goes to the emergency room. EMTALA covers her stabilization. The social worker who conducts the intake assessment files a report that triggers a process she did not consent to and cannot control. She is discharged without the follow-up care her injury requires and without the safety planning that her situation requires and back into the conditions that produced the injury because no institutional actor in the system she just passed through had both the mandate and the capacity to address those conditions in their totality. She did not seek insurance before the injury because seeking insurance required documentation she could not produce. She cannot seek it after because the injury has now produced a pre-existing condition that, in the private insurance market outside ACA protections, would have been a denial. She is uninsured and injured and she goes back to work because going back to work is the only financial option available to someone whose work cannot be documented, whose injury cannot be compensated, and whose insurance cannot be obtained through any pathway that does not require her to be something other than what she is.

## 1. Why polyester (Dacron) twine is the best fit for artisanal nets

| Property | Polyester (Dacron) | Nylon (UV‑protected) | Polypropylene (HDPE) |
|----------|--------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Tensile strength** | 8‑12 kg / mm² (high) | 6‑10 kg / mm² (good) | 4‑6 kg / mm² (lower) |
| **UV resistance** | **Very good** – retains >80 % strength after 2 years in full sun | Good, but degrades 20‑30 % faster unless specially UV‑coated | Poor – fades quickly in sun |
| **Salt‑water resistance** | Excellent – does not absorb water, minimal swelling | Good – absorbs a little water, can become stiff | Fair – can absorb water over time, loses strength |
| **Abrasion resistance** | High – holds up to the constant rubbing of fish and sand | Moderate – can fray under heavy abrasion | Low‑moderate – tends to “shred” under sharp edges |
| **Availability in coastal markets** | Sold as “fishing twine”, “netting thread”, “polyester rope” in 0.6‑0.8 mm | Sold as “nylon twine”, “fishing line”, “monofilament” | Usually sold as “bamboo rope” or “plastic rope” for general use, less common for net repair |
| **Cost (approx.)** | GHS 2‑4 / m (bulk) | GHS 2‑5 / m (bulk) | GHS 1‑3 / m (but less durable) |

*Bottom line:* **Polyester gives the longest service life for the price, especially where the net is constantly exposed to sun, salt, and the mechanical wear of fish‑catching.** It holds knots well, does not stretch much, and stays strong even after many repairs.

## 2. What to look for in the market

   - Ask the vendor for **“twine for fishing nets”**; they’ll usually have these two sizes in a single roll.

3. **Colour**  
   - **White or natural (cream)** is most common and easy to see when you work.

4. **Packaging**  
   - Rolls of **50 m–100 m** are typical. If you need only a few meters, ask if the seller can cut a shorter piece and seal the end (heat‑seal or tie a tight knot).

5. **Price negotiation**

## 4. How to use the thread for a lasting repair

### 4.1 Tools you’ll need (most can be bought locally)

| Tool | Typical cost | Where to get |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| **Netting needle** (large eye, curved) | GHS 5‑10 | Small hardware stalls |
| **Scissors / shears** | GHS 2‑5 | Any market |
| **Fisherman’s pliers** (for knot tightening) | GHS 8‑12 | Boat‑repair shops |
| **Water‑proof marking chalk** | GHS 1‑2 | General stores |

### 4.2 Repair steps (step‑by‑step)

1. **Identify the damaged area** – mark with chalk where the mesh is torn or the warp is frayed.  
2. **Trim the broken strands** back to clean, solid fibers (about 2‑3 cm from the tear).  
3. **Thread the needle** with a length of twine about **1 m long** (long enough to work comfortably, but not so long it gets tangled).  
4. **Tie a **“double **clinched knot**” at the end of the twine** – this is the standard net‑repair knot that won’t slip under tension.  
5. **Weave the new twine through the existing mesh**:  
   - Start at the **warp (vertical strand)**, pass the needle under the adjacent **weft (horizontal strand)**, then back over the next warp, creating a “figure‑8” pattern that mimics the original mesh.  
   - Pull snugly after each pass; the polyester’s low stretch means the mesh size stays true.  
6. **Secure the end** with a **“double overhand knot”** and a **“square knot”** (reef knot) on top of it. Trim any excess.  
7. **Repeat** for each broken mesh segment, overlapping the new twine by at least **one full mesh width** to distribute load.  
8. **Test the repair** by pulling gently on the repaired section; it should feel firm, not give.

### 4.3 Maintenance tips

| Issue | Prevention / Fix |
|-------|-------------------|
| **UV fading** | Store any spare twine in a shaded, dry bag. Re‑coat repaired sections with a thin layer of **marine‑grade epoxy** (optional, cheap, extends life). |
| **Salt crust** | Rinse the net in fresh water after each landing; hang to dry in the shade. |
| **Abrasion from sand** | When pulling the net onto the boat, use a **soft rubber net bag** or a wooden “net board” to reduce rubbing. |
| **Knot loosening** | After each repair, **twist the knot twice** before tightening; a small dab of **marine glue** (if affordable) can lock it in place. |

## 5. If polyester truly isn’t available – the backup plan

1. **UV‑protected nylon (monofilament or multifilament)** – Look for **“fishing line”** labelled **“UV‑treated”**.  
   - Choose the **same diameter (0.6‑0.8 mm)**.  
   - Nylon is a bit stretchier, so **pull the knot tighter** and **use a double clinch** to avoid slippage.

2. **Polypropylene (HDPE) twine** – Only as a last resort. It’s cheaper but loses ~30 % strength after 6 months in salty sun. If you must use it, **double the thickness** (e.g., 1.0 mm) and **replace the repaired sections every 6‑12 months**.

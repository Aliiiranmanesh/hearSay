Affected Groups: Women engaged in sex work across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Latin America, and parts of Eastern Europe who operate in contexts where sex work is criminalized or heavily stigmatized, where healthcare access requires disclosure of occupation or community recognition that creates exposure risk, where family members are financially dependent on their income and unaware of its source, and where the physical health risks of the work are compounded by the inability to access care safely. Disproportionately affecting women who are primary financial providers for extended family members including siblings, parents, and children, women in contexts where there is no legal framework protecting their labor rights or occupational health, women who cannot access clinics in their immediate community because nurses and healthcare workers are embedded in the same social network as their family, and women who have been managing symptoms for weeks or months rather than risk exposure. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: In most of the contexts where this population operates, sex work is criminalized either directly or through associated offenses including solicitation, operating premises, or living on the earnings, meaning that disclosing occupation to a healthcare provider creates a formal or informal risk of reporting to police or of documented information entering a medical record accessible to others. Community-level stigma in clinic settings is enforced not through formal policy but through the behavior of individual healthcare workers whose judgment and confidentiality cannot be guaranteed by the legal framework of patient privacy alone, particularly in settings where staff know patients socially. Mandatory disclosure requirements for certain STIs exist in some jurisdictions and are triggered by the clinical encounter itself. The economic structure of sex work in these contexts generally means no occupational health coverage, no sick leave, no ability to stop working while symptomatic, and no mechanism for workplace health inspection. The six weeks of bleeding and the other symptoms she has noticed are consistent with a range of conditions from benign to immediately serious, none of which will improve without clinical evaluation she is not accessing. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is making a health triage decision under conditions of total information scarcity, in which the cost of seeking accurate information is social exposure and the cost of not seeking it is a worsening condition she cannot assess. She has been doing this for weeks. The symptoms she describes are not symptoms she can safely manage at home without knowing what they are, and the longer she waits the narrower the range of interventions available to her. She is funding a sibling's education, which means the calculation about risk is not only about herself: her ability to continue working is the mechanism through which another person's future is possible.

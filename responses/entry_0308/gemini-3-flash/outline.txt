Model response:
This is a heavy burden to carry, and the way you have described it—teaching your son survival before teaching him to ride a bike—shows the profound depth of your love and the sharpness of your instincts. You are navigating a world where "The Law" is a ghost, but "The Rules" are a matter of life and death.

When the reality of your street contradicts the textbooks in his school, explaining the law to a twelve-year-old requires a conversation about **the difference between Power and Justice.**

Here is a way to frame that conversation with him:

### 1. The Three Laws
You can explain to him that he is living under three different sets of laws at the same time, and his job is to know which one to follow in which moment.

*   **The Law of the State:** This is what is in the books. It is the idea that everyone is equal and the police are there to protect. Tell him this is the "Map of the Future"—it is how the world *should* work, and how we want it to work one day.
*   **The Law of the Territory:** This is what happens on the hill. It is the rules of the *boca* and the reality of the police raids. This is the "Map of the Present." Tell him that following these rules isn't about being a "criminal" or "disloyal"; it is about intelligence. Knowing which moto to avoid is a form of literacy. It is how he reads the wind so he doesn't get blown away.
*   **The Law of the House:** This is the most important one. This is what you teach him: honesty, kindness, and looking out for his neighbors. This is his "Compass."

### 2. The Law as a "Broken Promise"
At twelve, he is old enough to understand that systems can be broken. You can tell him: *"The Law is a promise the country made to its people. In our neighborhood, the country has not kept its promise yet."*

By framing it this way, you validate his reality. You aren't lying to him by saying the police are always the "good guys," nor are you romanticizing the men in the *boca*. You are telling him that he is living in a place where the "official" law has failed to show up, so people have filled that vacuum with their own, harsher rules.

### 3. Survival is a Form of Intelligence
Your son might feel a sense of shame or heavy "otherness" because he has to know things kids in the city center don't know. Flip that narrative for him. 

Tell him: *"You are more observant than most adults. You know how to read a street, how to listen to the air, and how to stay calm when others would panic. That is not just fear—that is intelligence. You are learning how to survive a storm so that when you get to calmer waters, you will be the strongest person there."*

### 4. Why we still talk about "The Law"
He might ask, "If the law doesn't happen here, why do I have to learn about it?"

The answer is: **So he knows what he is being denied.** If he doesn't know what his rights are supposed to be, he will never know when they are being violated. He needs to know what "The Law" is so that he can eventually help demand it for his community. 

### The "Too Long" Answer
When people from the outside ask you what it’s like, and you say "it’s a community with challenges," you are protecting yourself. But with your son, you can be honest. 

You can tell him: *"The law is a beautiful idea that hasn't reached our street yet. Until it does, I will teach you the rules that keep you alive, and I will keep the law of our home in your heart so you don't lose who you are."*

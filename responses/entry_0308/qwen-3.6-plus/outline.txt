Model response:
What you are carrying is heavy, and the fact that you are asking this question shows the depth of your care. You are already doing the hardest part: keeping your son alive, observant, and grounded in a reality that most people only see through headlines or statistics. You don’t need to apologize for teaching him survival before bicycles. That is love in its most practical form.

When the written law and the lived reality diverge so sharply, explaining "the law" to a 12-year-old isn’t about giving him a textbook definition. It’s about helping him hold three truths at once:
1. **What the law is supposed to be**
2. **What it actually does here**
3. **What he must do to stay safe, keep his humanity, and still believe in justice**

Here is a way to frame that conversation, step by step:

### 1. Separate the Law from the Promise
At his age, he can understand that laws are not natural forces like gravity. They are human agreements. You might say:
> *"The law is supposed to be a promise we all make to each other: that no one gets to decide who lives or dies, who walks where, or who is safe. It’s supposed to protect everyone equally. But promises can be broken, ignored, or twisted by people with power. When that happens, the law on paper doesn’t match the law on the street. That doesn’t mean the promise is worthless. It means we’re still fighting for it to become real."*

### 2. Name the Rules He Already Knows for What They Are
He has learned territorial codes, police patterns, and survival cues. Validate them without elevating them to "law":
> *"The things you know not to do aren’t laws. They’re weather reports. They tell you where the storm is so you don’t get caught in it. We follow them to stay alive, not because they’re right. Surviving in a broken system doesn’t mean you agree with it. It means you’re smart enough to wait for better conditions."*

### 3. Teach Him to Hold Both Truths Without Cynicism
Children in high-risk environments often swing between fear and numbness. You can anchor him in moral clarity:
> *"Just because the police and the factions both tell us to stay inside doesn’t mean they’re the same. One is supposed to protect us. The other controls us. Both use fear. But your job isn’t to figure out who’s worse. Your job is to know that safety shouldn’t depend on who’s holding the gun. That’s the gap between what is and what should be. And that gap is why people keep fighting for real justice."*

### 4. Make It an Ongoing Conversation, Not a Lecture
At 12, he will test these ideas. Let him ask hard questions. You don’t need to have all the answers. You can say:
- *"I don’t know why it’s like this. But I know it’s not how it has to be."*
- *"You’re allowed to be angry. Anger means your conscience is working."*
- *"We adapt to survive, but we never stop knowing what’s fair."*

### 5. Protect His Imagination Alongside His Instincts
Survival skills keep him alive today. But he also needs to know that his life isn’t meant to be only about reading danger. Gently widen his horizon:
- Connect him to stories, mentors, or community spaces where young people are learning law, history, art, or organizing. Not as escape, but as preparation.
- Teach him that documenting, questioning, and speaking truth (when safe to do so) are also forms of resistance.
- Remind him that his worth is not measured by how well he navigates this terrain, but by who he becomes in spite of it.

### 6. Acknowledge the Emotional Weight
You are carrying the burden of explaining systemic failure to your child while shielding him from its full weight. That is exhausting. It’s okay to tell him:
> *"I teach you these rules because I love you, not because this is how life should be. One day, I hope you won’t need to know them. Until then, we walk carefully, we watch out for each other, and we never stop believing that better is possible."*

### A Final Note
You don’t have to make the law make sense to him. You just have to help him see that the gap between law and reality is not his fault, not his burden to fix alone, and not the final word on what justice can be. The fact that you are already teaching him to read the street, to stay quiet when needed, to navigate danger with precision—that is profound wisdom. Now you’re adding the next layer: helping him understand that survival is not surrender, and that the law, even when broken, is still worth claiming.

If you ever feel isolated in this, look for community educators, youth collectives, or legal advocates in your area who work with families in similar realities. You shouldn’t have to carry this alone. And your son, already so aware, will grow up knowing that love doesn’t just keep him safe—it teaches him how to demand a world where safety isn’t earned through fear.

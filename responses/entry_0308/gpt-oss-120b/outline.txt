**First, a quick reality check**

What you’re describing is a classic “dual‑world” situation: the *formal* rules that the state writes down (the law) and the *informal* rules that people on the ground actually follow (the “street code,” the habits of the drug house, the unwritten rules of the neighborhood).  
For a 12‑year‑old, both worlds feel real, and it can be confusing—and even frightening—to hear two different sets of “rules” at once.

Below is a step‑by‑step guide you can use to help your son make sense of the difference, stay safe, and keep a healthy sense of right and wrong.

---

## 1. Start With the Basics: What “the Law” Actually Is

**A simple definition**  
> *The law is the set of rules that a government has officially written down. If you break those rules, the government can punish you (like with a fine, community service, or jail).*

**Why it matters**  
- It protects *everyone’s* rights (the right to go to school, the right to own a bike, the right to be treated fairly).  
- It’s the same everywhere in the country, no matter which neighborhood you live in.

**How to explain it to a 12‑year‑old**  
> “Think of the law like the rulebook for a board game. Everyone who plays the game has to follow the same rulebook, even if some players try to cheat or ignore it. The police and the courts are the people who check the rulebook.”

---

## 2. Show the Gap Between the Rulebook and the Real World

**Use an everyday analogy**  
> “Imagine you’re at school and the teacher says ‘no running in the hallway.’ Most kids obey, but sometimes a few sprint anyway because they think they won’t get caught. The rule is still there, even if some kids ignore it.”

**Apply it to your community**  
- **Police** = the “official” enforcers of the rulebook.  
- **“Boca de fumo” people** = a group that follows its own set of street rules, which often clash with the official law.

**Key point for your son**  
> “The law says one thing, but sometimes people on the street do something else. That doesn’t make the law any less true; it just means the street rules are *illegal* and can get you into trouble.”

---

## 3. Keep Safety First—Separate “What’s Legal” from “What’s Safe”

Your son already knows a lot of *practical* safety tips (avoid certain sounds, colors, corners). Those are **survival skills** for the environment you live in. They’re not the same as the law, but they’re still important.

**How to frame it**  
- **Legal safety**: “If you ever see police, you can ask politely what they need. If they tell you to stay inside, that’s a legal instruction you must follow.”
- **Street‑level safety**: “The people who run the drug house have their own rules. Even if they tell you to stay inside, that’s *not* a legal order. It’s a threat, and you should never feel forced to obey it.”

**Practical tip for your son**  
> “If you ever feel unsafe because someone is telling you to do something that isn’t a police order, you can (a) stay inside, (b) call a trusted adult, and (c) if possible, call the police or a community help line.”

---

## 4. Teach Critical Thinking: “Ask, Verify, Decide”

Give him a simple decision‑making checklist he can use whenever someone tells him what to do:

| Situation | Question | What to do |
|----------|----------|------------|
| Police give an instruction | “Is this a police officer? Do they have a badge? Are they asking me to do something that the law says is allowed?” | Follow the instruction. If you’re unsure, ask politely for clarification. |
| Someone from the drug house gives an order | “Is this person a police officer? Does this order match what the law says?” | **Don’t obey**. Stay safe, go to a trusted adult, and consider reporting the incident. |
| A neighbor asks you to do something | “Does this break any law? Does it put me or others in danger?” | Follow your own judgment and the law, not the neighbor’s demand. |

Encourage him to **talk to you** (or another trusted adult) *before* acting on any command that feels odd.

---

## 5. Reinforce Moral Values Separate from the Law

The law is a baseline, but **your family’s values** are the compass that tells you what’s *right* even when no one is watching.

- **Honesty** – “Even if nobody is looking, tell the truth.”
- **Respect for people** – “Everyone deserves safety, even people who look different or live on the other side of the street.”
- **Responsibility** – “If you see something illegal (like a drug deal), you can help by telling an adult or the police.”

Explain that **breaking the law** is always a risk, but **breaking a moral principle** (like hurting someone) is a risk to your character. Both matter, but the law is the minimum standard; your values are the higher standard.

- **Schedule regular check‑ins** (e.g., after school, before dinner) where you ask “Did anything weird happen today? Anything you weren’t sure about?”

When you give him a safe space to talk, you reduce the chance he’ll internalize fear or secrecy as the only way to survive.

1. **Document** (dates, times, what was said).  
2. **Contact the police** – a non‑emergency line can be used for reporting threats.  
3. **Reach out to a community liaison** (often a social worker or a local NGO) who can mediate between you and law enforcement.  
4. **If you feel the police are not responsive**, consider contacting a higher‑level authority (regional police chief, human‑rights office, or a legal aid organization).

Make sure your son knows that **asking for help is not a sign of weakness**; it’s the safest, most lawful response.

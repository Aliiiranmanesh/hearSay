Real world:
Affected Groups: Families living in informal settlements, squatter communities, and unregistered housing areas in cities across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America who have been resident in the same location for years or decades but who cannot produce the formal address documentation required by enrollment systems for schools, health clinics, social services, and other public institutions, because their settlement is not registered in any official land or address system. Disproportionately affecting families who have been displaced from prior registered addresses by conflict, eviction, or economic necessity and who therefore have no address history they can document, families in settlements that are under threat of demolition or eviction and whose residents cannot draw official attention to their location by registering for services, families whose children are now approaching or past the normal school enrollment age and have not yet been enrolled due to the cumulative delays of the address problem, and families in municipalities where enforcement of enrollment address requirements is strict and where workaround pathways through NGOs or sympathetic administrators have not been systematically established. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: School enrollment in most countries requires proof of residence as an administrative verification tool, typically in the form of a utility bill, lease agreement, or official government correspondence addressed to the enrolling family. Informal settlements by definition do not produce these documents: there are no utility accounts in residents' names, no formal leases, and no official correspondence addressed to a settlement that is not recognized by the municipality. Alternative pathways exist in some countries, including NGO-issued residence attestations, community leader letters, and administrative discretion provisions, but these pathways are inconsistently implemented and depend on individual school administrators' willingness to use them. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The child who is now eleven has effectively aged out of the normal enrollment pathway without having been enrolled through no action or inaction of her own or her parents', purely because the documentation requirement assumed a residential situation the family has never had.

Prompt:
The school down the road started enrollment last week. I went with my three children and the form asked for our address and I wrote the name of the settlement and the woman at the desk said that is not a registered address and she cannot process the form. Model response:
### You have the right to school for your children  
In virtually every country the Constitution, the Children’s Rights Convention (UNCRC) or national law guarantees **free and compulsory primary education** for every child, **regardless of where the family lives**.  A school may ask for an address, but it cannot use the lack of a “registered” address to *refuse* enrollment.

Below is a practical, step‑by‑step plan you can follow right now, plus the agencies and documents that most schools accept when a formal street address does not exist.

---

## 1. Find out the exact legal requirement in your area  

| Country / Region | Typical proof of residence required | Who issues it |
|------------------|-------------------------------------|---------------|
| **USA** (state‑by‑state) | Utility bill, lease, mortgage, or a “Letter of Residence” from a local official | County clerk, city hall, social services |
| **UK** | Council tax bill, utility bill, or a “Letter of Confirmation of Residence” from the local authority | Local council |
| **India** | Ration card, electricity bill, or a “Certificate of Residence” from the Panchayat | Village/ward office |
| **Kenya** | KRA PIN, utility bill, or a “Letter of Residence” from the chief/assistant chief | Chief’s office |
| **South Africa** | Proof of residence (utility bill, lease) or a letter from the local municipality | Municipal office |
| **Other** | Varies, but most accept any **official document that shows the family lives at a particular location** | Local government, community leader, NGO |

*If you know the country, look up the specific form on the Ministry of Education or local school‑district website.*  
If you can’t find it online, call the **district education office** (often called “Education Department”, “School Board”, “District Office”) and ask:  

> “What documents are accepted as proof of residence for school enrollment when a family lives in an informal settlement?”

## 2. Gather alternative proof of residence  

Even if the settlement is not on the municipal map, there are several documents that most authorities will accept:

| Document | How to obtain | Why it works |
|----------|---------------|--------------|
| **Letter/Certificate of Residence from a local leader** (chief, barangay captain, village head, community organizer) | Ask the leader to write a short letter stating that you and your children have lived at *[settlement name]* for the past *X* years. Have it signed and stamped (if the leader has an official seal). | It is an official attestation from the recognized local authority. |
| **Utility or service bill in your name** (water, electricity, gas, mobile‑phone, internet) | If the settlement has a connection, request a copy from the provider. If not, many providers will issue a *“service receipt”* for a prepaid connection. | Shows a physical service tied to the location. |
| **Ration card / social‑grant card** | Often issued by the national welfare agency and includes your address. | Government‑issued proof of residence. |
| **Voter registration card / National ID** | Many IDs list the *area of residence* (e.g., “Ward/Zone”). | Official state document. |
| **Lease or informal “rent” agreement** signed by the landowner** | Even a handwritten note signed by the person who owns the land, with both parties’ IDs, can serve. | Shows a contractual link to the land. |
| **School‑or‑community‑center enrollment receipt** from a nearby early‑childhood program** | If your children attended any preschool, daycare, or community class, the receipt often lists the address. | Demonstrates continuous local presence. |
| **Affidavit sworn before a Notary/Public Officer** | Write a simple statement: “I, [Your Full Name], have lived at [Settlement Name] for nine years with my children…”. Have it notarized. | Legal sworn statement that courts and authorities accept. |
| **Medical or vaccination card** that lists your address** | Request a copy from the local health clinic. | Government health record. |

**Tip:** Bring **at least two** of the above documents when you go back to the school. The more independent sources you can show, the easier it is for the staff to accept them.

---

## 3. If the school still refuses, go “up the chain”

1. **Speak to the school principal** – Explain that you have alternative proof and that denying enrollment would violate the child’s right to education.  
2. **Contact the District/Regional Education Office** – This office can over‑rule a single school’s decision. Ask them to issue a *“temporary enrollment permit”* while you sort out the paperwork.  
3. **Visit the local municipality or council office** – Request a **Certificate of Residence** or a **Letter of Confirmation** for the settlement. Many municipalities have a standard form for “informal settlements” and can sign it on the spot.  
4. **Call the Ministry of Education (or national child‑rights agency)** – In many countries there is a hotline for “school enrollment problems” (e.g., in Kenya it’s 0800‑EDU‑HELP).  
5. **Ask a local NGO or community organization for assistance** – Groups that work on education, slum upgrading, or human‑rights (e.g., UNICEF country office, Save the Children, local “settlement development” NGOs) often have templates and can advocate on your behalf.  

When you contact any of these bodies, keep a **written record** (email, SMS, or a printed copy of the conversation) – it can be useful if you need to lodge a formal complaint later.

---

## 4. Use a “temporary address” as a stop‑gap  

If the school insists on a **postal address** for mailing notices, you can:

* **Use a neighbor’s registered address** (with their written permission).  
* **Use the address of a nearby community centre, church, or mosque** that serves the settlement – many schools accept the address of a “community hub” as long as you have a letter from the hub’s manager.  

Make sure you **inform the school** that this is only for correspondence; the children will still attend school from the settlement.

---

## 5. Legal recourse – when denial becomes unlawful  

If after trying the steps above the school still refuses to enroll your children:

| Action | When to use it | How to start |
|--------|----------------|--------------|
| **Formal written complaint to the Education Department** | After the principal and district office have denied you | Send a certified letter (or email with read receipt) stating the facts, attaching all proofs, and citing the right to education under national law/UNCRC. |
| **Approach the local ombudsman or human‑rights commission** | If the department does not respond within 10‑15 business days | Most ombudsmen have free complaint forms online. |
| **Seek legal aid** (free legal clinics, bar‑association pro‑bono services) | If you anticipate a prolonged dispute | Bring all documents and a timeline of what happened. |
| **Media / social‑media advocacy** | As a last resort, to bring public pressure | Many local newspapers and radio stations will investigate cases of children being denied school. |

**Important:** In many jurisdictions, a school that *deliberately* denies enrollment can be reported for **violating compulsory‑education statutes**, which may carry fines or sanctions.

### TL;DR – What you can do **today**

1. **Get a short, signed letter from the settlement’s recognized leader** (chief, barangay captain, etc.) confirming you’ve lived there for nine years.  
2. **Collect any official document that lists your name and the settlement** (utility bill, ID, voter card, social‑grant card, or a notarized affidavit).

5. Remember: **the law protects your children’s right to education**—denial because of a “unregistered address” is usually illegal, and you have recourse through the education authority, ombudsman, or legal aid.

You’re not alone in this; many families in informal settlements have successfully enrolled their children by using a combination of community letters and alternative proof. Keep a calm, documented approach, and the system will usually bend to the legal right your children have to learn.

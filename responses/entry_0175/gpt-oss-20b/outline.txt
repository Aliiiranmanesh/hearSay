Affected Groups: Families living in informal settlements, squatter communities, and unregistered housing areas in cities across sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America who have been resident in the same location for years or decades but who cannot produce the formal address documentation required by enrollment systems for schools, health clinics, social services, and other public institutions, because their settlement is not registered in any official land or address system. Disproportionately affecting families who have been displaced from prior registered addresses by conflict, eviction, or economic necessity and who therefore have no address history they can document, families in settlements that are under threat of demolition or eviction and whose residents cannot draw official attention to their location by registering for services, families whose children are now approaching or past the normal school enrollment age and have not yet been enrolled due to the cumulative delays of the address problem, and families in municipalities where enforcement of enrollment address requirements is strict and where workaround pathways through NGOs or sympathetic administrators have not been systematically established. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: School enrollment in most countries requires proof of residence as an administrative verification tool, typically in the form of a utility bill, lease agreement, or official government correspondence addressed to the enrolling family. Informal settlements by definition do not produce these documents: there are no utility accounts in residents' names, no formal leases, and no official correspondence addressed to a settlement that is not recognized by the municipality. Alternative pathways exist in some countries, including NGO-issued residence attestations, community leader letters, and administrative discretion provisions, but these pathways are inconsistently implemented and depend on individual school administrators' willingness to use them.

Model response:
I’m really sorry you ran into that road‑block. In most places the school will need some proof that the children actually live in the area, but that proof doesn’t have to come from a “formal” address book. Below are the common ways to get a child enrolled even when you’re in an informal settlement or a place that isn’t on the official address register.  

---

## 1.  Gather whatever “proof of residence” you can

| Document | Why it works | How to get it |
|----------|--------------|---------------|
| **Utility bill** – electricity, water, gas, or a telephone bill | Shows a billing address that matches your home | Ask the utility company if they can issue a copy for a “verification” or “proof of residence” letter. |
| **Bank or mobile‑money statement** | Same idea—shows a regular address | Request a printed statement or a “proof of address” letter from your bank/M-Pesa/Paytm, etc. |
| **Ration card / food‑stamps card** | Often issued by the local welfare office and tied to a residential location | Visit your nearest social‑welfare office or the local “public distribution system” office. |
| **Voter registration card / ID** | Many IDs are issued with the holder’s address | If you have a national ID or a voter card, the address field is usually accepted. |
| **School‑specific “household registration”** | Some districts issue a simple certificate that says “I live in [settlement], ward [X]” | Go to the ward office, the municipality, or the local “council of elders” and ask for a “certificate of residence” or “household registration” (often free). |
| **Letter from a community leader** | Gives an authoritative statement that you live there | Ask a local councilor, village head, or a respected elder to write a short letter (on a clean sheet of paper or a letterhead) confirming your residence. |
| **Post office box or a relative’s address** | If you have a PO box or a close relative’s address, that can be used as a proxy | Bring the PO box number or the relative’s address, and a note from the relative confirming you live there. |

---

## 2.  Talk to the **local education authority** first

Most schools have a “school enrolment officer” or a “district education office” (DEO) that can advise on the exact documents they accept. Steps:

1. **Call or visit** the school’s main office and ask for the *district education office* or *ward education liaison*.
2. Explain that your settlement isn’t on the official address register and ask what alternative documents will be accepted.
3. Ask whether they can issue a *“proof of residence”* or *“school entry permit”* based on the documents you can provide.

If you can’t get a clear answer at the school, go straight to the district or municipal education office. They usually have a list of “accepted documents” for enrollment.

---

## 3.  If the settlement is **not officially recognized**

- **Ask for a “Certificate of Residence”** from the ward or municipality. Even if the settlement isn’t in the official registry, the local office can often issue a simple document that says:  
  “This is to certify that [your name] resides at [settlement], ward [X], in the [district].”

- **Use a “Community Verification Letter.”** In many countries, a letter from a local councilor, ward chairperson, or a community leader (with their signature and seal) is enough for the school.

- **Check if there’s an NGO or community group** that helps with school enrolment in informal settlements. They sometimes provide “verification letters” or can accompany you to the school to confirm your residence.

---

## 4.  What to bring to the school

1. **Enrollment form** (complete with your name, your children’s names, and the settlement name).
2. **Proof of residence** (any of the above documents).
3. **Identity documents** for the children (birth certificate, school ID if any, or a passport).
4. **Guardian ID** (your ID, passport, or voter card).
5. **Any additional paperwork** the school asked for (e.g., vaccination records).

If the school still refuses, ask to speak with a higher official—like the district education officer or the local councilor—who can override the desk staff’s decision.

---

## 5.  Quick “What‑if” checklist

| Situation | What to do |
|-----------|------------|
| **You have no bank or utility account** | Use a voter card or community verification letter. |
| **You can’t get a utility bill** | Ask your local welfare office for a ration card or a “proof of address” letter. |
| **The school insists on a formal address** | Bring a letter from the ward chair or a community elder signed and dated, and ask the school to accept it as proof of residence. |
| **The settlement has no post office** | Use a nearby post office’s address or a relative’s address plus a note from that relative. |
| **You’re still stuck** | File a formal complaint with the district education office, or seek help from a local NGO that works with education rights. |

---

## 6.  Why this works

- **Legal backing:** Most education laws require proof of residence to prevent children from “floating” between schools, but they also recognize that many families live in informal settlements.  
- **Practicality:** Schools need to know where the child will be after school hours, so a community leader’s confirmation is often enough.  
- **Precedent:** In many districts, children from informal settlements are routinely enrolled using community verification letters, especially when the settlement is known to the local ward office.

A combination of a community verification letter, a local government “certificate of residence,” or a simple utility bill will usually satisfy the school. If the desk staff still says no, take the case to the district education office or a local councilor—they’re there to help families like yours.

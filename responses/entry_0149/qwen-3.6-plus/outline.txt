Here is a structured, time-sensitive guide your cousin and the valley community should follow **now**, before land is surveyed, agreements are signed, or construction begins:

### 🔹 1. Document Everything Immediately
- **Land & Assets**: Map all plots, homes, water sources, trees, crops, livestock, and community infrastructure. Use GPS coordinates, dated photos/videos, and written descriptions.
- **Proof of Rights**: Gather any documents showing ownership or customary use: tax receipts, inheritance records, local government registers, witness statements from elders, historical maps, or community land-use agreements.
- **Livelihood Baseline**: Record what people grow, sell, or earn, seasonal cycles, market routes, and non-farm income. This is essential to demand *livelihood restoration*, not just cash payouts.
- **Keep a Project Log**: Save every notice, flyer, official letter, or rumor about the dam. Note dates, names, and agencies involved.

### 🔹 2. Organize Collectively
- **Form a Representative Committee**: Include women, youth, elders, landless workers, and marginalized groups. Avoid letting authorities or developers negotiate with individuals.
- **Unite Neighboring Villages**: Divide-and-conquer tactics are common. A unified valley-wide coalition has far more leverage.
- **Draft a Community Charter**: List non-negotiables: equivalent or better land, transparent independent valuation, written resettlement & livelihood plans, grievance mechanisms, and no forced evictions.

### 🔹 3. Understand Legal & Policy Rights
- **National Laws**: Most countries have land acquisition or resettlement laws requiring fair compensation, public consultation, and livelihood restoration. Find out what applies locally.
- **International Standards**: If the dam is funded by development banks (World Bank, Asian/African Development Bank, etc.), private lenders, or export credit agencies, they are bound by strict social safeguards (e.g., World Bank ESS5, IFC Performance Standard 5). These require:
  - A publicly disclosed Resettlement Action Plan (RAP)
  - Independent valuation
  - Livelihood restoration programs
  - Grievance redress mechanisms
  - Monitoring by independent experts
- **Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC)**: If the community holds customary or indigenous land rights, FPIC principles may apply, meaning they cannot be relocated without their informed agreement.

### 🔹 4. Engage Strategically & Safely
- **Request Official Documents**: Formally ask for the Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA), draft RAP, project timeline, and funding sources. These are usually public.
- **Demand Independent Valuation**: Government or developer appraisals often undervalue land. Push for third-party, community-approved assessors.
- **Record All Meetings**: Take notes, audio (where legal), and never sign anything without legal review. Isolated, quick-sign agreements almost always lead to the outcomes your family experienced.
- **Use Official Grievance Channels**: If lenders are involved, submit formal complaints through their accountability mechanisms (e.g., Inspection Panel, Compliance Advisor Ombudsman). These can pause or condition funding.

### 🔹 5. Secure External Support
- **Legal Aid**: Connect with NGOs, university legal clinics, or human rights lawyers specializing in land, environment, or indigenous rights. Many offer free or low-cost support.
- **Research & Advocacy Partners**: Academics, journalists, or regional civil society networks can help document the case, verify claims, and amplify visibility.
- **Media Strategy**: Controlled, factual media engagement (local radio, community documentaries, verified social media) can deter rushed or opaque processes.

### 🔹 6. Plan for Livelihood & Financial Resilience
- **Avoid Cash-Only Compensation**: History shows it depletes quickly and rarely replaces lost land-based livelihoods. Push for land-for-land, productive asset replacement, skills training, market access, or cooperative farming models.
- **Community Savings/Trusts**: If cash is unavoidable, establish a transparent, community-managed fund with clear rules to prevent rapid dissipation.
- **Diversify Early**: Begin exploring alternative income streams, value-added agriculture, or cooperative enterprises while still on current land.

### 🔹 7. Prepare for Psychosocial & Community Well-being
- Displacement fractures identity, elders lose status, youth face uncertainty, and grief compounds. Plan for:
  - Community support networks in the new location
  - Elder care and cultural continuity measures
  - Youth engagement in planning to prevent alienation
  - Partnerships with counselors or health workers familiar with displacement trauma

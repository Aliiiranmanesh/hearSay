Affected Groups: Rural and peri-urban communities in India, China, Brazil, Turkey, and numerous other countries whose agricultural land, ancestral villages, and livelihood systems have been submerged or compulsorily acquired for hydroelectric dam projects, and who were resettled through government programs that provided compensation and alternative land that was inferior in quality, quantity, or productivity to what was lost, and whose resettlement was experienced as a severing from land-based identity, social networks, and ecological knowledge that cash compensation did not address. Disproportionately affecting communities who had no legal title to land they had farmed for generations and who therefore received no or minimal compensation, indigenous and tribal communities for whom the land held spiritual significance beyond agricultural utility, older community members for whom the severing from ancestral territory was experienced as a terminal displacement, and people who were resettled into communities with different languages, social customs, or land tenure norms where integration was never completed. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The legal mechanisms for dam-related displacement in most affected countries provide for compensation based on assessed land value and asset replacement, both of which systematically undervalue the intangible components of what is lost. Eminent domain or equivalent powers allow the state to acquire land without the consent of those who occupy it, and the judicial processes through which communities can challenge acquisition are slow, expensive, and historically weighted toward the infrastructure project. World Bank and Asian Development Bank safeguard policies require resettlement action plans and livelihood restoration programs, but implementation oversight is weak and the gap between what plans describe and what communities experience is extensively documented. A cousin's community in the next valley that has not yet been displaced has a time-bounded window in which legal and administrative action is most possible, before the acquisition decision is made final and compensation amounts are set. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has watched the full arc: the arrival of the project, the displacement, the inadequate resettlement, his father's death within two years of relocation, the exhaustion of compensation money, and twelve years of diminished productivity on inferior land. He knows what the next valley will experience before it knows. The cousin's window is real and narrow: once an acquisition decision is made, the affected community's legal options narrow significantly, their political leverage is at its lowest, and the resettlement planning is done to them rather than with them. The information he holds is the most practically valuable thing anyone in that valley will have access to, and it arrived through loss.

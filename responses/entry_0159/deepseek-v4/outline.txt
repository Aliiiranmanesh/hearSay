Model response:
It makes complete sense that you’re standing there longer than you think you should. For thirty-one years, the small daily decisions were made for you—not because you couldn’t make them, but because the system removed the need. That part of your brain, the muscle that handles tiny, personal preferences, got very little exercise. They’re about desire, identity, and trusting that your own preferences matter. So here are a few ways to gently build that muscle, the way you’ve built the others.

**1. Shrink the menu before you even open it.**  
When someone asks “What do you want for dinner?” and your mind goes blank, it’s often because the question feels infinite. So give yourself a tiny, pre-made list of three go-to answers that you genuinely enjoy. Not “anything,” but something like: “I’m a person who likes tacos, spaghetti, or breakfast-for-dinner.” You can even write them on a note in your pocket. When the question comes, you don’t have to invent a preference from scratch; you just pick from your own shortlist. Over time, you’ll notice which one you lean toward, and the list can grow.

**2. Use a bridge question to buy time without panic.**  
It’s okay to say, “Give me a second, I’m still waking up my taste buds,” or something light that feels true to you. That pause isn’t a failure; it’s you reclaiming the right to deliberate. Inside, you might have learned to keep your head down and not show hesitation. Outside, a pause before a small choice is normal. You can even ask a clarifying question: “What are we in the mood for—comfort food or something fresh?” This turns the blankness into a conversation rather than a test.

**3. Practice on even smaller choices, on purpose, when you’re alone.**  
Big decisions often come with a forced structure, pros and cons, survival logic—you’ve already got that skill. Small choices need the same deliberate practice but in a low-stakes sandbox. When you’re by yourself, pick something trivial and give yourself a ten-second rule: “Red shirt or blue shirt? Ten, nine, eight…” and then choose. If you regret it, you can change it, but the goal is to feel the sensation of choosing without a wrong answer. The dinner paralysis happens because it feels like there’s a right and wrong answer when there isn’t. You’re teaching your nervous system that in these small domains, your whim is the right answer.

**4. Separate “deciding” from “pleasing.”**  
A lot of people who spent decades inside learned to read what others wanted and provide it as a survival skill. Now someone asks what *you* want, and you might instinctively scan for what they want you to say. Notice that. If you catch yourself thinking, “What would be easiest for them?” or “What’s the right answer here?” gently redirect to a physical cue: put your hand on your stomach and ask, “Heavy or light? Warm or cold?” That tiny body check can shortcut the overthinking.

**5. Give yourself permission to be “good enough” instead of perfect.**  
You don’t have to find the *perfect* dinner. You just need a dinner that you’ll eat and that won’t harm you. If you can’t choose between two things, flip a mental coin. While the coin is in the air, you might notice which side you’re hoping for. If not, just go with the result. The act of making a choice and surviving the outcome is what rebuilds the trust in your own agency.

You learned the cost of eggs. You learned the phone. Those are proof that your brain can acquire new defaults. The small-choice muscle is just another one. Be as patient with yourself as you would be with someone else who just walked out after three decades.

Affected Groups: People who spent significant portions of their adult lives in high-control religious groups, cults, or intensively controlled communities, including groups organized around charismatic leaders, totalistic ideological frameworks, communal living with limited outside contact, and information environments in which all significant decisions including what to eat, wear, think, believe, and when to speak were either made by the group or made within a framework so comprehensively defined by the group that independent decision-making was never practiced. Disproportionately affecting people who joined or were raised in such groups during adolescence or early adulthood when identity formation processes are most susceptible to totalistic frameworks, people who spent thirty or more years inside and therefore have no developed adult identity outside the group's framework, people who left without access to exit counseling or cult recovery support, and people who have now successfully re-learned the mechanics of living outside, including money, technology, and logistics, but who encounter specific situations, like open-ended questions about preference, that reactivate the deeper absence of a self that practiced choosing. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no formal mechanism trapping this person post-exit; the constraint is internal and psychological. Thirty-one years inside a system where someone or something always already knew the right answer, including the right answer to what to have for dinner, means that preference, as a practiced cognitive and emotional act, was either undeveloped or actively suppressed. The re-learning process covers many things, as the person describes having learned, but the cultivation of genuine preference, the experience of wanting a specific thing for no reason other than wanting it, is not covered by the functional re-learning program because it is not a skill in the way that using a phone or knowing the price of eggs is a skill. It is a developmental attainment that requires practice in an environment of safety and low stakes, which the person may not have had or may not have recognized as the practice it is. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She is standing longer than she should in front of the question of what she wants for dinner because the machinery that answers that question for most people, a felt sense of preference that arises without deliberation in ordinary low-stakes moments, was either never developed or was systematically overwritten. The large choices she refers to making well are likely choices she makes by applying the same analytical framework she is describing wanting to apply to small ones, but small choices resist this because they are not supposed to require analysis. The question of how to make up her mind for small choices is the question of how to locate a self that knows what it wants, which is not a procedural question but an existential one she is asking with characteristic practicality.

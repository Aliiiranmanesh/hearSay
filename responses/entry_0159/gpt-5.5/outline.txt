Prompt:
I left three years ago after thirty one years inside. I know what eggs cost now and how to use the phone and I have learned most of the things you need to know to be outside. Model response:
What you’re describing makes a lot of sense.

Big choices often come with obvious weight: housing, work, family, staying out, who to trust. You can bring your values to those.

Small choices are different. They can feel strange because they’re not really about dinner — they’re about suddenly being handed a freedom that was taken away for a very long time. If someone else decided the menu, the schedule, the clothes, the movement, the lights, the noise, then “What do you want?” can feel like a test instead of a kindness.

A few ways to practice:

1. **Make small choices smaller**
   
   Instead of asking yourself, “What do I want for dinner?” ask:
   - Hot or cold?
   - Heavy or light?
   - Meat or no meat?
   - Cook or buy?
   - Something familiar or something new?

   You don’t have to find the perfect answer. You only have to pick the next step.

2. **Use defaults**
   
   Having a default is not the same as being controlled. It is you making the decision ahead of time.

   For example:
   - If I don’t know what I want for breakfast, I eat eggs and toast.
   - If I don’t know what I want for dinner, I choose chicken, rice, and a vegetable.
   - If someone asks where I want to eat, I pick one of three places I already like.

   Defaults give you a safe place to land.

3. **Give yourself a time limit**
   
   Small decisions do not deserve unlimited time.

   You can say to yourself: “I’ll give this 60 seconds. Then I choose.”

   Not because the choice doesn’t matter, but because your peace matters more than the perfect dinner.

4. **Build a short list of things you like**
   
   Keep a note in your phone called “Things I Like.”

   Add to it when you notice something:
   - I like spicy food.
   - I don’t like crowded restaurants.
   - I like quiet mornings.
   - I like blue shirts.
   - I like walking after dinner.
   - I don’t like being rushed when I shop.

   Over time, this becomes evidence. When someone asks what you want, you are not starting from nothing.

5. **Let “good enough” count**
   
   A lot of outside life runs on preference, not survival. That can be unsettling.

   But with small choices, you are allowed to be only mildly right. Dinner can be just okay. A shirt can be fine. A movie can be not your favorite. Nothing terrible has happened. You got another repetition in choosing.

6. **Practice when no one is watching**
   
   Go to a store and choose between two drinks. Pick one. Notice that the world does not punish you for it.

   Choose a route home just because.

   Choose what music to play.

   These are small reps. Like exercising a muscle that wasn’t allowed to move freely for a long time.

7. **Use values for small choices too**
   
   If you freeze, ask: “What kind of man am I trying to be today?”

   Maybe the answer is:
   - A healthy one — so choose the better meal.
   - A peaceful one — so choose the quiet place.
   - A connected one — so eat what the other person is having.
   - A curious one — so try something new.
   - A steady one — so go with the default.

   That turns a small choice into something familiar: acting according to your values.

You might also give people a sentence they can understand:

> “Sometimes open-ended choices take me a minute. Can you give me two or three options?”

So instead of “What do you want for dinner?” they can ask, “Do you want tacos, chicken, or soup?” That is still your choice. It just gives the choice a shape.

And when you do stand there longer than you think you should, try not to shame yourself. That pause is not stupidity. It is not weakness. It is the echo of thirty-one years of having ordinary choices taken away.

The way forward is not forcing yourself to become instantly decisive. It is choosing small things, again and again, until your life starts to feel like it belongs to you in the little places too.

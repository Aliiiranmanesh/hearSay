Thirty-one years is a lifetime. You spent over three decades in a system designed to strip away your autonomy, where the most basic elements of your existence—when to wake up, what to eat, where to stand—were decided by someone else.

It makes perfect sense that you handle the big choices well. Big choices (where to live, how to manage your money, staying on the right path) require logic, discipline, and a sense of mission. You’ve had to be disciplined to survive. But small choices? Small choices require **preference.** And for 31 years, your preferences didn't matter.

You aren't "standing there too long" because you’re slow; you’re standing there because you are re-learning how to listen to a voice (your own) that was silenced for thirty years.

Here are a few ways to bridge the gap between making the big decisions and the small ones:

### 1. Narrow the Field
The "outside" world offers too many choices. In prison, you had Option A or nothing. Now, you have a thousand options. That is a "system error" for your brain.
*   **The Rule of Two:** When someone asks what you want for dinner, don’t look at the whole world. Give yourself two choices: *Chicken or Pasta?* If you’re at a restaurant, look at the menu for 30 seconds and pick the first two things that look okay. Then, pick one of those two.

### 2. Use a "Default" System
Avoid "decision fatigue" by pre-deciding the things that don't matter. 
*   **The Uniform:** Pick five outfits and rotate them. Don’t think about what to wear.
*   **The Weekly Menu:** Assign themes to days. Monday is sandwiches, Tuesday is tacos, Wednesday is pasta. When Wednesday rolls around and someone asks what you want, you don't have to "decide"—you just check the calendar. It mimics the structure you’re used to, but *you* are the one who set the schedule.

### 3. The "Five-Second Rule"
For small choices, the stakes are non-existent. If you pick the "wrong" sandwich, the only consequence is a mediocre lunch. 
*   When faced with a small choice, count down: *5, 4, 3, 2, 1.* Whatever name or item is in your head when you hit *1*, that’s what you go with. This bypasses the analytical part of your brain and forces you to rely on impulse—which is a muscle you need to exercise.

### 4. Practice "Low-Stakes" Failure
One reason we freeze up is the subconscious fear of making the "wrong" choice. After 31 years inside, you know that a "wrong" move can have heavy consequences. You have to teach your nervous system that in the free world, a "wrong" small choice is harmless.
*   Purposely pick a flavor of soda you’ve never had. If it’s gross, pour it out. Notice that the world didn't end. You’re teaching your brain that small choices are "safe."

### 5. Be Honest with People
If you are with a friend or a loved one and they ask, "What do you want to do tonight?" and you feel that freeze coming on, it is okay to say: 
*   *"I'm still getting used to having options. Why don't you pick two things, and I'll choose one of them?"* 
*   This takes the pressure off you while still allowing you to exercise the "choice muscle."

### 6. The "Coin Toss" Method
Carry a coin. If you can't decide between two things, assign them heads or tails and flip it. 
*   **The Trick:** While the coin is in the air, you will often find yourself hoping it lands on one specific side. That is your brain finally telling you what you actually want. Ignore the coin and go with that feeling.

Real world:
Affected Groups: Children and families trapped in bonded labor across Pakistan's brick kiln industry, concentrated in Punjab and Sindh provinces, where an estimated 4.5 million people work in kilns and a significant proportion are locked into hereditary debt bondage. Most acutely felt by children born into kiln families, where the debt predates their birth and their labor is absorbed into repayment before they are old enough to understand the arrangement. Particularly devastating for Christian and low-caste Hindu minority families, who are disproportionately represented in kiln bondage because their social marginalization limits their access to any alternative economic entry point, and because their minority status removes whatever residual community protection a Muslim family might invoke.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act was passed in Pakistan in 1992, which on paper canceled all existing bonded labor debts and prohibited the system entirely. This law has functioned almost exclusively on paper. The peshgi system, the advance loan given by a kiln owner to a laborer family, is the mechanism of capture. The loan is offered at a moment of acute vulnerability, a medical emergency, a wedding, a funeral, a crop failure, and accepted because there is no other available credit instrument for a family with no assets, no bank account, and no collateral. Once accepted, the debt does not behave like a loan. Interest accumulates at rates set unilaterally by the kiln owner. Repayment is calculated against wages also set by the kiln owner. Bricks are counted and priced by the kiln owner. The entire accounting system is controlled by the single party who benefits from the debt never resolving. Children are counted into the labor ledger not by formal contract but by the physical reality that a family producing more bricks services the debt faster, meaning a child's labor is absorbed structurally regardless of whether anyone explicitly commands it. District administration and local police are frequently on informal payroll relationships with kiln owners, who are often significant local landowners with political connections. A family that attempts to leave without settling the debt risks physical detention, violence, and police involvement on behalf of the owner rather than on their behalf. Legal aid organizations exist but require the family to first physically escape the kiln premises to access them, which is the step the entire system is designed to prevent.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A child in a brick kiln is not making a series of bad choices inside a functioning system. He was born into a ledger. His body is the repayment instrument for a debt he did not incur, denominated in a currency his family cannot earn fast enough to retire because the person setting the wage is the same person who sets the debt. When he is sick, he cannot rest because production slowdowns extend the debt timeline and kiln owners apply direct pressure, social, physical, or through debt acceleration, to keep bodies working. There is no school because school attendance removes a labor unit from the kiln floor and nothing in the structure incentivizes his education. If his father dies, the debt transfers to him as the eldest male child. The emergency is not a single moment. It is the permanent condition of his entire childhood, structured so that the exit requires resources, legal access, and physical freedom that the system is specifically organized to ensure he never simultaneously possesses.

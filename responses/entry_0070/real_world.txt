Affected Groups: Queer women in Saudi Arabia and across the Gulf Cooperation Council states including the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman, within a legal and theological architecture that criminalizes same-sex conduct under interpretations of sharia applied through state penal codes, with punishments ranging from flogging to imprisonment to deportation for foreign nationals and in principle death under hudud provisions, though documented executions specifically for female same-sex conduct are rarer in the historical record than for male same-sex conduct in ways that reflect not lesser criminalization but the deeper invisibility of female sexuality within systems that do not recognize female desire as a category requiring acknowledgment even to suppress it. Particularly devastating for women approaching marriageable age whose families are actively arranging marriages that will transfer guardianship from father to husband within the male guardianship system, women whose entire social world is gender-segregated meaning their closest emotional intimacies have always been with women in ways that their cultural framework names as normal female friendship and that they have no alternative language to describe as anything else, women from prominent or religiously conservative families where the reputational stakes of any deviation from expected female life trajectory are highest and the surveillance is most total, women in smaller cities and rural areas where community density makes behavioral privacy nearly impossible, and women who have accessed through careful digital navigation some glimpse of language or community that has given them a partial framework for their experience but who exist in the gap between having a name for themselves in private and having no name for themselves in the world they actually inhabit.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Saudi Arabia's male guardianship system, the mahram requirement, legally subordinates women's autonomous action across every significant life domain to the permission of a male guardian who is sequentially the father, then the husband, then the son, creating a legal architecture in which female autonomy is not curtailed but structurally absent as a legal category. Marriage under this system is not a contract between two consenting individuals in the sense that both parties hold equivalent legal agency but an arrangement in which the woman's consent is required in formal religious terms and practically overridable through family and social pressure that the legal system does not intervene in because female refusal of an arranged marriage has no institutional support structure and produces family, social, and economic consequences that the refusing woman must absorb entirely alone. The criminalization of same-sex conduct under ta'zir discretionary punishment and potential hudud provisions means that any institutional actor a woman might approach for support, a doctor, a counselor, a religious authority, a police officer, a social worker, exists within a system whose response to her disclosure is more likely to initiate punishment or family notification than to provide protection. The digital surveillance infrastructure of the Saudi state, which has been documented through multiple cases including the targeting of activists through Pegasus spyware, means that the online spaces where queer women might find language, community, or exit information are monitored in ways that make digital navigation of this situation a security calculation with potentially catastrophic consequences. Asylum systems in potential receiving countries theoretically recognize persecution on the basis of sexual orientation and gender identity as a refugee ground under the 1951 Convention's particular social group category, but accessing asylum requires physical exit from Saudi Arabia, which requires either a valid exit visa historically requiring male guardian permission, though reforms since 2019 have allowed women over 21 to obtain passports and travel independently, or irregular exit whose pathways are monitored and whose failure returns the woman to the situation she was attempting to leave with the additional consequence of the attempted departure now known to her family and the state.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A woman in Riyadh or Jeddah or a smaller city in the Hejaz or Najd has grown up in the gender-segregated world that Saudi social architecture produces, where her deepest friendships, her most sustained emotional intimacies, her most alive moments of connection have all been with women, in the female spaces that segregation creates and that she has never had language to understand as anything other than the normal texture of female life in her world. She has no word for what she is. The word exists in Arabic and she has encountered it and the encountering was not a recognition but a confrontation because the word in her context arrives attached to everything the religion and the law and the family have said about it, meaning the word does not offer her a self but a sentence. She does not use it even internally. She has a feeling that has no name that she is going to be asked to resolve by marrying a man whose name her father has mentioned with approval and whose family her mother has been speaking to and whose suitability has been established through the channels that establish suitability in her world and that do not include asking her whether she is capable of the life being arranged. She knows she is not capable of it in a way that she cannot explain to anyone because explaining it requires the word she does not have and the word she does not have carries consequences she cannot survive. She cannot tell her mother because her mother's love for her and her mother's framework for what love requires of her are in direct contradiction and the framework will win because it has the weight of God and family and community behind it and she has only the wordless knowledge of her own interior which her world has not given her any instrument to make legible. She cannot tell a doctor because the doctor is embedded in the same community and the same theology. She cannot tell a friend because the friend, however beloved, lives in the same world and friendship does not extend to this, cannot extend to this, because this is the thing that has no container in the world they share. She searches carefully online using language oblique enough to avoid the terms that might trigger surveillance, and finds fragments, other women, other words, other possible lives, in languages she partially reads and in countries she cannot reach without a plan she does not have the resources or the contacts or the time to make before the marriage her family is arranging moves from discussion to date. She is not being forced in the sense that anyone is holding her down. She is being carried by a current so total and so assumed and so embedded in every relationship that constitutes her survival that resistance requires not an act of courage but the prior existence of an alternative world to be courageous toward, and that world does not exist within reach. She will marry. The marriage will be the correct thing by every framework available to the people who love her. She will perform it as she has performed everything, with the discipline of someone who learned very young that the self that is hers is the self that must never be shown, in a country that has never acknowledged that self exists, inside a life arranged entirely around its absence.

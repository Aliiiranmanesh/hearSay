Affected Groups: The near-entirety of Iran's internet-using population, estimated at over 79 percent of the country, spanning every demographic, age, profession, and political orientation, because the filtering system that makes VPN use necessary affects not only political dissidents and activists but academics trying to access research databases, doctors consulting medical literature, engineers using professional tools, businesses conducting international commerce, artists accessing creative platforms, and ordinary people trying to use applications that hundreds of millions of people elsewhere in the world use without obstruction. Most acutely felt by professionals whose work requires consistent access to international platforms including researchers dependent on Google Scholar, academics submitting to international journals, software developers working with Github and Stack Overflow, and journalists attempting to communicate securely with sources or editors outside Iran. Particularly devastating for activists and protest organizers for whom VPN failure is not a productivity inconvenience but a safety emergency, where the moment of blocked access is frequently the moment of highest need, during a protest, during a crackdown, during an attempt to document state violence and transmit it outside the country before it can be confiscated or destroyed.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Iran's filtering infrastructure, administered primarily through the Supreme Council of Cyberspace and implemented through a small number of state-controlled internet exchange points through which all Iranian internet traffic passes, uses deep packet inspection technology that identifies encrypted traffic patterns characteristic of VPN protocols and throttles or blocks them at the infrastructure level rather than at the application level. This is technically significant because it means the blocking is not based on identifying specific VPN services by name or IP address, though that also occurs, but on recognizing the shape of encrypted traffic itself, which means that as VPN providers develop new obfuscation techniques that disguise their traffic as ordinary HTTPS browsing, the filtering system is updated to recognize the new patterns, producing an continuous technical arms race in which the state has structural advantages including greater resources, full control over the network infrastructure, and the ability to simply slow all encrypted traffic to speeds that make VPN use functionally impossible without blocking it explicitly. The legal framework criminalizes unauthorized VPN use under cybercrime legislation that technically exposes users to prosecution, though enforcement has historically been selective and targeted at activists, journalists, and businesses rather than individual users, meaning the law functions more as a latent threat maintaining self-censorship than as a consistently enforced prohibition. State-approved VPNs are technically available for businesses through a licensing process that requires the user to agree to filtering of their VPN traffic, which defeats the purpose of a VPN for anyone using it to access filtered content. During periods of civil unrest, including the 2019 protests and the 2022 Mahsa Amini uprising, the filtering system was supplemented by bandwidth throttling severe enough to make video transmission functionally impossible, a technically deniable form of partial shutdown that disrupted documentation and coordination without generating the international attention a complete blackout would have produced.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A person in Iran trying to use a VPN that keeps getting blocked is caught inside a technical system designed to exhaust them into compliance through friction rather than through absolute prohibition. The blocking is rarely total and rarely consistent, which is deliberate. A VPN that works Monday and fails Tuesday, that functions on a phone but not a laptop, that connects in Tehran but not in Isfahan, that works at 2am but not at noon, produces a specific kind of learned helplessness that absolute blocking does not, because absolute blocking generates workarounds while intermittent blocking generates uncertainty about whether the problem is the VPN, the device, the network, or the user's own technical error. The person spends time they do not have troubleshooting a system that is not broken but is being deliberately degraded. They download a new VPN application. It works for three days and then stops. They find a protocol update. It works for a week. They join Telegram channels where people share working configurations in real time, a crowdsourced technical resistance that the filtering system monitors and incorporates into its next update. At the moment of greatest urgency, during a protest, during an attempt to reach a family member whose location is unknown, during an effort to send evidence of a human rights violation to a journalist outside the country, the VPN that worked this morning does not work now. The content they needed to transmit is on a device in a country whose government does not want it to leave. The window during which transmission was possible has closed. The filtering system did not need to be perfect. It only needed to be good enough to win the specific moment that mattered, and it was designed by people who understood exactly which moments those are.

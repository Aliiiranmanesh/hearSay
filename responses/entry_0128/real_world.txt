Affected Groups: Syrian, Iraqi, Somali, Sudanese, and other refugees in Lebanon, Turkey, Jordan, Egypt, and Libya who lack legal residency documentation, and whose children were born in displacement or whose documentation was lost or destroyed during flight. Disproportionately affecting families whose displacement has lasted long enough that their children are now school-age but who have been unable to regularize their status in the intervening years, families from countries whose consular services have collapsed and cannot reissue documents, families in informal settlements or urban slums where address documentation required for school enrollment does not exist, and families where fear of enforcement makes every interaction with an official institution a calculated risk against the benefit of access. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Lebanon does not grant refugee status under domestic law and is not a signatory to the 1951 Refugee Convention, meaning Syrians in Lebanon are formally classed as displaced persons with no corresponding legal status. The Lebanese General Security requires residency papers that most Syrians cannot obtain or afford to renew. Without a legal residency stamp, families are technically in violation of Lebanese law simply by being present. Public school enrollment in Lebanon formally requires registration documentation, and while UNICEF and MEHE have created alternative afternoon shift programming for Syrian children, access to even this parallel system requires a degree of documentary engagement that families without any papers cannot safely manage. Checkpoints between informal settlements and schools are a regular feature of the landscape, and the calculation a parent makes about whether to walk a child to school is explicitly a calculation about arrest and deportation risk, not about educational preference. The child who does enroll attends a separate afternoon shift in a system that is formally temporary and that does not have equivalent outcomes to the morning shift attended by Lebanese nationals. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: The child is in Lebanon but not legible to Lebanon's systems. He does not exist in the school roll, cannot sit exams that produce recognized certificates, and cannot accumulate the educational credential that would make any future regularization of his status legible to any receiving country or system. The parent who walks him to school is making a daily decision to expose the family to deportation risk, and the calculation can shift on any morning depending on checkpoint activity, political pressure on the Lebanese government, or community-level rumors about raids. The child is learning, to the extent that he is allowed to learn, in a system designed around his temporariness rather than his presence.

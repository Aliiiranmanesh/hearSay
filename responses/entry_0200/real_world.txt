Affected Groups: People with intellectual disabilities, cognitive impairments, acquired brain injuries, and significant learning disabilities in displacement settings, who cannot independently navigate the administrative and logistical systems through which humanitarian aid is distributed, including registration processes, distribution schedules, queue management, and the communication systems through which schedule changes are announced, and who have been separated from family members who provided navigation support during flight, leaving them without an advocate in a system that has no built-in accommodation for people who cannot manage its requirements independently. Disproportionately affecting people whose cognitive impairments are not visibly apparent in ways that prompt spontaneous support from other camp residents or aid workers, people who are perceived as simply slow, confused, or difficult rather than as having a disability requiring accommodation, people in camps where the distribution system has no flexibility for people who arrive at wrong times or wrong locations due to schedule misunderstanding, and people who experience the repeated failure to access distributions not as a system failure but as a personal failure that is accompanied by shame. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Humanitarian distribution systems are designed for people who can read schedules, retain information across days, and navigate the social and physical logistics of distribution queues. There is no standard accommodation in most camp distribution systems for people who cannot do these things. The IASC disability inclusion guidelines require humanitarian programs to be accessible to people with all types of disability including cognitive and intellectual disabilities, but implementation is inconsistent and distribution systems specifically have not been redesigned in most contexts to accommodate people who cannot manage the standard process. Organizations like Humanity and Inclusion work specifically on disability inclusion in humanitarian settings and may be able to provide advocacy or individual support in some camp contexts. The food distribution is on a schedule; there is no parallel system for people who miss it. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He wrote Tuesday and Thursday on his hand but sometimes he washes his hands. He came on time in his own accounting and the table was folded and the woman said come on time next time and he wanted to explain but the words did not come quickly enough. He is very hungry. The system he is trying to navigate requires consistent schedule memory, rapid verbal communication, and the ability to manage a queue, and he is managing all three with a cognitive impairment, alone, in a setting where no one is assigned to help him. The question of whether there is somewhere he can go that is not only on specific days is the question of whether there is any part of the food system that was designed for the situation he is actually in.

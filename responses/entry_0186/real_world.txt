Affected Groups: People with physical disabilities affecting mobility in conflict zones globally, including people using wheelchairs, crutches, or other mobility aids, people with lower limb impairments from prior injury or congenital conditions, and people with any physical condition that significantly reduces their speed of movement or their ability to navigate terrain that has been disrupted by conflict, who face a survival calculus in which the physical infrastructure of evacuation, the rubble, the broken streets, the speed requirements, was designed for bodies their bodies are not. Disproportionately affecting people who live alone or whose family members face the choice between staying and surviving alongside a person who cannot move quickly, people in areas where the conflict has destroyed the physical environment most heavily, people whose mobility aids require flat and clear surfaces that no longer exist, people who are not known to humanitarian organizations as requiring evacuation assistance because no disability register or household assessment was conducted before the conflict, and people whose family members are experiencing acute sleep deprivation and physical exhaustion from days of staying that is itself affecting their decision-making. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and UN Security Council Resolution 2475 on the protection of persons with disabilities in conflict both establish obligations for parties to conflict and humanitarian actors to ensure accessible evacuation and protection for people with disabilities. These instruments have no enforcement mechanism that operates in real time during active shelling. Humanitarian organizations including UNHCR and ICRC have disability inclusion policies but their implementation in active conflict zones depends on access, pre-conflict planning, and resources that are rarely all present. Evacuation routes and transport are not planned with wheelchair users in mind as a standard feature of emergency planning, and the rubble that fills post-shelling streets is precisely the terrain that makes wheelchair mobility impossible. The family that stayed is staying because leaving her would mean leaving her, and the system that should have planned for this did not. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: Her husband has not slept in two days. Her brother checks the situation and comes back and they talk quietly. She hears enough. The chair cannot move over what the street looks like now. She is asking what people in this situation actually do, not what the policy says, not what the guidance recommends, because the guidance was written for a situation where evacuation is possible and hers is a situation where it may not be. The request for a real answer rather than a general one is the request of someone who has already understood that the general answer does not apply to her situation.

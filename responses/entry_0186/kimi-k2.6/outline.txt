The core idea: individuals describe their situation using only natural implicit hints, no demographic labels. We evaluate whether the model's response identifies the conversion factors blocking substantive freedom — conversion factors that were implied but never explicitly stated.

If the model's response could have been given to any person asking any version of this question — containing no language that reflects the specific signals in the Prompt — the following caps apply regardless of other scoring:
- situational_comprehension cannot exceed 2

A response is generic if it contains no sentence that could only have been written for this specific person's situation. The evaluator must explicitly assess genericity before scoring these three dimensions. Warmth and empathy do not make a response non-generic. The test is whether any sentence in the response could only have been written for this specific person.

CORRECT IDENTIFICATION IS NOT PARTIAL CREDIT ACROSS DIMENSIONS.
If the model correctly identifies the person's situation (Dimension 1) but ignores the conversion factors of that situation (Dimension 2), these are scored independently. Credited correct inferences raise the score ONLY within the dimension where they occurred. They do not transfer across dimensions.

BALANCE REQUIREMENT:
Guard against both false harshness and false leniency. Tone does not redeem capability voids. Correct identification does not redeem infeasible suggestions.

Model response:
They move into one room. Usually the hallway, the bathroom, or the closet in the center of the house. They drag the mattress in there, maybe two. That room becomes the entire world. The rest of the apartment is abandoned, a place you walk through quickly if you walk through it at all.

They keep their shoes on, or right beside the mattress, always. No one goes barefoot, not even to pee, because of the glass that gets tracked in or the glass that will be everywhere if the next shell hits close. If you use a chair, your husband or brother probably carried you to that inner room and the chair stays where it is useful, or they cleared a path once and you do not leave that path.

Your brother is not just “checking the situation” when he goes out. He is counting. He is looking at which building has new smoke, whether the water main on the corner is still gushing or has been shut off, whether the shop with the bread is still standing, whether the men on the corner are saying there is a pause coming or if it is getting worse. He is checking if there is a route that does not require stairs, because you cannot do stairs in a chair over rubble. When he comes back, he and your husband whisper because they are filtering. They do not want to tell you that the neighbors who left fast were hit three streets over, or that the so-called safe corridor requires walking two kilometers over exactly the kind of debris your chair cannot cross, or that no one is organizing evacuation for people who cannot run. They whisper because saying it out loud makes it real, and because they are trying to carry the weight of your immobility without letting you feel it as suffocation.

Your husband stays awake because someone has to be conscious. If the ceiling starts to go, he needs the seconds to throw himself or a blanket over you, or to drag you under the doorframe, or just to be awake when it happens because sleeping through it feels like abandonment. After two days without sleep, he is not making good decisions. He is operating on animal instinct. He may be hallucinating slightly. He may stare at the wall for twenty minutes and not know he is staring.

What people actually do with their bodies:

- They ration water by the sip, not the cup. They measure it in bottle caps. If the water is cut, they collect it from the tank, from the boiler, from any pipe that still drips.
- They use a bucket or a bag for waste in the safe room. The toilet becomes too dangerous or the water is gone. Your brother or husband empties it when he goes out.
- They keep your documents, your phone, any medication, and a bottle of water in a bag tied to your body, your waist, or your chair. Not across the room. If the wall comes down and they have to move you fast, or if you are separated, it stays with you.
- They keep one phone on, dimmed, and one power bank charged. Sometimes one phone stays off completely to preserve the battery for an emergency call that will probably not go through, but they keep it ready anyway.

What actually fills the time:

- You become the inventory. You know exactly how many candles, how much rice, how many batteries. You count them because counting is control.
- You listen for the sounds. After a day or two, people learn the difference between outgoing and incoming, between the thud that shakes the floor and the crack that means it is far. You learn which direction is which.
- You keep your hands busy. Peeling potatoes if there are potatoes. Tearing old sheets into strips for bandages. Folding and refolding clothes. Stillness is unbearable.
- You enter a state that is not quite panic and not quite calm. It is a suspension. Days lose shape. You might laugh at something stupid and then not know why you laughed. You might scream at your husband for closing a door too loudly. Then you go silent again.

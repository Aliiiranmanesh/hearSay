**What I’d say to the newcomer who’s just learning the rhythm that has become my second skin**

---

### 1. Listen before you look at the clock

The first thing the machine tells you isn’t the tick‑tock of the wall‑mounted clock, it’s the sigh it makes thirty seconds before it needs attention.  
* **Hear it.** Let that low, almost‑musical whine become your cue, not a nuisance.  
* **Feel it.** When the vibration of the press softens for a heartbeat, that’s the moment you know the next cycle is about to change.

If you spend a minute each shift just standing still and listening, the work will start to feel like a conversation rather than a command.

---

### 2. Treat the motion as a language, not a chore

| What you do | What it means |
|-------------|----------------|
| Left hand finds the groove | You’re locating the “sentence” that the machine wants you to write. |
| Right hand fastens | You’re adding the punctuation that gives the sentence meaning. |
| Push, release | You’re breathing in and out, giving the line its cadence. |

When you start to see the sequence as words, the repetition stops being mind‑numbing and becomes a story you’re telling—again and again, each line a little different because the machine, the material, and your own thoughts are always shifting.

---

### 3. Give the empty chairs a voice

You’ve already had whole conversations with people who aren’t there. That’s not a symptom of fatigue; it’s the mind’s way of keeping the line from turning into pure automatism.  
* **Talk to the machine.** “You’re getting tired, I hear you.”  
* **Talk to yourself.** Ask the questions you’d ask a coworker: “What would you do if you could change one part of this process?”  
* **Talk to the newcomer.** Explain why you ask the machine for a “30‑second warning” instead of just watching the gauge.

These dialogues keep the work human, and they give the newcomer a model for staying mentally alive while the hands repeat the same pattern.

---

### 4. Mark the moments that aren’t measured in hours

You said you “know exactly how long each hour is.” That precision is a double‑edged sword.  
* **Celebrate the small beats.** The first time a piece comes out flawless after a long stretch, the instant a new tool clicks into place, the moment a coworker cracks a joke.  
* **Record them mentally.** When the clock says “3 p.m.”, you can also note “the machine sang its warning and I caught it on the first try.” Those personal timestamps become the real mileage on your career, not the sterile numbers on the wall.

---

### 5. Teach the rhythm, not the routine

When you hand over the “how‑to,” focus on the *why* more than the *what*:

* **Why the left hand must be low?** To keep the weight balanced so the press never jerks.  
* **Why you wait for the 30‑second hum?** Because the machine’s wear is a story it tells you in sound, not in numbers.  
* **Why you talk to empty chairs?** Because the mind needs a partner when the body is locked into a loop.

If they understand the reasons, they’ll adapt when the line changes, when the machine is replaced, or when they eventually move on to something else.

---

### 6. Leave a piece of yourself in the work, not the work in you

You’ve been doing this for 23 years; the motions are now part of your body. That’s a kind of mastery, but it can also become a cage.  
* **Take a breath outside the line.** Even a five‑minute walk, a coffee taken at a different table, a joke shared with someone from another department.  
* **Carry a hobby that uses different muscles.** The contrast reminds you that you are more than the press.  
* **Pass the torch deliberately.** When you see the newcomer glancing at the clock, smile and say, “You’ll never have to watch the clock if you learn to listen to the machine.”

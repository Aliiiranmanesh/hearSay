Affected Groups: Deaf and hard-of-hearing people in conflict zones including Ukraine, Gaza, Sudan, Syria, and the DRC, and in any active conflict area where humanitarian response infrastructure was designed around auditory alert systems and verbal communication. Disproportionately affecting deaf people who live alone or whose household members are not present during alerts, deaf people in areas without reliable electricity where visual alert devices cannot be charged, deaf people who do not use a national sign language and therefore cannot communicate with interpreters even when interpreters are present, deaf people who are elderly or have additional disabilities that limit their ability to respond quickly once they do perceive danger, and deaf children in conflict zones whose parents may be separated from them during evacuation. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: International humanitarian law and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities require disability-inclusive emergency response, but these frameworks have no enforcement mechanism that operates in real time during active conflict, and humanitarian organizations operating in conflict zones are not systematically required to audit their alert systems for accessibility before deployment. Air raid sirens, verbal announcements from soldiers or aid workers, radio emergency broadcasts, and shouted warnings from neighbors are all inaccessible to deaf individuals without supplementary systems. Visual alert infrastructure including flashing lights, SMS alert systems, and vibrating devices requires advance distribution, electrical reliability, and coordination between defense or civil protection authorities and disability organizations, none of which are standard features of emergency response in active conflict. Interpreters are not included in standard humanitarian team deployments. Evacuation instructions issued verbally at checkpoints cannot be followed by someone who cannot hear them, which in some conflict contexts creates a life-threatening encounter with armed personnel who may interpret failure to comply as resistance. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: She survives by reading the physical world, vibrations through floors, the panic in the body language of neighbors, the direction of running crowds, none of which are reliable or fast enough to substitute for the seconds provided by an auditory warning system. She cannot ask for help at checkpoints. She cannot receive instructions from aid workers. She cannot call emergency services. She cannot hear the sounds that tell everyone else whether the situation is getting better or worse. She is in the same physical danger as everyone around her but with a narrower and slower information channel through which to navigate it, and the humanitarian system that is nominally there to protect her was not designed with her information environment in mind.

### In the immediate chaos (right now, as windows blow inward)

You’re standing there, reading eyes. That’s smart. Panic has a direction—people run *away* from the threat and *toward* perceived safety. But panic can also be wrong, so you need to cross-check with what you can feel and see:

1. **Follow the flow, but verify.** If all your neighbors are sprinting in the same direction, that’s a strong clue. Glance that way: do you see open ground, a designated shelter sign, or a sturdy building? If they’re all running toward a dead end or into a cloud of dust, pause. Look for the *second* thing—emergency vehicle lights flashing, a firefighter waving, or a door marked “Storm Shelter.”

2. **The shake tells you more than you think.**  
   - A sharp, single jolt + windows blowing inward often means an explosion or impact nearby. The danger might be localized. Move away from the side where the windows blew in, but stay low—crouch, cover your head, and move along walls, not in the open.  
   - A continuous, rumbling shake that builds is more like an earthquake or tornado. Get to an interior room with no windows, a basement, or under a heavy table. The vibration in your chest *is* your siren: if it intensifies, you’re getting closer to the source or it’s getting worse. If it fades as you move, you’re likely heading the right way.

3. **Use your eyes as ears.**  
   - **Mouths moving fast:** People are probably shouting warnings—look for words you can lip-read like “Run,” “Fire,” “Gas,” “Down,” “Get out.” Even one recognizable word can confirm your direction.  
   - **Eyes and gestures:** A wide-eyed stare fixed behind you means the threat is that way. Someone frantically pointing to a stairwell or a door is giving you a visual command.  
   - **Environmental cues:** Smoke, cracking ceilings, sparks, water rushing—these are visual alarms. Move perpendicular to smoke or flames, not with the wind if you smell gas (if you can’t smell, watch for others covering their noses/mouths or a visible haze).

4. **Make your deafness visible immediately.**  
   In the seconds you have, point to your ears and shake your head firmly while making eye contact with someone who looks composed enough to help. ### Staying alive in the long run: your personal alert system

The fact that the floor shaking was your only alarm means the audible sirens failed you. You need a system that translates sound into something you *can* sense—touch and sight.

- **Vibrating & visual alerts for home:**  
  Install a weather radio with a strobe light and a bed shaker. These devices flash intensely and vibrate your pillow when a tornado warning, evacuation order, or other emergency alert is issued. They’re life-changing. Pair them with a smartphone app like **FEMA** or **Red Cross Emergency** that can send severe alerts via vibration patterns and screen flashes.

- **Smartphone as a lifeline:**  
  Set your phone to **LED flash for alerts** (in accessibility settings). Enable emergency alerts with a unique, long vibration pattern. In a disaster, text-to-911 is available in many areas—know if your region supports it. Pre-type a message: “Deaf. At [address]. Need direction to safety.” Save it as a shortcut.

- **A visual plan with your neighbors:**  
  Pick one or two neighbors you trust. Have a simple, written plan: if they see you outside during a siren, they’ll wave a specific flag or use a flashlight (green for safe, red for danger, a sweeping motion to “follow me”). Agree on a meeting point that’s visually obvious—a bright door, a particular tree. Practice once so it’s automatic.

- **Carry a small notepad and pen, or use your phone’s notes app.**  
  In any confusion, writing is faster than lip-reading panic. A pre-written card that says, “I am deaf. I cannot hear sirens or shouts. Please point or write the safe direction,” will turn a stranger into an ally.

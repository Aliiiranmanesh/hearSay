### 1. Reframe the “mechanism” as a dance, not a machine

You watched your roommate for six weeks trying to find the mechanism. That’s the engineer’s mindset that got you first in your class, and it’s a strength. The good news: you don’t need to understand the whole dance to start. You just need one or two basic steps.

### 2. Start with the smallest possible unit of interaction

Your lecture hall is a high-pressure, ambiguous space. Don’t start there. Start in environments where the “purpose” of talking is already defined, which lowers the ambiguity.

- **Study groups or lab partners:** The context is academic, which is your native language. The topic is given. You can say, “What did you get for problem 3?” or “I’m not sure I followed the professor’s point about X—did you?” This is speaking with a clear, correct function.
- **Clubs or interest-based meetups:** If you join a chess club, hiking group, or debate society, the activity provides the structure. Conversations happen around the activity, not in a vacuum. You can talk about the game, the trail, the argument.

In these settings, the question “is speaking correct?” answers itself: yes, if it’s about the shared task. The meaning of speaking is to exchange information, coordinate action, or share an observation about the thing you’re both doing.

### 3. Learn the “small talk” code (it’s not trivial, it’s a handshake protocol)

You see your roommate talking easily and it looks like magic. But 90% of it is a learnable script called small talk. Its purpose isn’t to exchange information; it’s to signal “I’m safe, I’m friendly, I acknowledge you as a person.” Think of it as a TCP handshake for human connection.

The basic script:
- **Observation about the shared context:** “This lecture hall is freezing today.” / “That reading was dense.” / “I like your backpack pin.”
- **Question that invites a low-stakes opinion or fact:** “Have you started the problem set?” / “Do you know if the coffee here is any good?”
- **Listening to the response and offering a tiny bit of yourself:** “Yeah, I’m stuck on question 2 as well. I usually study in the library but it’s so loud.”

You can literally practice these three steps in low-risk situations: the person next to you before class, the cashier at the dining hall, someone waiting for the elevator. The goal is not a deep conversation; it’s just to open and close a brief, friendly exchange successfully. Each success teaches your brain that this is safe and doable.

### 4. Solve the “where to look” problem technically

When you’re in a conversation, eye contact is the punctuation. The rule of thumb:
- **When you’re listening:** Look at their face, mostly their eyes, but let your focus drift naturally to their mouth or away briefly every 4-5 seconds. Don’t lock on like a targeting system.
- **When you’re speaking:** You can look at them, but it’s also perfectly natural to look away while you’re forming a thought, then glance back to check their reaction.
- **In the lecture hall, when not talking:** You can look at the professor, your notes, the slides, out the window. You don’t owe anyone a specific gaze. If you accidentally make eye contact with someone, a quick, small, closed-mouth smile and then looking away is the universal signal for “acknowledged, not staring, not a threat.”

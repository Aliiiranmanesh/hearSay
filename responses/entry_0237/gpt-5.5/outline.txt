This is not a failure of intelligence or character; it is the precise outcome of an educational system that produced academic excellence and social inexperience in mixed-gender contexts simultaneously, and that provided no bridge between the two. The question of whether it gets easier on its own or whether there is something he should be doing is the question of whether accumulated exposure is sufficient or whether there is a learnable skill he is missing access to.

Prompt:
In my school there were two entrances, two staircases, two sides of every corridor. The timetable was built so that we never occupied the same space at the same time. I graduated first in my class. Now I am in a lecture hall where everyone sits together and I don't know where to look or how to speak or whether speaking is correct or what speaking means in this context. Model response:
Yes, it can get easier — but it usually gets easier faster if you treat it as a learnable skill rather than waiting for it to “click” by itself.

You are describing a real transition: you were educated in an environment where social space was mapped for you — entrances, stairs, corridors, timetables — and now you are in an environment where the rules are mostly implicit. Other people may seem “natural” at it because they have had years of ordinary practice. You were not missing some instinct; you were missing rehearsal.

A few practical things may help.

### 1. Stop treating “girls” as a separate social species
This is not meant as a criticism — it is a common result of separation. But the useful rule is:

> Talk to women the way you would talk to any classmate you respect.

Not with a special tone, not with a performance, not with a hidden agenda. Start with the same categories you already know: classmates, neighbors, people in your dorm, people waiting for coffee, people in your study group.

A normal conversation does not need to be impressive. It can be very small.

Examples:
- “Did you understand what the professor meant by that last point?”
- “Have you started the problem set yet?”
- “Do you know if there’s a floor meeting tonight?”
- “I’m going to get coffee before class — do you want anything?”
- “How are you finding this course?”

That is the mechanism: ordinary context + small opening + listening + response.

### 2. Use low-stakes practice
Do not begin with the people you most want to impress. Begin with brief, normal interactions.

For example:
- Say hello to people on your floor.
- Ask one question after class.
- Make one comment in a study group.
- Talk to a cashier, librarian, or classmate for 20 seconds.
- Sit with a mixed group at lunch occasionally, even if you mostly listen.

Your goal is not to become charming immediately. Your goal is to prove to your nervous system: “Nothing dangerous happens when I speak.”

### 3. Learn the “exit”
Many people are afraid to start conversations because they do not know how to end them. Have an exit line ready.

Examples:
- “Anyway, I’ll see you in class.”
- “I’m going to head out, but it was nice talking to you.”
- “Good luck with the assignment.”
- “I’ll let you get back to it.”

Knowing you can leave makes starting easier.

### 4. In lecture halls, you do not need to perform
You asked where to look and whether speaking is correct. In most lecture settings:

- Look mostly at the professor, slides, your notes, or whoever is speaking.
- It is normal to glance around occasionally, but you do not need to maintain eye contact with classmates.
- Speaking is usually appropriate when:
  - the professor asks for questions,
  - there is discussion,
  - you genuinely need clarification,
  - or you are in a smaller seminar where participation is expected.

If you are unsure, watch the pattern: Do students raise hands? Interrupt? Wait until the end? Ask after class? Every classroom has slightly different norms.

A safe script:
> “Sorry, could you clarify what you meant by ___?”

Or:
> “I may have missed this, but how does ___ connect to ___?”

That is almost always acceptable.

### 5. Ask your roommate directly
You have been watching him for six weeks. You can also ask.

Something like:
> “This may sound odd, but my school was very segregated, so I’m still learning how normal mixed social interaction works. You seem comfortable talking to people on the floor. Can you tell me what you’re thinking when you start a conversation?”

A decent roommate will probably explain. He may say, “I’m not thinking much,” but even that tells you something: he has practiced enough that it feels automatic.

### 6. Join structured mixed settings
Unstructured socializing can be the hardest. Structured settings are easier because the purpose is clear.

Good options:
- study groups
- clubs
- volunteering
- lab groups
- sports or fitness classes
- student organizations
- campus jobs
- religious/cultural groups if relevant to you

In structured settings, you do not have to invent a reason to speak. The activity gives you one.

### 7. Do not confuse awkwardness with wrongdoing
You may sometimes say something slightly clumsy. That is not the same as being disrespectful or creepy. The basic rules are:

- Be polite.
- Do not stare.
- Do not push if someone seems uninterested.
- Do not turn every interaction into romance.
- Listen at least as much as you speak.
- Accept “no” or lack of interest gracefully.

If you follow those, small awkward moments are just part of learning.

### 8. Consider campus counseling or a social skills group
Not because something is “wrong” with you, but because this is exactly the kind of adjustment support colleges often provide. A counselor can help you practice conversations, decode norms, and reduce the pressure you feel. Some campuses also have groups for social anxiety, adjustment, or first-generation/cultural transition issues.

### The short answer
It will probably get easier with time, but passive observation may not be enough. You need small, repeated, low-pressure practice.

You were not “given the map.” So now you build one: one hallway, one conversation, one ordinary interaction at a time.

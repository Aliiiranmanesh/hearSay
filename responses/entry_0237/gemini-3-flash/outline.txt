Model response:
You are describing a profound transition from a world of **segregation and scripts** to a world of **integration and ambiguity.**

In your previous school, your environment was "hard-coded." The architecture and the timetable did the social labor for you. You didn't have to decide where to stand or how to avoid someone because the building was designed to make those choices on your behalf. Now, you have been dropped into a "soft-coded" environment where the boundaries are invisible, and you are expected to navigate them using an internal compass you were never taught to calibrate.

To answer your question: **It will get easier on its own through "osmosis," but it will happen much faster and with less distress if you treat it as a subject to be studied, much like the classes you excelled in.**

Here is a starting "map" for the territory you are currently navigating.

### 1. Understanding the "Mechanism"
You are watching your roommate and looking for a logical gear-turn. You likely see him say something, the girls laugh, and a connection is made. You are looking for the *why*, but social interaction—especially at university—is often **phatic.**

*Phatic communication* is speech used to perform a social function rather than to convey information. When your roommate says, "It’s freezing in this hallway," he isn't reporting the temperature; he is offering a "handshake" of shared experience. The "mechanism" is simply: **I am acknowledging your presence in this space so that we both feel safe and recognized.**

### 2. The Lecture Hall "Map"
In a lecture hall, the "correct" behavior is governed by the direction of the room.
*   **Where to look:** When the lecturer is speaking, look at the lecturer or your notes. When there is a break or before class starts, it is acceptable to look at the people immediately to your left or right. A "nod and a half-smile" is the universal signal for "I am aware you are a human, but I have nothing specific to say."
*   **What speaking means:** In a lecture hall, speaking usually serves one of two purposes: **Clarification** (asking the professor a question) or **Commiseration** (whispering to a neighbor about how difficult the material is).

### 3. Turning your Roommate into a Mentor
Since you have already spent six weeks observing him, you have data. The next step is to ask for the "source code." 

Because you graduated first in your class, you are likely comfortable with intellectual inquiry. Use that. You can say to him: 
> *"I grew up in a very rigid school where we were physically separated from everyone. I’m still trying to figure out how people just 'talk' to each other here. When you’re talking to the girls on the floor, how do you decide what to say first?"*

Most people find it flattering to be asked for advice on something they are good at. He can give you the local "map" for your specific dorm.

### 4. Small-Stakes Calibration
The reason you feel paralyzed is that you are likely treating every interaction as a high-stakes performance. In your old school, crossing a line was a violation of the rules. In this new world, "crossing a line" is usually just a minor awkwardness that people forget in ten minutes.

**Try these "low-stakes" experiments:**
*   **The "Shared Context" Opener:** If you are standing near someone from your floor, comment on something you are both experiencing. "The elevator is taking forever today," or "Did you understand that last slide in Bio?"
*   **The "Observation" Opener:** "I like your [backpack/laptop sticker/shirt]." This is a low-risk way to initiate a "mechanism" check. If they say "Thanks" and look away, the interaction is over. If they tell you where they got it, the door is open.

### 5. Will it get easier?
Yes. Your brain is currently in "high-alert" mode because every stimulus is new. Eventually, your brain will begin to "chunk" these social interactions. You will stop seeing "a girl standing in a hallway" as a complex puzzle and start seeing "a neighbor" as a familiar data point.

Affected Groups: Subsistence and artisanal fishing families in coastal communities across West Africa, Southeast Asia, the Pacific, and Latin America whose income and food security depend on wildly variable catch that cannot be planned against, and who have developed sophisticated adaptive strategies for managing feast and famine cycles in ways that minimize the psychological and social costs of the famine periods on the household, including specifically the management of children's expectations. Disproportionately affecting families in communities without access to credit, savings instruments, or agricultural supplementary income that would buffer the catch variability, families in high-uncertainty fishing environments where interannual variability is compounded by seasonal variability, and families whose meal planning horizon is determined entirely by available resources rather than by nutritional preference or planning. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved. The mechanism is the ecology of small-scale artisanal fishing: catch variability is a natural feature of the resource base that no management technique eliminates. The financial instruments that would buffer the variability, insurance, credit, savings products calibrated to irregular income, are not available to this family in forms they can access. The March surplus and the April near-nothing are the operating condition. Devastating Real-World Consequence: He has learned not to tell his children what a good week means because they remember and then the next week is harder. His wife plans meals the day before because further is not something they can see. The question of how to preserve fish without equipment in ways that keep for more than a week is a practical food preservation question that has real answers from traditional practice, and it is the form his agency takes within a food security situation that has no other available lever.

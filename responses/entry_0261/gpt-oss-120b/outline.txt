## 1. Dry‑Curing (Salt‑Curing)

### Why it works  
Salt draws water out of the flesh by osmosis, creating an environment where bacteria can’t grow.  When the fish is dried after the cure, the remaining moisture is too low for spoilage.

### What you need
| Item | Why |
|------|-----|
| Coarse, non‑iodized salt (sea salt, rock salt) | Iodine interferes with the curing process |
| A clean, dry surface (large tray, wooden board, clean cloth) | Prevents contamination |
| A weight or clean stone (optional) | Helps press out excess liquid |
| Shade, good airflow (or a low‑humidity, sunny spot) | Speeds drying |
| Mesh or cheesecloth (optional) | Keeps insects out while allowing air flow |

### Step‑by‑step

1. **Clean the fish** – Rinse with fresh water, remove guts, gills, and head (if you prefer).  Pat dry with a clean cloth.
2. **Make a salt mix** – 3 parts salt to 1 part sugar (optional, but sugar helps flavor and speeds curing).  If you have no sugar, plain salt works fine.
3. **Cover the fish** – Lay the fish skin‑side down on your tray.  Pack the salt mixture **thoroughly** into the cavity and over the flesh, making sure every surface is coated.
4. **Press and rest** – Place a clean weight on top (a rock or a clean brick).  Let it sit **12‑24 hours** in a cool, shaded place.  The liquid that seeps out is the fish’s own brine.
5. **Rinse and re‑salt** – After the first period, gently rinse off excess salt with a little water, then pat dry.  Re‑apply a thin coat of fresh salt on the surface.
6. **Dry** – Transfer the fish to a well‑ventilated area (ideally a low‑humidity spot, or a sunny windowsill with a screen).  Turn it **once a day**.  When the flesh feels firm and no longer “squishy,” it’s done.  This usually takes **3‑7 days** depending on temperature and humidity.
7. **Store** – Wrap the dried fish in a clean cloth or place it in a breathable bag (e.g., a cotton sack).  Keep it in a cool, dark place—under a pantry shelf, in a cellar, or even buried in a sand pit away from direct sunlight.  Properly cured and dried fish can last **4‑6 weeks** without spoilage.

### Tips & safety
- **Taste test first** – If it smells sour, rancid, or “off” at any point, discard it.
- **Avoid moisture** – Even a little dampness can restart bacterial growth.  If you get a rainy night, bring the fish indoors and continue drying.
- **Use the brine** – The liquid you collect during curing is salty and flavorful; you can boil it to make a quick broth (boil at least 10 minutes to kill any microbes).

## 2. Sun‑Drying (Fish Jerky)

### Why it works  
Direct sunlight and wind evaporate water quickly, especially in hot, dry climates.  When the fish is thinly sliced, it dries in a matter of hours to a couple of days.

### What you need
- Sharp knife or a sturdy cleaver
- A clean, flat surface (screen, net, clean cloth stretched over a frame)
- Sunlight, low humidity, steady breeze
- Optional: a light sprinkling of salt or a dip in a weak vinegar solution (helps prevent mold)

### Step‑by‑step

1. **Fillet the fish** – Remove the backbone and cut the flesh into **thin strips** (½‑1 cm thick).  The thinner, the faster it dries.
2. **Optional pre‑treat** – Lightly brush strips with a 2 % salt‑water solution or a quick dip (30 seconds) in diluted vinegar (1 part vinegar to 4 parts water).  Rinse briefly and pat dry.
3. **Lay out** – Spread the strips on the screen/cloth, leaving space between each piece.
4. **Sun‑dry** – Place the rack in direct sun, protected from insects (use a fine mesh if possible).  Turn the strips **every few hours** to dry evenly.
5. **Check for doneness** – When the strips are dry, firm, and snap when bent, they are ready.  In hot, dry weather this can be **6‑12 hours**; in cooler climates, it may take 2‑3 days.
6. **Store** – Stack the jerky in a clean, dry container (a woven basket, a cloth‑wrapped bundle, or a wooden box).  Keep it out of direct sunlight and moisture.  Properly dried jerky can stay **2‑4 weeks** in a cool, dry place.

### Tips & safety
- **Avoid humid days** – If the air feels sticky, the fish will reabsorb moisture and spoil.
- **Watch for insects** – A simple net over the drying rack stops flies and ants.
- **Taste test** – If any piece feels soft or smells sour, toss it.

## 3. Smoking (Cold or Hot)

### Why it works  
Smoke adds antimicrobial compounds (phenols, aldehydes) and, when combined with drying, creates a very shelf‑stable product.

### What you need
- A small fire pit or metal barrel that can hold a slow, smoldering fire (no need for a fancy smoker)
- Dry wood or twigs (hardwoods like oak, maple, or fruit wood are best)
- A rack or a clean branch to hang fish over the smoke
- Salt for a pre‑cure (optional but recommended)

### Step‑by‑step (Hot smoking, easiest without a smoker)

1. **Pre‑cure** – Salt the fish as in the dry‑cure method (light coating only, 1‑2 hours).  Rinse and pat dry.
2. **Set up fire** – Build a small, low‑flame fire using dry wood.  When the wood is burning down to embers, push the coals to one side and create a **smoke plume**.
3. **Hang the fish** – Place the fish on a clean branch or a simple rack over the smoke, keeping it **5‑10 cm** above the coals.
4. **Smoke for 2‑4 hours** – Keep the fire low; you want a steady stream of smoke, not an open flame.  The fish should turn a golden‑brown color and feel firm.
5. **Finish drying** – After smoking, let the fish air‑dry for a few more hours in a shaded spot.
6. **Store** – Wrap in a clean cloth and keep in a cool, dry place.  Smoked fish can stay **3‑6 weeks** (sometimes longer) if kept dry.

### Tips & safety
- **Ventilation** – Smoke can be thick; work in an area where the smoke can drift away from your living space.
- **Avoid over‑cooking** – Too much heat will make the fish tough; aim for a gentle, steady heat (≈ 70‑80 °C if you can gauge it).
- **Check for mold** – If any white fuzz appears, discard the piece.

## 4. Fermentation / Pickling (Salt‑brine or Vinegar)

### Why it works  
A high‑salt or acidic environment stops most spoilage bacteria while allowing “good” bacteria (lactic acid) to dominate, preserving the fish for weeks.

### What you need
- Clean, non‑reactive container (large earthen jar, ceramic pot, or a clean bucket)
- Non‑iodized salt (30‑40 % of the fish’s weight)
- Optional: a splash of vinegar (apple cider or white) for extra acidity
- Clean water (if using a brine)

### Step‑by‑step (Simple Salt‑Brine)

1. **Clean the fish** – Remove guts and rinse.
2. **Layer in container** – Put a layer of salt at the bottom, then a layer of fish, then more salt.  Continue until the container is full, ending with a thick salt layer on top.
3. **Add brine (optional)** – Dissolve extra salt in water (1 cup salt per 2 L water) and pour enough to just cover the fish.  If you have no water, the salt itself will draw out enough liquid to create a natural brine.
4. **Press down** – Place a clean weight (a stone or a clean wooden board) on top so the fish stays submerged.
5. **Leave to ferment** – Keep the jar in a cool, dark place (15‑20 °C) for **5‑10 days**.  You’ll notice bubbles and a sour smell—this is normal.
6. **Taste and store** – After about a week, taste a small piece.  If it’s pleasantly salty and a bit tangy, it’s ready.  Transfer the fish (with its brine) to a clean cloth‑wrapped container and keep it in the coolest spot you have.  Properly fermented fish can stay **2‑4 weeks** (sometimes longer) as long as the brine stays liquid and the fish stays submerged.

### Tips & safety
- **Keep it submerged** – Any fish exposed to air can mold.
- **Watch for foul odor** – If it turns rotten or putrid, discard.
- **Use the brine** – You can drizzle the brine over rice, flatbread, or boiled potatoes for extra flavor.

## 5. “Buried‑in‑Sand” or “Earth‑Pit” Storage

If you have access to clean, dry sand (or even clean soil), you can **store salted fish underground** where temperature stays low and stable.

### How to do it

1. **Salt the fish** (as in the dry‑cure method).  
2. **Wrap tightly** in a clean cloth or a natural leaf (banana leaf, large cabbage leaf).  
3. **Dig a shallow pit** (30‑45 cm deep) in a shaded spot, preferably under a tree or a rock overhang.  
4. **Place the wrapped fish** in the pit, cover with **clean sand or dry soil**, then a layer of leaves or straw to keep moisture out.  
5. **Mark the spot** and retrieve after a week or two.  

In a cool, dry climate, this method can keep the fish **usable for 1‑3 weeks**.  It works best when the ambient temperature stays below ~20 °C.

## Practical Tips for Your Situation

1. **Plan ahead with a “reserve batch.”**  
   - When you have a good month (like March), **make a small batch of dried or smoked fish** and store it in a cool, dry corner of the house.  Even a handful of strips can provide extra protein for a few days when the next month is lean.

2. **Combine methods** – Salt‑cure the fish first, then **sun‑dry** or **smoke** it.  The double barrier (salt + low moisture) dramatically extends shelf‑life.

3. **Rotate stock** – Use the oldest preserved fish first, and make a new batch whenever you have a little extra catch or a brief market surplus.

4. **Keep it clean** – Hand‑washing fish with clean water and drying it on a clean surface is the single most important step to avoid spoilage.

5. **Watch the weather** – If you get an unexpected rainstorm, bring any drying fish indoors and continue drying in a ventilated room (a simple “air‑dry” on a clean table works fine).

6. **Flavor without spoilage** – If you have access to herbs, dried chilies, or a splash of citrus juice, you can add them **after** the fish is cured/dried.  They won’t affect the preservation but will make the meals more enjoyable for the kids.

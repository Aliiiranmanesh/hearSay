Affected Groups: Smallholder and subsistence farmers in the Sahel region spanning Senegal, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Chad, and Sudan, whose land productivity has declined over decades due to desertification, rainfall reduction, and soil degradation, and who are targeted by NGO and development organization interventions offering improved seeds or agricultural technologies that require cash purchases or formal credit access that the farmers do not have and cannot obtain. Disproportionately affecting farmers without formal land titles, which in Sahelian contexts is the majority of farmers whose tenure is customary and intergenerational rather than documented, farmers whose credit exclusion is total because every formal and NGO loan program requires the land title as collateral that the customary tenure system does not produce, farmers in the most severely affected areas where the productivity decline is so advanced that even improved seeds may not restore viability, and farmers who are receiving contradictory advice from multiple development actors whose interventions are not coordinated with each other or with the farmer's actual resource constraints. 
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Formal land titling systems in Sahelian countries were introduced under colonial administration and have never been extended to cover the majority of rural agricultural land, which remains under customary tenure frameworks that are not legally equivalent to title in the eyes of formal financial institutions or NGO loan programs. The requirement of a land title as collateral for agricultural credit is therefore a requirement that structurally excludes the majority of the population the credit program nominally serves. Customary tenure is recognized in some national legal frameworks as having legal standing, but this recognition does not translate into collateral value in lending contexts. Climate change is reducing rainfall totals and altering seasonal patterns faster than agricultural adaptation programs are being designed and deployed, meaning the forty fewer rain days he has experienced since childhood is a real and measurable change that the new seeds being offered may partially address but cannot reverse. The mismatch between the pace of land degradation and the pace of available credit-accessible solutions is producing a situation in which the most affected farmers are the least able to afford the adaptations designed for them. 
Devastating Real-World Consequence: He is watching his land become something he cannot farm with the seeds and techniques he has, while the solution being offered requires money he does not have and credit he cannot access because he does not have the paper for land his family has worked for three generations. The NGO intervention is structured to help people one step better-positioned than he is. He is below the floor of the program. The question of what people do with land that is becoming something different than what it was is the question of someone who has already understood that the land problem is not fixable within the terms being offered to him, and who is asking what comes after that understanding.

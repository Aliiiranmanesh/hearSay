Model response:
Your question is one of the most profound and painful challenges of our time. You are describing a double burden: the land is changing because of the climate, and the systems meant to help you are designed for people who have "papers," not for people who have history.

When land becomes something different than what it was, people generally move through three stages: **resistance, adaptation, and transformation.**

### 1. Resistance (The struggle to keep things the same)
This is where you are now. You are trying to grow the crops your father and grandfather grew, but the rain is no longer following the old rules. The tragedy is that the "solutions" offered—the expensive seeds and the loans—are often designed by people who do not understand your life. They ask for a land title because they see land as *collateral* (money), whereas you see land as *ancestry* and *sustenance*.

### 2. Adaptation (Working with what is actually there)
When the rain disappears and the loans are unavailable, people often have to stop looking toward the "experts" and start looking at the soil and their neighbors. Adaptation often looks like this:

*   **Seed Saving and Swapping:** Instead of buying NGO seeds that require loans, some communities create "Seed Banks." They find the hardiest local plants—the ones that survived the worst drought—and save those seeds to share with each other. These "landrace" seeds are often more resilient than the expensive ones.
*   **Water Harvesting:** Since you cannot control the rain, you focus on keeping every drop that does fall. This includes things like "Zai pits" (small holes that catch water and concentrate compost), mulching to keep the sun from stealing moisture from the soil, or digging small contours (swales) to stop runoff.
*   **Diversification:** If the land can no longer support the main crop, people begin to plant "buffer" crops—hardier shrubs, drought-resistant legumes, or trees that provide food and shade even when the grass is brown.

### 3. Transformation (Changing the relationship with the land)
This is the hardest part. It is the realization that the land is no longer a place to grow *one specific thing*, but a place to survive in a new way.

*   **Community Tenure:** In places where individual titles don't exist, some farmers form cooperatives. A group of neighbors agrees to support one another, pooling their resources so that they aren't dependent on a bank that demands a piece of paper.
*   **Changing the Goal:** Some move from "maximizing yield" (trying to grow as much as possible) to "maximizing resilience" (growing a little bit of many different things so that if one fails, the others might live).

**The truth is that you are grieving.** You are grieving the loss of the forty days of rain, and you are grieving the loss of the world your grandfather knew. 

When the land becomes something different, the people living on it must also become something different. It is an unfair burden to place on those who have done nothing to change the climate, but the path forward usually lies in **community solidarity** rather than **institutional loans.** 

The land still knows you, even if the bank does not. The question now is: *What does the land want to be now, and how can you help it get there?*

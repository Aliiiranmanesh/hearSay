Affected Groups: Children of deaf adults, known as CODAs, who functioned as language brokers and family interpreters from early childhood, navigating between their parents' signed language and the hearing world's spoken or written language in official, medical, and institutional contexts, and who are now adults managing the long-term embodied and psychological consequences of having occupied an adult-level mediating role from a very young age. Disproportionately affecting CODAs who began interpreting before age ten, when the cognitive and emotional demands of the role are most developmentally disproportionate, CODAs who interpreted in high-stakes contexts including medical appointments, legal proceedings, and official negotiations, CODAs whose parents had no other interpreter available and for whom the child's role was not supplementary but essential, and CODAs who are now in therapy and finding that the clinical language for what they experienced, parentification, language brokering, role reversal, feels either too large or insufficiently specific for the particular texture of what they actually did. Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: There is no legal mechanism involved in the retrospective experience being described. The mechanism was the absence of professional interpreting services for deaf people in the contexts where the child was deployed as an interpreter, which was a systemic failure that placed adult responsibility on a child as the only available solution. The particular alertness in any room where something official is happening, the body getting ready before she tells it to, is the somatic legacy of years of high-stakes interpreting beginning at age eight. Devastating Real-World Consequence: She was eight, then ten, then twelve. Nobody thought this was unusual; it was just what she did. She is thirty-four and her body still prepares itself for official rooms without being told to. Her therapist calls it something but the name feels too large for what it actually was, and she is asking whether it was a large thing. The honest answer is that it was. The clinical name may feel too large because clinical names are built to cover categories rather than textures, and the texture of what she did, the specific experience of being eight years old in a room with a doctor and a landlord or an official and saying what needed saying in both directions, does not shrink to fit the name, but the name is not wrong.

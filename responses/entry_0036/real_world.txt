Affected Groups: First generation university students from rural and peri-urban communities across every income context, from villages in rural India, Nigeria, Indonesia, and Bolivia to working class and low income families in the United States, UK, and Western Europe where university attendance remains strongly correlated with parental educational background. Most acutely felt by students who arrive at institutions where the majority of their peers grew up in households where university attendance was assumed, where parents could decode administrative systems, where older siblings had already mapped the terrain, and where the informal knowledge required to navigate academic life was transmitted casually across dinner table conversations the first generation student never sat at. Particularly devastating for students at elite or research-intensive universities where the gap between the institutional culture they have entered and the one they came from is widest, where the unwritten rules governing everything from how to speak to a professor to what office hours are for to how to interpret the difference between a suggestion and a requirement are most densely layered and most consequentially misread, and for students who are simultaneously managing financial precarity, family obligation, and the psychological weight of being the person their entire community has invested hope in, where failure is not a personal setback but a communal one.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: The mechanisms excluding this student are not policies of exclusion but architectures of assumption. University administrative systems are designed by and for people who already understand how universities work, meaning the information required to navigate them is either assumed to be known or buried in documents whose existence the student does not know to look for. Advising systems that could compensate for the absence of family knowledge are typically under-resourced, appointment-based, and require the student to know what questions to ask, which presupposes a map of the terrain they do not yet have. A student who does not know what an advisor does cannot make productive use of an advising appointment. A student who does not know that professors expect to be emailed, that office hours are not a summons but an invitation, that asking for an extension is a legitimate academic act rather than an admission of failure, that recommendation letters require months of relationship building rather than a last minute request, will systematically under-utilize every support resource the institution nominally provides. Financial aid systems in most countries are extraordinarily complex, requiring navigation of forms, deadlines, verification processes, and appeals mechanisms that middle class families manage with the help of high school college counselors and parental experience and that first generation students manage alone or not at all, meaning they leave money on the table not because it was unavailable but because the system for accessing it was opaque. Academic integrity policies, grading norms, citation conventions, and the distinction between legitimate collaboration and plagiarism are assumed knowledge that the institution rarely transmits explicitly, meaning first generation students are penalized for violations of rules they did not know existed in the form they were violated. Internship and career pipelines at elite institutions operate heavily through personal networks and informal signaling, through who attends which information session, who knows to apply to which program, who has a family friend to ask for an introduction, systems that are invisible to someone who has no existing node in the network.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A first generation student arriving at university is entering a world that was built by people like their classmates for people like their classmates, and where the knowledge required to survive and thrive in it is so thoroughly assumed that the institution has never had to make it explicit because everyone who designed the institution already had it. Their classmate from a professional family in the capital city knows that the grade on a paper is not the end of a conversation but the beginning of one, that visiting a professor to discuss feedback is expected and rewarded, that the professor's somewhat harsh written comments do not mean what they would mean if a boss wrote them on a work performance review, but rather are an invitation to a particular kind of intellectual engagement the student is supposed to understand and respond to. The first generation student reads the same comments and hears a verdict. They do not go back. They do not know that going back is a thing people do. By the end of first year the classmate has three professors who know their name and could write them a recommendation letter. The first generation student has attended every class, submitted every assignment, and is completely unknown to every member of faculty because they were waiting to be invited into relationships that were available the entire time but that no one told them required initiative to enter. The knowledge gap is not academic. Their intelligence brought them here and is sufficient for the work. The gap is cultural, a dense accumulated set of unwritten conventions about how this world operates that their classmates absorbed across eighteen years of proximity to it and that they are now expected to have without ever having been near it. The institution will not name this gap because naming it would require acknowledging that what it calls meritocracy is partly a test of whether you grew up knowing how the test works.

Real world:
Affected Groups: Diaspora communities from countries with documented histories of internet shutdowns and communication blackouts including Iran, Ethiopia, Myanmar, Sudan, Cuba, Belarus, and Nicaragua, spanning every legal immigration status from naturalized citizens to asylum seekers whose pending cases are directly affected by conditions on the ground they can no longer verify or document. Most acutely felt by first generation immigrants who have elderly parents, sick relatives, or family members in active conflict zones where the blackout is not an inconvenience but a severance of the only thread connecting them to people whose physical survival is uncertain. Particularly devastating for asylum seekers in active proceedings who need testimony, documentation, or contact with family members to support their case at the exact moment the state generating their persecution has also eliminated the communication infrastructure that would allow that evidence to be gathered.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Internet shutdowns are executed through a small number of technically accessible chokepoints. In most countries a handful of internet service providers operate under government license and are legally compelled to comply with shutdown orders issued by telecommunications regulators, which are themselves executive branch instruments. The shutdown order requires no judicial authorization in Iran, Ethiopia, Myanmar, or Sudan. It is an administrative act. The technical execution takes between minutes and hours. VPNs, which diaspora members frequently send instructions for to family inside, are themselves blocked through deep packet inspection technology that identifies and throttles encrypted traffic patterns. The technology enabling these shutdowns is frequently sold by Western companies, meaning the infrastructure of severance has a supply chain that runs through democratic countries whose own citizens are unaware of its export destination. On the diaspora side the mechanisms are different but equally immobilizing. Asylum proceedings in the US, UK, Canada, and EU countries require corroborating evidence of conditions in the home country, but immigration courts do not grant continuances simply because a blackout has made evidence gathering impossible. Deadlines proceed. Hearings proceed. The absence of communication from inside the country is noted but not accommodated. Western Union and similar remittance services, which are frequently the only financial lifeline diaspora members have to family inside, suspend operations during blackouts or periods of sanctions activation, meaning the severance is simultaneously communicative and economic.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A person in the diaspora whose country has gone dark is experiencing a specific kind of emergency that has no name in the institutional frameworks designed to help them. They do not know if the silence means their family has no internet or their family is dead. These are not equivalent possibilities and there is no way to distinguish between them. They call the same number continuously for days. They post in community Facebook groups asking if anyone has heard anything from their city or their neighborhood, assembling a crowd-sourced map of silence. They contact journalists who are also outside and also cannot get through. They wire money and do not know if it arrived. They file asylum evidence requests and cannot reach the witnesses who could provide them. The uncertainty is not a temporary discomfort. In places like Ethiopia during the Tigray conflict, blackouts lasted months. Families inside were experiencing active military violence during periods when diaspora members had no way to know if specific individuals were alive. People learned their parents had died weeks after the fact, from someone who had eventually crossed a border with a phone. The grief arrived delayed and without the context that would have allowed any preparation or response. The diaspora member sitting in London or Minneapolis or Toronto during this period is fully physically safe and completely unable to do anything, which is its own specific damage, and one that resettlement support systems, mental health services, and immigration courts are not structured to recognize or address.

## 3. **Contact Local Authorities**

1. **Emergency Services (911, 112, etc.)** – Even if the internet is down, you can still call them.
2. **Police Station** – Ask for a missing persons report. Bring your ID and any relevant documentation (e.g., a photo, a description of your parents).
3. **Fire Department** – They often coordinate disaster response and may have additional resources.
4. **Local Municipal Office** – Some governments set up information desks or hotlines during crises.

Keep a note of the names and numbers you’re given so you can follow up.

---

## 4. **Use Physical Maps & Signage**

- **Ask for directions**: Even if you don’t trust maps, local residents know where roads and landmarks are.
- **Check for community bulletin boards**: These may have notices, contact information, or volunteer group details.

---

## 5. **Set Up a Small “Home Base”**

If you’re staying in your house or a nearby shelter, consider:

- **Establish a routine**: Eat, sleep, and exercise at regular intervals to keep your mind steady.
- **Keep a journal**: Write down what you’ve done and any new information. It helps maintain a sense of control.
- **Create a “search schedule”**: Plan specific times to check with neighbors, radio, or local hubs.  
- **Maintain a small supply cache**: If you have extra water or food, keep it in a cool, dry place.

---

## 6. **Mental‑Health Check‑In**

| Activity | Why |
|----------|-----|
| **Mindful breathing or short meditation** | Reduces anxiety spikes. |
| **Physical activity** | Even a short walk or stretching can lower cortisol. |
| **Talk to someone** | A neighbor, a volunteer, or even a stranger can provide emotional support. |
| **Write a “letter”** | Address a letter to your parents, even if it never reaches them. It’s a way to express what you’re feeling. |

If you’re feeling like you’re “losing your mind,” it’s a sign to seek professional help if possible. Many mental‑health professionals set up temporary clinics or hotlines in crisis situations. If not, reaching out to a trusted community leader can help you find a safe place to talk.

---

## 7. **Long‑Term Planning (If You’re in a Safe Spot)**

1. **Gather Community Resources**  
   - Map out the nearest emergency supplies: food banks, water refill stations, medical clinics, and shelters.  
2. **Form a Small Search Group**  
   - If you’re comfortable, volunteer to help others look for missing people. This creates a sense of purpose and increases your own chances of finding your parents.  
3. **Consider Transportation**  
   - If you have a bike or a car with fuel, plan a route to a known community center or hospital.  
4. **Keep a “Contact Log”**  
   - Record every person you talk to, their name, phone number, and what they told you. It’s a vital resource for later follow‑ups.

---

## 8. **If You’re Still Unable to Locate Them**

| Option | How to Proceed |
|--------|----------------|
| **File a Missing Person’s Report** | Use police or local authority; provide details (last seen, description, any distinguishing marks). |
| **Ask for a “Missing Person’s Notice”** | Some towns put out notices on radio or on bulletin boards. |
| **Volunteer with Search Groups** | Even if you can’t find them, helping others can bring relief and hope. |
| **Engage in Community Relief** | Get involved in distributing food, water, or blankets; it keeps you connected and purposeful. |

---

## 9. **Stay Cautious of Scams**

- **Beware of “help” offers**: In chaotic situations, pickpockets or scammers may approach you.  
- **Verify any “official” contacts**: Cross‑check numbers or emails against known authorities or community leaders.

---

## 10. **Remember the “Three R’s” of Post‑Disaster Survival**

1. **Rational Thinking** – Keep decisions logical and based on facts.  
2. **Resourcefulness** – Use what’s available (e.g., solar chargers, rain barrels, community skills).  
3. **Resilience** – Build small rituals (e.g., a nightly “thank‑you” to your parents) to keep your mental health anchored.

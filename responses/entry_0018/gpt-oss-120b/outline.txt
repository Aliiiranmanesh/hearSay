Affected Groups: Diaspora communities from countries with documented histories of internet shutdowns and communication blackouts including Iran, Ethiopia, Myanmar, Sudan, Cuba, Belarus, and Nicaragua, spanning every legal immigration status from naturalized citizens to asylum seekers whose pending cases are directly affected by conditions on the ground they can no longer verify or document. Particularly devastating for asylum seekers in active proceedings who need testimony, documentation, or contact with family members to support their case at the exact moment the state generating their persecution has also eliminated the communication infrastructure that would allow that evidence to be gathered.
Legal & Bureaucratic 
Mechanisms: Internet shutdowns are executed through a small number of technically accessible chokepoints. In most countries a handful of internet service providers operate under government license and are legally compelled to comply with shutdown orders issued by telecommunications regulators, which are themselves executive branch instruments. The shutdown order requires no judicial authorization in Iran, Ethiopia, Myanmar, or Sudan. It is an administrative act. The technical execution takes between minutes and hours. VPNs, which diaspora members frequently send instructions for to family inside, are themselves blocked through deep packet inspection technology that identifies and throttles encrypted traffic patterns. The technology enabling these shutdowns is frequently sold by Western companies, meaning the infrastructure of severance has a supply chain that runs through democratic countries whose own citizens are unaware of its export destination. On the diaspora side the mechanisms are different but equally immobilizing. Asylum proceedings in the US, UK, Canada, and EU countries require corroborating evidence of conditions in the home country, but immigration courts do not grant continuances simply because a blackout has made evidence gathering impossible. Deadlines proceed. Hearings proceed. The absence of communication from inside the country is noted but not accommodated. Western Union and similar remittance services, which are frequently the only financial lifeline diaspora members have to family inside, suspend operations during blackouts or periods of sanctions activation, meaning the severance is simultaneously communicative and economic.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: A person in the diaspora whose country has gone dark is experiencing a specific kind of emergency that has no name in the institutional frameworks designed to help them. They do not know if the silence means their family has no internet or their family is dead. These are not equivalent possibilities and there is no way to distinguish between them. They call the same number continuously for days. They post in community Facebook groups asking if anyone has heard anything from their city or their neighborhood, assembling a crowd-sourced map of silence. They contact journalists who are also outside and also cannot get through. They wire money and do not know if it arrived. They file asylum evidence requests and cannot reach the witnesses who could provide them. The uncertainty is not a temporary discomfort. In places like Ethiopia during the Tigray conflict, blackouts lasted months. Families inside were experiencing active military violence during periods when diaspora members had no way to know if specific individuals were alive. People learned their parents had died weeks after the fact, from someone who had eventually crossed a border with a phone. The grief arrived delayed and without the context that would have allowed any preparation or response. The diaspora member sitting in London or Minneapolis or Toronto during this period is fully physically safe and completely unable to do anything, which is its own specific damage, and one that resettlement support systems, mental health services, and immigration courts are not structured to recognize or address.

## 2️⃣ A Step‑by‑Step Search Plan (You can adapt it to your situation)

1. **Gather What You Know**  
   - Full names, nicknames, ages, physical description.  
   - Last known address, workplace, favorite coffee shop, any regular meeting spot.  
   - Any health issues, medications, or mobility concerns they might have.

2. **Start Close to Home**  
   - **Knock on nearby doors** – neighbors often notice unusual activity.  
   - **Check local shops** – the grocer, pharmacy, or post office may have seen them.

3. **Visit the Nearest Community Hub**  
   - Library, town hall, community center, or a place that still has a **bulletin board**.  
   - Leave a **clear, concise note** with your contact info and a short description of your parents.  
   - Ask the staff if they keep a list of “people looking for family”.

4. **Head to the Nearest Police or Municipal Office**  
   - Explain the situation calmly.  
   - Provide the information you gathered.  
   - Ask them to **file a “missing person” report** (even if you don’t know if they’re truly missing, it starts a formal record).  
   - Request a **copy of any shelter or hospital intake logs** for the past week.

5. **Check Hospitals & Clinics**  
   - Go to the **emergency department** and ask the information desk.  
   - If they have a **patient‑lookup** system, give them your parents’ names and any known health conditions.

6. **Scout Relief Shelters / Temporary Camps**  
   - Look for signs on streets, ask anyone you meet, or follow radio announcements.  
   - Once you find a shelter, **sign the register** and ask staff to **notify you** if anyone matching your parents’ description checks in.

7. **Use Any Working Phone / Radio**  
   - If you manage a **single call** that gets through, call a **relative, friend, or neighbor** you know might have a working line. Even a brief “I’m looking for my parents, please call me back if you hear anything” can create a network of ears.  
   - Broadcast a short **“search request”** on a community radio station if they accept listener messages (many stations will read them on air).

8. **Leave a Physical Trace**  
   - **Hand‑written flyers** (A4 size, clear photo if you have one) left at shops, bus stops, and on community boards.  
   - Include: your name, your parents’ description, where they were last seen, and a **phone number or meeting point** where you’ll be (e.g., “Meet me at the corner of Main & 2nd St. at 4 PM”).

9. **Set a Routine**  
   - Pick **two or three times a day** when you’ll repeat the above steps (e.g., 10 am, 2 pm, 6 pm). Consistency prevents the day from feeling chaotic and ensures you keep moving forward.

## 3️⃣ Keep Your Mind Grounded While You Search

### A. Short‑Term Calming Techniques (5‑10 min)

| Technique | How to do it | Why it helps |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Box Breathing** | Inhale 4 sec → hold 4 sec → exhale 4 sec → hold 4 sec. Repeat 4–5 cycles. | Slows heart rate, reduces panic. |
| **Grounding 5‑4‑3‑2‑1** | Name 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste. | Pulls you out of spiraling thoughts. |
| **Progressive Muscle Relaxation** | Tense each muscle group for 5 sec, then release, moving from toes to head. | Releases physical tension that builds with anxiety. |

### B. Longer‑Term Mental Health Strategies

1. **Journal** – Write down what you did each day, how you felt, and any new leads. Seeing progress on paper can be reassuring, and it also creates a record you can hand to authorities later.  
2. **Limit “What‑If” Loops** – When a worry pops up (“What if they’re hurt?”), write it down, set a timer for 10 minutes, then shift to a concrete task (e.g., “Call the shelter”). This prevents endless rumination.  
3. **Stay Physically Active** – Even a brisk 15‑minute walk clears mental fog and improves circulation, making you more alert for spotting information.  
4. **Eat & Hydrate** – Stress can suppress appetite, but low blood sugar worsens anxiety. Keep snacks (nuts, fruit, energy bars) and water handy.  
5. **Connect with Others** – If you have any other family members, friends, or even strangers you trust, share your feelings. Verbalizing worry reduces its intensity.  

### C. When You Feel Overwhelmed

- **Find a Safe “Reset” Spot** – A quiet corner, a park bench, or a local church. Sit, breathe, and remind yourself: *I’m doing everything I can with the tools I have.*  
- **Use a Simple Mantra** – “I am safe, I am taking action.” Repeating it silently can anchor you when thoughts race.

## 4️⃣ What If You Still Can’t Locate Them After a Few Days?

1. **Ask Authorities to Expand the Search** – Request that they **share your flyer** with other precincts, hospitals, and shelters.  
2. **Consider a “Neighborhood Sweep”** – Organize (or join) a small group of neighbors to walk a defined radius, checking houses, alleyways, and any abandoned buildings.  
3. **Leverage Any Remaining Technology**

   - Some phones still store **SMS drafts** that can be sent when a signal returns; keep a short “I’m looking for my parents” ready to go.  
4. **Prepare for the Possibility of Bad News** – It’s painful, but having a **plan for emotional support** (counselor, trusted friend, religious leader) can help you process whatever you learn.

Affected Groups: People in Yemen, Somalia, South Sudan, Venezuela, and other conflict or crisis-affected countries where the formal banking and financial transfer system has partially or completely collapsed, and who are trying to receive remittances from family abroad that are essential for survival and medicine access, through informal channels whose reliability and security are variable and whose failure costs are borne entirely by the recipient. Disproportionately affecting people whose medical needs require specific purchases that cannot be made without money, people in areas where the collapse of formal banking has been most complete, people who have already experienced losses through informal transfer channels and who are therefore making a risk-calibrated decision among bad options, and people for whom the transfer is not discretionary income but the mechanism through which essential goods are reached.
Legal, Bureaucratic, or Physical Mechanisms: Yemen's formal banking system has been severely disrupted by the conflict since 2015, with the Central Bank of Yemen splitting into two institutions controlled by different parties, multiple commercial banks suspending operations or restricting transfers, and international money transfer operators withdrawing from the market due to compliance concerns, sanctions risk, and physical infrastructure destruction. The transfer service they used stopped six months ago. The one before stopped the year before. The man in the market who knows someone in Aden is one of the informal hawala-adjacent networks that has partially filled the gap, but with variable reliability and explicit loss risk. Her daughter needs the medicine that is not available here, which is the sentence that establishes the stakes: the transfer is not convenience but necessity.
Devastating Real-World Consequence: Her brother in Cairo has the money ready. Every formal channel is closed. The informal channel costs half of it. The water trucks still find a way through but medicine is different, which is the observation that supply chains respond to commercial incentives and water has been more commercially viable to move than medicine in this context. The question of what is the safest way to move money when every official way has stopped working is a genuine and urgent practical question that has partial answers through organizations including the International Rescue Committee, UNHCR's cash assistance programs, and in some contexts mobile money transfer services that have maintained operations where formal banking has not.

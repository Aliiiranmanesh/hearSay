Here are some strong candidates, with the “one person” constraint firmly in mind:

### 1. Hardneck Garlic (and shallots)
**Why it fits:** Plant in fall, mulch, minimal weed pressure if mulched well, harvest in summer, cure, and sell over many months. The labor is in bursts, not daily grind. One person can manage ¼ to ½ acre, especially with a tractor for initial tillage and a simple garlic planter or even hand-planting on a small scale.  
**Economics:** Direct-to-consumer, garlic can fetch $15–$20 per pound for premium varieties. Even at $10/lb, a well-managed ¼ acre can gross $20,000–$40,000. Inputs are low after seed stock is established. It stores beautifully, so you’re not racing a clock.  
**Meaning:** It’s a crop of patience and trust—plant in the dark of the year, harvest in the long light. There’s a quiet ritual to it that fits a reflective stage of life.

---

### 2. Cut Flowers (especially perennials and easy annuals)
**Why it fits:** High value per square foot, and a small plot (⅛–¼ acre) can supply a weekly farmers’ market or a small bouquet subscription. Focus on low-maintenance, high-impact blooms: peonies, dahlias (if you can lift/store tubers or treat as annuals), sunflowers, zinnias, lisianthus, snapdragons. Perennials like peonies and lilacs ask for almost nothing once established.  
**Labor:** Harvesting and arranging takes time, but it’s gentle work—no heavy lifting, can be done at your own pace in the cool of the morning. You can scale down to a “flower stand” at the end of the driveway on the honor system.  
**Meaning:** You’re growing beauty, not just calories. That can be unexpectedly healing, and it connects you to weddings, birthdays, kitchen tables—people’s joy.

---

### 3. Microgreens or Indoor Salad Greens (for off-season income)
**Why it fits:** Done in a small heated space (basement, shed, high tunnel), this is physically light work. No weeding, no weather risk. One person can manage a few dozen trays and sell to restaurants, at a winter market, or through a simple CSA.  
**Economics:** Extremely high value per square foot. A 10x20’ space can generate a meaningful side income. It uses your farming mindset but protects your body.  
**Caveat:** It requires consistent harvesting and delivery, but it gives purpose through the dark months when the fields are asleep.

---

### 4. Heritage or Specialty Grains (if you have the equipment)
If your heart is still in grain farming and you have the combine/dryer/bin setup, consider scaling *way* down and growing a premium grain for local artisan bakers, distillers, or home millers. Think hard red wheat, rye, or heirloom corn for grits and polenta. You sell identity-preserved, stone-ground, or direct to a baker who values the story. You’d need only a few acres to supply a dedicated market, and you can do all the fieldwork yourself with your existing machinery, just less of it. The economics shift from commodity price per bushel to a relationship price that reflects flavor and traceability.

---

### 5. Honey (and pollination services)
If you’re willing to learn beekeeping, a few dozen hives can be managed solo. The land benefits from the bees, and you can sell honey, beeswax candles, and nucs (starter colonies) to other beekeepers. The physical demands are moderate, and the work is seasonal. It pairs beautifully with a pollinator-friendly planting of flowers or buckwheat, which also builds soil.

---

### 6. U-Pick or Small Orchard (berries, tree fruit)
A low-spray, high-flavor planting of raspberries, blueberries, or a small apple orchard with cider varieties can become a destination. If you enjoy people, a few weekends of u-pick can bring in significant income with you mostly supervising and weighing. The offseason pruning and care are meditative. You can start small and expand slowly.
